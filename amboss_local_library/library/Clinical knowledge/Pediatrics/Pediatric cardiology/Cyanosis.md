## Summary

Cyanosis is a discoloration of the <u>skin</u> and <u>mucous membranes</u> that ranges from pale gray to blue and is caused by elevated concentrations of desaturated <u>hemoglobin</u> (typically > 3–5 g/dL). Cyanosis is not a sensitive indicator for <u>hypoxia</u> and/or <u>hypoxemia</u> because it is affected by <u>skin</u> pigmentation, venous congestion, and lighting. <u>Central cyanosis</u> results from reduced <u>arterial oxygen content</u> and is most evident in the oral <u>mucosa</u>, <u>tongue</u>, and <u>conjunctivae</u>. Common causes include pulmonary disease, intracardiac or intrapulmonary shunting, and abnormal <u>hemoglobin</u>. <u>Peripheral cyanosis</u> results from reduced peripheral blood flow with normal arterial oxygenation, leading to increased <u>deoxygenated hemoglobin</u>, and it is typically seen in the extremities, <u>nail</u> beds, and circumoral <u>skin</u>. Causes include low <u>cardiac output</u>, arterial obstruction, and peripheral <u>vasoconstriction</u>. The response of cyanosis and SpO₂ to <u>supplemental oxygen</u> helps guide diagnosis and distinguish between central and peripheral causes. Management is directed at the underlying cause. In <u>newborns</u>, <u>peripheral cyanosis</u> may be normal, but <u>central cyanosis</u> that persists despite <u>supplemental oxygen</u> requires urgent evaluation to exclude <u>congenital heart disease</u>.
