# Epididymitis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Pediatrics > Pediatric urology > Epididymitis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Fi0gtf)

---

## Summary

Epididymitis is the inflammation of the <u>epididymis</u> and is commonly associated with genitourinary tract infections. Epididymitis is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>, with patients typically presenting with a gradual onset of <u>pain</u> and swelling of the affected <u>scrotum</u> and a positive <u>Prehn sign</u>. <u>Urinalysis</u> and testing for <u>sexually transmitted infections</u> (<u>STIs</u>) can help confirm the diagnosis. <u>Testicular torsion</u> is the most important differential diagnosis of epididymitis; if it cannot be ruled out clinically, an urgent <u>ultrasound</u> must be performed. The <u>treatment for epididymitis</u> includes <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> based on the most likely source of infection and symptomatic management, e.g., scrotal elevation and <u>NSAIDs</u>.

---

## Etiology

* Acute epididymitis: an inflammation of the <u>epididymis</u>, usually as a result of an infection 

* <u>Urinary tract infections</u>

* Most common cause among older men and children

* Pathogens: <u>E. coli</u> (most common), <u>Pseudomonas</u>  (common), <u>Proteus mirabilis</u>, <u>Klebsiella pneumoniae</u>

* <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u>

* Most common cause among young males (usually < 35 years of age)

* Pathogens: <u>Chlamydia trachomatis</u>;  (common), <u>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</u> (common), <u>Treponema pallidum</u>, <u>Trichomonas vaginalis</u>, <u>Gardnerella vaginalis</u>

* Chronic epididymitis (> 6-week course of the disease)

* Recurrent and/or untreated acute epididymitis

* <u>Tuberculosis</u> (usually associated with renal <u>tuberculosis</u>)

* Rare, noninfectious causes

* <u>Amiodarone</u>-induced epididymitis

* Autoimmune diseases (e.g., <u>Behcet disease</u>): associated with <u>granuloma</u> formation in <u>seminiferous tubules</u>

---

## Clinical features

* Acute epididymitis

* Unilateral scrotal <u>pain</u> and swelling;  , which develops over several days and radiates to the <u>ipsilateral</u> flank [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/69ajo5)

* Tenderness along the <u>posterior</u> <u>testis</u>

* Positive Prehn sign: reduced <u>pain</u> when the affected hemiscrotum is elevated

* Scrotal <u>skin</u> overlying the <u>epididymis</u> may appear red, shiny, and <u>edematous</u>

* Low-grade <u>fever</u> (especially among children)

* <u>Symptoms of lower urinary tract infection</u> (e.g., <u>dysuria</u>, frequency, urgency), including <u>urethritis</u> (urethral discharge)

* <u>Chronic epididymitis</u>

* Recurrent bouts of scrotal <u>pain</u>

* Swelling is minimal when compared to acute epididymitis

* Thickened <u>epididymis</u>

## Diagnosis

The following recommendations are consistent with the 2021 <u>CDC</u> <u>STI</u> guidelines. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




### General principles




* Epididymitis is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>.

* Urine and urethral studies help tailor <u>antibiotic therapy</u> and identify the causative organism.

* Imaging is primarily used to rule out <u>testicular torsion</u> but can confirm epididymitis if the diagnosis is uncertain.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Testicular torsion</u> is the most important differential diagnosis of epididymitis and must be ruled out first! Consult urology if the diagnosis remains unclear.




### Routine <u>laboratory studies</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LLcwy10)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aPbQWF)




* <u>Urinalysis</u>: to identify <u>pyuria</u> and/or <u>bacteriuria</u>

* <u>Urine culture</u>: to identify the causative organism and assess for <u>antibiotic</u> resistance  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aPbQWF)

* <u>Nucleic acid amplification testing</u> (<u>NAAT</u>) for <u>Chlamydia trachomatis</u> and <u>Neisseria gonorrhea</u>;  (urine sample or urethral swab)  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aPbQWF)

* <u>Gram stain</u> of urethral secretions: for <u>diagnosis of urethritis</u>





> [!TIP]
> Testing for <u>HIV</u> and <u>syphilis</u> is recommended for all patients with acute epididymitis. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




### Additional <u>laboratory studies</u>




Not usually indicated




* Nonspecific elevated <u>acute phase reactants</u>, e.g., <u>leukocytosis</u>, ↑ <u>ESR</u>, ↑ <u>CRP</u> may be present.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aPbQWF)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JLcsA10)

* In patients with persistent clinical features despite <u>antibiotic</u> treatment, consider testing for alternate diagnoses, e.g., <u>neoplasms</u>, <u>abscess</u>, <u>infarction</u>, fungal organisms. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

* Consider <u>TB workup</u> in patients with <u>chronic epididymitis</u> and recent <u>TB</u> exposure. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)





### <u>Duplex ultrasound</u> of the <u>scrotum</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* May be used to rule out <u>testicular torsion</u> and <u>scrotal abscess</u>

* Findings: An enlarged, <u>hyperemic</u> <u>epididymis</u> with increased blood flow indicates epididymitis.

* Consult urology for further radiological evaluation in patients with <u>chronic epididymitis</u>.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HLcK_10)

## Differential diagnoses

* <u>Testicular torsion</u>

* <u>Hydrocele</u>

* <u>Varicocele</u>

* <u>Inguinal hernia</u>

* See “<u>Overview of scrotal abnormalities</u>.”




The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Treatment

The following recommendations apply to acute epididymitis and are consistent with the 2021 <u>CDC</u> <u>STI</u> guidelines. Consult urology regarding the management of <u>chronic epididymitis</u>.  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HLcK_10)




### Empiric antibiotics for epididymitis




* Indicated for all patients with acute epididymitis

* Start <u>empiric antibiotic treatment</u> based on the most likely causative organism. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

* Patients with a suspected <u>UTI</u> source;   (e.g., <u>enteric</u> organisms): <u>fluoroquinolone</u>, e.g., <u>levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aPbQWF)

* Patients with a suspected <u>STI</u> source (e.g., <u>chlamydia</u> or <u>gonorrhea</u>): <u>ceftriaxone</u>  DOSAGE PLUS <u>doxycycline</u> DOSAGE

* Patients who practice insertive anal sex: Cover both <u>STIs</u> and <u>enteric</u> organisms, e.g., <u>ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE PLUS <u>levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE.

* Adjust <u>antibiotic therapy</u> once the causative organism is identified.

* If symptoms do not improve within 72 hours after starting treatment, reconsider other diagnoses.





> [!TIP]
> If an <u>STI</u> is suspected, patients should abstain from sex until they and any partners have been successfully treated. Refer all sexual partners from the previous 60 days before symptom onset for evaluation, testing, and presumptive treatment. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




### Symptomatic management




Indicated for all patients as needed




* Scrotal elevation

* <u>NSAIDs</u>

* Avoidance of aggravating activities

* Application of a cold pack





### Surgical intervention




* Not generally indicated

* Exceptions include intrascrotal <u>abscesses</u> and some cases of <u>chronic epididymitis</u>





### Disposition [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* Outpatient management is usually sufficient for patients with acute epididymitis.

* Consider inpatient treatment and/or consult urology:

* For acutely ill patients (<u>fever</u>, high <u>CRP</u>, severe <u>pain</u>)

* For older patients and patients with <u>diabetes</u>

* If complications are suspected, e.g., intrascrotal <u>abscess</u> or <u>necrotizing fasciitis</u>

## Complications

* Epididymal <u>abscess</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J9aso5)

* Epididymo-orchitis: the spread of infection from the <u>epididymis</u> to the <u>testicle</u>

* <u>Testicular infarction</u>

* <u>Infertility</u>




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
