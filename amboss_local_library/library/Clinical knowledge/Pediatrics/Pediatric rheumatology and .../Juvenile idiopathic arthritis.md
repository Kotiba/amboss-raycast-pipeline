# Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w40hlT)

## Summary

Juvenile <u>idiopathic</u> <u>arthritis</u> (JIA; formerly called juvenile <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>) is a broad term for childhood rheumatic diseases that begin before the age of 16 and are characterized by <u>joint</u> inflammation that lasts more than 6 weeks. It is classified into various types based on the pattern of <u>joint</u> involvement, the presence of extra-articular manifestations (e.g., <u>uveitis</u>, <u>rash</u>, <u>nail</u> changes, <u>lymphadenopathy</u>, <u>hepatosplenomegaly</u>), laboratory findings, and disease prognosis. <u>Oligoarticular JIA</u>, which is the most common type, presents with asymmetric involvement of up to four <u>joints</u> (with <u>the knee joint</u> most often affected). Nearly half of all cases of <u>oligoarticular JIA</u> are associated with <u>anterior uveitis</u>, which may be diagnosed by <u>slit-lamp examination</u>. Laboratory tests such as <u>ESR</u>, <u>rheumatoid factor</u> (RF), <u>antinuclear antibodies</u> (<u>ANA</u>), and the <u>HLA-B27</u> <u>antigen</u> test are used to classify and determine the prognosis of JIA. <u>Treatment of JIA</u> is similar to that of adult <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u> and involves the use of <u>NSAIDs</u>, intra-articular <u>steroid</u> injections, and <u>disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs</u> (<u>DMARDs</u>) such as <u>methotrexate</u>. <u>Systemic glucocorticoid</u> therapy should be avoided because of the risk of growth impairment.

## Etiology

* <u>Idiopathic</u>

* Immunological predisposition: different <u>HLA</u> associations [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SdYy6L)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FE0gx3)

* <u>Oligoarticular JIA</u>: <u>HLA</u>-DR8, <u>HLA</u>-DR5

* Polyarticular JIA: <u>HLA-DR4</u>

* <u>Enthesis</u> associated and <u>psoriatic JIA</u>: <u>HLA-B27</u>

* Possibly triggered by a viral or bacterial infection

* Exposure to <u>antibiotics</u> during childhood may increase the risk of JIA.

---

## Pathophysiology

Autoimmune and/or autoinflammatory disease → chronic synovial inflammation with infiltration of <u>plasma cells</u>, <u>B lymphocytes</u>, and <u>T lymphocytes</u> → <u>joint capsule</u> <u>hyperplasia</u> → growth of fibrovascular <u>connective tissue</u> (<u>pannus</u>) → invasion of the articular surface → loss of <u>joint</u> function [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uE0px3)

---

## Clinical features

For the exact pattern of <u>joint</u> involvement and specific extra-articular symptoms, see “Subtypes and variants” below.




* <u>Arthritic</u> symptoms

* Swollen <u>joints</u>, rarely <u>erythema</u>

* Early morning stiffness

* Limited or painful <u>joint</u> movement

* Extra-articular manifestations [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8ybOSw)

* <u>Fever</u> (for ≥ 2 weeks)

* <u>Uveitis</u>

* <u>Rash</u>

* <u>Nail</u> changes

* <u>Lymphadenopathy</u>

* <u>Hepatosplenomegaly</u>

* Nonspecific features 

* Excessive crying

* <u>Lethargy</u>

* Decreased scholastic performance





> [!TIP]
> The affected <u>joints</u> are often stiff in the morning or after longer periods of inactivity (e.g., sitting). <u>Joint</u> stiffness improves with movement and decreases later in the day.

## Diagnosis

The clinical <u>diagnosis of JIA</u> can be supported by a number of diagnostic tests.




### Laboratory tests




Blood tests are used to classify JIA, assess the prognosis, and rule out other similar conditions (see “Subtypes and variants” above).




* <u>Autoantibodies</u> levels [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dyb1hw)

* <u>Rheumatoid factor</u> (RF)

* Absent in most cases of JIA (except <u>seropositive polyarticular JIA</u>)

* Associated with poor prognosis

* ↑ <u>ANA</u>

* Most commonly associated with <u>oligoarticular JIA</u> (∼ 70%)

* <u>Incidence</u> of <u>anterior uveitis</u> increases when <u>antinuclear antibodies</u> (<u>ANA</u>) are present.

* <u>Anti-CCP</u> <u>antibodies</u>: indicate a poor prognosis [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9ybNhw)

* <u>Acute phase reactants</u>

* ↑ <u>ESR</u>: usually seen with all forms of JIA

* ↑ <u>CRP</u>, <u>ferritin</u>: usually associated with <u>systemic JIA</u>

* <u>CBC</u>

* <u>Anemia</u>

* <u>Leukocytosis</u>

* <u>Thrombocytosis</u>: denotes a more severe inflammatory response in polyarticular and <u>systemic JIA</u>





### Imaging tests




* <u>Ultrasound</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DE01B3)

* Used to detect synovial <u>hypertrophy</u> and intraarticular fluid collections

* Best imagistic tool for the detection of early bone <u>erosions</u>

* <u>X-ray</u> [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xybEhw)

* May be used for the identification of JIA complications

* Should not be performed routinely





### Other diagnostic tests




* Synovial <u>biopsy</u>

* Can provide a definitive <u>diagnosis of JIA</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Bybzhw)

* Shows infiltration of <u>plasma cells</u>, <u>B lymphocytes</u>, and <u>T lymphocytes</u>

* <u>Slit lamp examination</u>: : should be performed for regular ophthalmological screening in patients with <u>anterior uveitis</u>  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dyb1hw)

* <u>Slit lamp examination</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dyb1hw)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UlXbwy)

* Patients with a high risk of developing <u>anterior uveitis</u> (<u>ANA</u> positive; , age of onset ≤ 7 years, and disease duration ≤ 4 years) should undergo ophthalmic screening every 3 months.

* All other patients should undergo ophthalmic screening every 12 months.

* Patients with <u>anterior uveitis</u> should be monitored every 1–3 months, depending on the choice of therapy and response to treatment.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Anterior uveitis</u> that occurs with JIA may be asymptomatic (especially in the case of chronic <u>anterior uveitis</u>). However, untreated <u>anterior uveitis</u> is associated with a high risk of developing <u>glaucoma</u>, <u>cataracts</u>, and <u>optic nerve</u> damage. Therefore, early detection via <u>slit lamp examination</u> and swift initiation of treatment are of paramount importance.

## Differential diagnoses

The differential <u>diagnosis of JIA</u> includes other causes of nonsuppurative <u>arthritis</u> in children: [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vybASw)




* <u>Acute lymphocytic leukemia</u>

* <u>Reactive arthritis</u>

* <u>Acute rheumatic fever</u>

* Juvenile <u>ankylosing spondylitis</u>

* <u>Connective tissue disease</u> (e.g., <u>SLE</u>, Sjögren syndrome)

* Trauma

* <u>Hemophilia</u>

* <u>Growing pains</u>





The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Treatment

### Definitive therapy




#### First-line therapy [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wE0hB3)




* <u>NSAIDs</u>

* <u>Indomethacin</u>

* Commonly used; highly effective in the <u>treatment of JIA</u>

* Has severe side effects: <u>headaches</u>, <u>renal failure</u>, and <u>bone marrow</u> <u>depression</u>

* Other commonly used <u>NSAIDs</u>: <u>naproxen</u>, <u>ibuprofen</u>, <u>meloxicam</u>, <u>celecoxib</u>, and tolmetin

* Local intra-articular <u>steroids</u> (e.g., <u>triamcinolone</u>): indicated in the case of active <u>arthritis</u>





#### Second-line therapy




* <u>Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs</u> (<u>DMARDs</u>) [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kdYmJL)

* Therapy with <u>DMARDs</u> should be started as early as possible in the case of high disease activity.

* The <u>DMARD</u> of choice is determined on an individual basis with <u>methotrexate</u> being used in most cases.

* Biologic agents

* Indicated if the response to <u>DMARDs</u> is poor.

* Examples: <u>etanercept</u>, <u>adalimumab</u>, <u>tocilizumab</u>, <u>anakinra</u>

* <u>Systemic glucocorticoid</u> therapy (oral, IV) [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Eyb8Sw)

* Rarely used in children because of the risk of <u>catabolic</u> side effects (e.g., <u>osteoporosis</u>, growth impairment)

* A short course of <u>systemic glucocorticoid</u> therapy may be prescribed in the following situations:

* Severe systemic involvement (e.g., severe <u>serositis</u>; see “<u>Still disease</u>”)

* As <u>bridge therapy</u> when <u>DMARDs</u> are initiated

* Acute <u>anterior uveitis</u>

* <u>Macrophage activation syndrome</u>





### Supportive therapy




* <u>Physiotherapy</u>: to prevent <u>joint</u> deformities

* <u>Surgery</u>, <u>splints</u>, and/or <u>orthotics</u>: to correct <u>limb length discrepancy</u> and/or <u>joint</u> deformities





> [!TIP]
> Most drugs that are used to treat adult <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u> may be used to treat JIA as well (see “Therapy” in “<u>Rheumatoid arthritis</u>”). However, certain forms of therapy (e.g., <u>systemic glucocorticoid</u> therapy) should, as a rule, be avoided in children.

## Complications

* Articular complications

* <u>Joint</u> destruction → <u>joint</u> subluxation and/or deformities (e.g., <u>swan-neck deformity</u>, <u>boutonniere deformity</u>)

* <u>Limb length discrepancy</u>

* Growth retardation

* Extra-articular complications

* Chronic <u>anterior</u> <u>uveitis</u> → blindness

* <u>Pericarditis</u>, <u>pleuritis</u>

* Macrophage activation syndrome (<u>MAS</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uE0px3)

* A rare complication of JIA (most commonly <u>systemic JIA</u>) characterized by <u>thrombocytopenia</u>, <u>elevated transaminases</u>, low <u>fibrinogen</u>, and markedly increased <u>ferritin</u> levels

* In a patient with <u>systemic JIA</u>, the presence of a normal or decreased <u>WBC count</u>, falling <u>ESR</u>, and/or increased <u>triglyceride</u> levels should raise the suspicion of <u>MAS</u>.




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.

## Prognosis

The clinical course and prognosis are highly variable (see “Subtypes and variants” above) . Most cases (∼ 95%) resolve by <u>puberty</u>.



* Factors associated with a poor prognosis [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wE0hB3)

* Early onset

* Prolonged active systemic disease

* Hip and/or wrist involvement

* Polyarticular involvement

* Symmetrical disease

* Presence of RF

* Presence of <u>anti-CCP antibodies</u>




> [!TIP]
> Early disease onset is associated with a greater degree of growth impairment and deformity.
