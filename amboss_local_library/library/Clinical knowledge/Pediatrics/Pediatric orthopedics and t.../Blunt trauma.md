# Blunt trauma

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Pediatrics > Pediatric orthopedics and trauma > Blunt trauma*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GL0B_g)

---

## Summary

Blunt trauma is any nonpenetrating injury caused by the impact of a blunt object against the body, resulting in damage to underlying structures. Common causes include <u>motor vehicle collisions</u> and falls from height. Clinical features and diagnostic studies vary based on the type and location of blunt trauma. Assessment of patients with blunt trauma follows the systematic evaluation outlined in the <u>advanced trauma life support</u> (<u>ATLS</u>) algorithm: <u>primary survey</u>, transfer to a <u>trauma center</u> (if indicated), <u>secondary survey</u>, and <u>tertiary survey</u>. Management involves treating immediately life-threatening injuries and surgical repair of traumatic injuries.




See also “<u>Management of trauma patients</u>” and “<u>Penetrating trauma</u>.”

---

## Overview

Management of blunt trauma is based on the type and location of the injury. Recommendations in this article are consistent with the 2018 guidelines on <u>ATLS</u>. See also “<u>Trauma in pregnant individuals</u>,” “<u>Trauma in older adults</u>,” and “<u>Trauma in children</u>.” [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




### Types of blunt trauma [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Open wounds</u> (e.g., <u>abrasions</u>, <u>lacerations</u>)

* <u>Contusions</u>

* <u>Fractures</u>

* <u>Crush injury</u>

* <u>Acceleration-deceleration injury</u> (e.g., <u>coup-contrecoup injury</u>)

## Blunt chest trauma

### Approach to blunt chest trauma [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




Ensure the following components are included as part of the standard <u>management of trauma patients</u> using the <u>ATLS algorithm</u>:




* <u>Primary survey</u>

* Identify and treat <u>respiratory failure</u> and <u>shock</u>, <u>tension pneumothorax</u>, <u>massive hemothorax</u>, and <u>cardiac tamponade</u> (see “Management” for details).

* Perform <u>resuscitative thoracotomy</u> in pulseless patients with previously documented signs of life.

* If at a nontrauma center, initiate transfer to a <u>trauma center</u>; high-speed <u>MVCs</u>, ejection from vehicle, or <u>death</u> of a passenger are <u>indications for trauma team activation</u>.

* See “<u>Traumatic circulatory arrest</u>” for the management of <u>cardiac arrest</u> due to trauma.

* <u>Secondary survey</u>

* Examine the chest for <u>paradoxical chest movement</u> and <u>crepitus</u>.

* Utilize clinical decision tools while obtaining <u>urgent diagnostics for trauma patients</u> (see “Diagnostics”).

* Assess for indications for <u>urgent thoracotomy</u>.

* Begin <u>initial management of vertebral injuries</u>, if suspected.





### Clinical features [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




* <u>Hemorrhagic shock</u> and/or <u>obstructive shock</u> (e.g., <u>hypotension</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>)

* <u>Respiratory distress</u> (e.g., <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>hypoxia</u>)

* Absent or decreased breath sounds

* <u>Jugular venous distention</u>

* <u>Pain</u> (e.g., <u>chest pain</u>, <u>rib</u> <u>pain</u>, <u>pleuritic chest pain</u>)

* <u>Chest wall</u> deformity (e.g., <u>flail chest</u>)

* <u>Subcutaneous emphysema</u>

* <u>Tracheal deviation</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Esophageal injuries</u> may be missed initially; suspect <u>esophageal rupture</u> in a patient with left-sided <u>pneumothorax</u> without apparent <u>rib fracture</u>. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




### Diagnostics [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0hWecO0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zlWrzl0)




Clinical decision tools can help identify patients who do not require imaging. See “<u>Urgent diagnostics for trauma patients</u>” for a comprehensive approach to trauma imaging.




* Bedside assessment: <u>CXR</u>, <u>eFAST</u>, <u>ECG</u>

* <u>CT imaging in trauma</u>

* Chest CT with IV contrast

* <u>CTA</u> chest if vascular injury is suspected

* Additional testing: <u>bronchoscopy</u>, <u>esophagoscopy</u>, <u>echocardiography</u>

### Management [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




#### Bedside interventions




The following bedside interventions may be performed during the <u>primary survey</u>:




* Pulseless patient;  with previously documented signs of life  after blunt injury: <u>resuscitative thoracotomy</u>

* <u>Respiratory failure</u>: <u>rapid sequence intubation</u> and <u>mechanical ventilation</u>

* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u>: <u>emergency chest decompression</u>

* <u>Massive hemothorax</u>: <u>chest tube placement</u>

* <u>Cardiac tamponade</u>: <u>pericardiocentesis</u> followed by <u>pericardial window</u>; or <u>resuscitative thoracotomy</u>

* <u>Esophageal injury</u>: <u>chest tube placement</u>

#### Further management




Further management depends on the type and extent of associated injuries.




* Close monitoring of <u>vital signs</u>

* Definitive surgical management after stabilization, e.g., <u>urgent thoracotomy</u> for <u>open pneumothorax</u>, <u>definitive management of vertebral injuries</u>

* Nonsurgical management, e.g., <u>analgesia</u> for <u>rib fractures</u>

## Chest wall injury

### Rib fracture [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ahWQcO0)




#### Etiology




* <u>Blunt thoracic trauma</u> (e.g., <u>MVC</u>, fall from height)

* <u>Nonaccidental trauma</u>

* <u>Pathological fractures</u>





#### Clinical features




* <u>Pleuritic chest pain</u>

* <u>Respiratory distress</u> (e.g., <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>)

* <u>Chest wall</u> tenderness, <u>bruising</u>, and/or deformity

* <u>Crepitus</u>

* Flail chest  

* Caused by three or more adjacent <u>ribs</u> fractured in two or more places

* <u>Paradoxical chest movement</u>: The <u>floating rib</u> segment moves inward during inspiration and outward during expiration.

* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u> based on <u>physical examination</u> findings

* Minor trauma: <u>CXR</u> (AP and <u>lateral</u> view)   [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YkWnml0)

* Major trauma: chest CT with or without IV contrast

#### Treatment [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6rWjR50)




* Isolated <u>rib fractures</u>: typically managed nonsurgically

* Provide <u>pain management</u> (e.g., <u>acetaminophen</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>, <u>lidocaine</u> patches).

* Encourage deep breathing exercises (e.g., <u>incentive spirometry</u>).

* Multiple <u>rib fractures</u> and/or <u>flail chest</u>

* Intubate and begin <u>positive pressure ventilation</u> (PPV) in patients with <u>signs of acute respiratory distress</u> (see “<u>Airway management</u>”).  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/snWtuN0)

* Manage <u>pneumothorax</u> and/or <u>hemothorax</u> with <u>chest tube placement</u>.

* Provide <u>pain management</u> (e.g., <u>patient-controlled analgesia</u>, <u>intercostal nerve block</u>).

* Encourage deep breathing exercises (e.g., <u>incentive spirometry</u>).

* <u>Flail chest</u>: Consult thoracic <u>surgery</u> for surgical management.





> [!TIP]
> In patients with <u>flail chest</u> and <u>respiratory distress</u>, <u>positive pressure ventilation</u> improves <u>gas exchange</u> and may provide internal stabilization while patients await <u>surgery</u>.




#### Disposition [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6rWjR50)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/chWa1O0)




* Patients with ≤ 2 <u>rib</u> <u>fractures</u> with adequate <u>pain control</u>: may be discharged

* Patients with ≥ 3 <u>rib</u> <u>fractures</u> and/or poorly controlled <u>pain</u>: Admit for <u>pain management</u> and monitoring.

* Patients at risk for respiratory decompensation (e.g., aged > 65 years, respiratory comorbidities) with ≥ 3 <u>rib</u> <u>fractures</u>: Consider <u>ICU</u> admission.





#### Complications




* <u>Respiratory failure</u>

* <u>Pneumothorax</u>

* <u>Hemothorax</u>

* <u>Atelectasis</u>

* <u>Pneumonia</u>

* <u>Pulmonary contusion</u>

* Intraabdominal organ injury

* <u>Nonunion</u>

## Cardiovascular injury

### Blunt cardiac injury (<u>BCI</u>) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gnWFsN0)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hnWcGN0)




Cardiac injuries from blunt trauma most commonly occur after high-speed <u>MVC</u> and range from minor asymptomatic injury to <u>myocardial</u> rupture and <u>death</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




#### Possible injuries




* Cardiac contusion: a cardiac injury secondary to <u>blunt force thoracic trauma</u>, which causes <u>myocardial</u> dysfunction with a wide range of clinical effects, including <u>chest pain</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>, elevated <u>cardiac biomarkers</u>, and <u>cardiogenic shock</u>

* Atrial and ventricular wall injuries

* Septal and valvular injuries

* <u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>, <u>myocardial infarction</u>

* <u>Pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade</u>

* <u>Myocardial</u> rupture





#### Clinical features




* Asymptomatic

* Chest <u>ecchymosis</u>

* <u>Chest pain</u> or tenderness

* <u>Hypotension</u>

* <u>Tachypnea</u>, <u>shortness of breath</u>

* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>

* New cardiac <u>murmur</u>, muffled <u>heart sounds</u>

* <u>Jugular venous distention</u>

* <u>Dizziness</u>, <u>syncope</u>

* <u>Cardiogenic shock</u>

* Sudden <u>cardiac arrest</u>





> [!TIP]
> Consider <u>BCI</u> if <u>tachycardia</u> persists despite <u>fluid resuscitation</u> and hemorrhage has been ruled out.




#### Diagnostics [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gnWFsN0)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hnWcGN0)




See “<u>Approach to blunt chest trauma</u>” for initial measures.




* <u>ECG</u>

* Obtain in all patients with suspected <u>BCI</u>.

* Potential findings include:

* <u>Arrhythmia</u> (e.g., <u>sinus tachycardia</u>, <u>dysrhythmia</u>)

* <u>ST-segment abnormalities</u>

* <u>ECG changes in STEMI</u>

* <u>AV block</u>

* <u>Troponin</u>

* Obtain in all patients with suspected <u>BCI</u>.

* May be elevated in patients with coronary or <u>myocardial injury</u>

* <u>Echocardiography</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BV1z9f0)

* Obtain in patients with <u>hemodynamic instability</u>, abnormal <u>ECG</u>, or elevated <u>troponin</u>.

* <u>TEE</u> may be performed if <u>TTE</u> is inconclusive.

* Potential findings include:

* <u>Pericardial effusion</u>

* <u>Cardiac tamponade</u>

* <u>Myocardial</u> rupture

* Wall motion abnormality

* Valvular injuries

* Septal injuries

* Additional diagnostics [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BV1z9f0)

* General imaging in trauma: e.g., <u>eFAST</u>, <u>CXR</u>, chest CT with or without IV contrast

* <u>CT angiography</u>: for suspected vascular injury





#### Management




* Initiate <u>immediate hemodynamic support</u> and <u>continuous cardiac monitoring</u>.

* Start treatment of underlying pathology (e.g., <u>management of cardiac tamponade</u>, <u>management of cardiogenic shock</u>).

* Patients with abnormal <u>ECG</u> or <u>troponin</u> levels: Monitor for delayed cardiac injuries (e.g., <u>myocardial</u> rupture).

* Patients with normal <u>ECG</u> and <u>troponin</u> levels: Consider discharge if there are no other injuries.





### Commotio cordis [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qdWCI40)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97WNL50)




* Definition: <u>ventricular fibrillation</u> and <u>sudden cardiac death</u> caused by a relatively mild nonpenetrating blunt force trauma to the precordial area

* Etiology: blow to the precordial area at a perpendicular angle, most commonly by a hard, spherical object (e.g., baseball, golf ball), during a brief electrically vulnerable period of <u>T-wave</u> upstroke

* Pathophysiology: blunt trauma leads to <u>myocardial</u> stretch → activation of ion channels because of mechano-electric coupling → augmentation of <u>repolarization</u> and premature ventricular <u>depolarization</u> → <u>ventricular fibrillation</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IdWYr40)

* Diagnosis is clinical and based on the following criteria:

* Blunt trauma to the chest followed by collapse

* Absence of structural <u>myocardial injury</u> (on imaging studies and/or <u>autopsy</u>)

* <u>ECG</u> showing <u>ventricular fibrillation</u> (if obtained)

* Management

* Immediate: <u>cardiopulmonary resuscitation</u>

* Long-term: cardiac workup (<u>ECG</u>, <u>echocardiogram</u>, <u>cardiac MRI</u>, <u>stress</u> testing)

* Prevention

* Avoidance of sports that involve <u>chest wall</u> impact (e.g., baseball, hockey, football)

* Use of chest protectors and/or safety balls may reduce risk.

* Treat any underlying cardiac disease.

* No underlying cardiac disease: no <u>ICD</u> necessary





### Blunt thoracic aortic injury [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3nWSGN0)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WhWP1O0)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DE11Bi0)




* Definition: an injury of the <u>thoracic aorta</u> resulting from blunt trauma; most commonly occurs <u>distal</u> to the <u>left subclavian artery</u> in the <u>aortic isthmus</u>

* Etiology: rapid deceleration from blunt trauma (e.g., <u>MVC</u>, fall from height)

* Clinical features: Severity ranges from <u>intimal</u> lesions (e.g., <u>aortic pseudocoarctation</u>) to thoracic <u>aortic rupture</u>.

* <u>Chest pain</u>

* Upper <u>back pain</u>

* <u>Dyspnea</u>, <u>hoarseness</u> and/or <u>stridor</u>

* <u>Dysphagia</u>

* <u>Chest wall</u> instability and/or <u>ecchymoses</u>

* New interscapular <u>murmur</u>

* Thoracic <u>aortic rupture</u>: signs of <u>hemorrhagic shock</u> (e.g., <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>) and tearing <u>pain</u>

* Imaging [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)

* <u>CXR</u> (initial imaging); potential findings include:

* Left main <u>bronchus</u> <u>depression</u>

* <u>Tracheal deviation</u>

* <u>Apical pleural cap</u>

* Left <u>pleural effusion</u> (<u>hemothorax</u>)

* Obscuration of the <u>aortic knob</u>

* <u>Mediastinal widening</u>

* Definitive imaging: based on the patient's hemodynamic status and trauma <u>surgeon</u> preference [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)

* <u>CTA</u> chest in hemodynamically stable patients

* <u>Transesophageal echocardiography</u>

* Management [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DE11Bi0)

* Initiate blood pressure and <u>heart rate</u> control.

* Goal: <u>heart rate</u> ≤ 100 bpm and systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mm Hg

* Treatment: <u>beta blockers</u> (e.g., <u>esmolol</u>, <u>labetalol</u>)  and <u>vasodilator</u> therapy (e.g., <u>nitroprusside</u>, <u>nicardipine</u>) if needed

* Definitive management

* Endovascular repair (<u>TEVAR</u>)

* Open surgical repair

* Prognosis: very poor (∼ 80% of patients die before reaching the hospital) [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DE11Bi0)

## Blunt abdominopelvic trauma

### Approach to blunt abdominal trauma [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Ensure the following components are included as part of the standard <u>management of trauma patients</u> using the <u>ATLS algorithm</u>:




* All patients

* Assess for <u>indications for trauma team activation</u> (e.g., high-speed <u>MVCs</u>, ejection from vehicle, <u>death</u> of a passenger) and initiate transfer to a <u>trauma center</u>, if necessary.

* Examine abdomen, flank, back, and groin for <u>bruising</u> and <u>signs of peritonitis</u>.

* Consider portable <u>CXR</u> and <u>pelvic</u> <u>x-ray</u>.

* Stabilize <u>pelvic fractures</u> with a <u>pelvic binder</u>.

* Begin <u>initial management of vertebral injuries</u>, if suspected.

* <u>Hemodynamically unstable</u> patients

* Perform <u>FAST</u> to quickly identify intraabdominal bleeding.

* Treat <u>hemorrhagic shock</u> with <u>emergency transfusion</u> and <u>immediate hemodynamic support</u>.

* Identify obvious indications for <u>exploratory laparotomy</u>, e.g., <u>hypotension</u> with a positive <u>FAST</u>, <u>peritoneal signs</u>, subdiaphragmatic air on <u>CXR</u>.

* Associated <u>pelvic fracture</u>: Coordinate <u>interventions for pelvic hemorrhage control</u> (e.g., <u>angioembolization</u>) concurrently with <u>emergency laparotomy</u> (see “<u>Initial management of pelvic fractures</u>” for details).

* Hemodynamically stable patients

* Obtain <u>urgent diagnostics for trauma patients</u> as indicated, e.g., CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u> with contrast.

* Identify other radiological indications for <u>exploratory laparotomy</u> (e.g., significant <u>hemoperitoneum</u> or <u>gastrointestinal perforation</u>).

* If there are no indications for <u>surgery</u>, initiate nonsurgical management with serial examinations and close monitoring of <u>vital signs</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> If there are obvious indications for immediate <u>exploratory laparotomy</u> (e.g., <u>hypotension</u> and <u>peritoneal signs</u>), do not delay transfer to the OR for diagnostic studies. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

### Management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




#### Nonoperative management [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F7Wgn50)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k4Wmjl0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O4WIjl0)




Nonoperative management should take place in a center with facilities for urgent <u>laparotomy</u>.




* May be considered for:

* Hemodynamically stable patients with no <u>signs of peritonitis</u>

* Renal, splenic, <u>pancreatic</u>, and hepatic injuries

* Typically includes:

* Monitoring for changes in clinical status (e.g., <u>vital signs</u>, <u>pain</u> levels, <u>serial abdominal examination</u>)

* Serial <u>laboratory studies</u> (e.g., <u>hemoglobin</u>)

* Repeat imaging for injury progression

* <u>Supportive care</u> (e.g., <u>pain management</u>, <u>wound care</u>)





#### Operative and interventional management [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/87WOn50)




<u>Hemodynamically unstable</u> patients typically require surgical or interventional management, which may include:




* <u>Exploratory laparotomy</u>: e.g., for <u>hemodynamically unstable</u> patients with a positive <u>FAST</u> and/or <u>CT scan</u> or <u>signs of peritonitis</u>

* <u>Angiographic embolization</u>: e.g., for patients with <u>pelvic fracture</u>, <u>retroperitoneal hematoma</u>, or <u>liver</u> injury

* <u>External fixation</u> of <u>pelvic fractures</u>





#### Injury-specific management




Management depends on the type and extent of injuries and is determined in consultation with specialists (e.g., trauma <u>surgery</u>, interventional radiology).




* <u>Splenic injury</u> [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k4Wmjl0)

* Monitoring for <u>delayed splenic rupture</u>

* <u>Laparotomy</u> and repair or <u>splenectomy</u> in unstable patients

* <u>Liver</u> injury [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O4WIjl0)

* Nonoperative management with observation and <u>supportive care</u>

* OR <u>arteriography</u> and hepatic embolization

* <u>Laparotomy</u> and repair in unstable patients

* <u>Gastrointestinal perforation</u>: <u>laparotomy</u>; see “<u>Treatment of gastrointestinal perforation</u>.”

* <u>Duodenal hematoma</u>: bowel rest, <u>nasogastric decompression</u>, and <u>total parenteral nutrition</u> [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4BW3aL0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kBWmaL0)

* <u>Pancreatic</u> injury [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/t7WXn50)[[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s7WtM50)

* Nasogastric suction and <u>total parenteral nutrition</u>

* OR <u>surgical debridement</u>, repair, and percutaneous drainage

* <u>Pelvic fracture</u> [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H7WKM50)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/77W4M50)

* External or <u>internal fixation</u>

* <u>Hemostatic measures</u>: <u>angiographic embolization</u>, preperitoneal packing

* <u>Vertebral injuries</u>: See "<u>Definitive management of vertebral injuries</u>.”

## Blunt neck trauma

### Approach to blunt neck trauma [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




Ensure the following components are included as part of the standard <u>management of trauma patients</u> using the <u>ATLS algorithm</u>:




* <u>Primary survey</u>

* Prepare for <u>difficult airway management</u> and <u>secure the airway</u> early (see “Treatment”).

* Apply <u>C-spine immobilization</u> (see “<u>Initial management of C-spine injury</u>”).

* Consider <u>neurogenic shock</u> in hypotensive patients with no evidence of hemorrhagic or <u>obstructive shock</u>.

* If at a nontrauma center, initiate transfer to a <u>trauma center</u>; <u>airway obstruction</u> or <u>hypotension</u> are <u>indications for trauma team activation</u>.

* <u>Secondary survey</u>

* Perform <u>neurovascular examination</u> focusing on <u>clinical and diagnostic features of BCVI</u>.

* Obtain <u>urgent diagnostics for trauma patients</u> (see “Diagnostics”).

* Consult specialists based on injured anatomical structures (see “Surgical management”).





### Associated injuries [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Laryngotracheal <u>contusions</u> and <u>lacerations</u>

* Laryngeal fracture: a <u>fracture</u> of the <u>thyroid</u> <u>cartilage</u> and/or the cricoid <u>cartilage</u>

* <u>Fractures</u> and/<u>dislocations</u> of the <u>tracheal</u> cartilages

* Tracheal rupture: the partial or complete puncture or <u>laceration</u> of the <u>trachea</u> or the main <u>bronchi</u>

* Pharyngoesophageal injury

* <u>Blunt cerebrovascular injury</u>

* <u>Cervical spine injuries</u>, e.g., <u>hangman fracture</u>





### Clinical features [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




* <u>Airway</u> or esophageal injury

* <u>Signs of airway compromise</u> (e.g., <u>dyspnea</u>)

* <u>Hoarseness</u> and/or <u>stridor</u>

* <u>Subcutaneous emphysema</u> (e.g., <u>crepitus</u>)

* <u>Mediastinal emphysema</u> (e.g., <u>Hamman sign</u>)

* <u>Hemoptysis</u>

* <u>Dysphagia</u>

* Vascular injury

* <u>Carotid bruit</u>

* Expanding neck <u>hematoma</u>

* <u>Focal neurological deficit</u>

* Clinical features of strangulation [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zqcrad0)

* <u>Asphyxiation</u>, <u>loss of consciousness</u>

* <u>Dysphagia</u>, voice changes

* <u>Subconjunctival hemorrhage</u>, facial <u>petechiae</u>

* <u>Bruising</u> around the neck, <u>strangulation marks</u>

### Treatment [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




#### <u>Airway management</u> [[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GB1BXj0)




* If there are <u>signs of airway compromise</u>, call for help immediately (e.g., emergent anesthesia or ENT consult).

* Perform <u>BMV</u> with caution.

* Prepare for <u>difficult airway management</u> (e.g., distorted anatomy, blood, and/or <u>edema</u>) by an experienced provider.

* <u>Intubation via direct laryngoscopy</u> or <u>video laryngoscopy</u> using a smaller-sized <u>ET tube</u> can be attempted initially.

* <u>Awake fiberoptic intubation</u> is preferred for distorted anatomy.

* Prepare for <u>emergency surgical airway</u> as a backup.

* Perform <u>manual in-line cervical stabilization</u> during <u>airway management</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Cricothyroidotomy</u> may be impossible if anatomical landmarks are invisible or deformed, e.g., due to a <u>laryngeal fracture</u>. Emergency <u>tracheostomy</u> may be required if <u>intubation</u> has failed. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




#### Surgical management




Consult surgical subspecialties based on clinical and diagnostic findings.




* Vascular injuries: vascular <u>surgery</u>

* Laryngotracheal injuries: otolaryngology

* <u>C-spine injuries</u>: <u>spine</u> <u>surgery</u> (See “<u>Definitive management of C-spine injuries</u>.”)

* <u>Esophageal injuries</u>: thoracic <u>surgery</u>
