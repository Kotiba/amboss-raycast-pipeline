# Vitamin K deficiency bleeding

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Pediatrics > Neonatology > Vitamin K deficiency bleeding*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Q40uQT)

---

## Summary

<u>Vitamin K deficiency</u> bleeding (VKDB) is a condition characterized by spontaneous bleeding in <u>infants</u> due to low levels of <u>vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors</u>. Because <u>vitamin K</u> has poor <u>placental</u> transfer, is present in low concentrations in <u>breast milk</u>, and the neonatal gut is initially sterile, all <u>newborns</u> have low <u>vitamin K</u> stores at <u>birth</u>. VKDB is rare in industrialized countries due to the standard prophylactic <u>vitamin K</u> injection given at <u>birth</u>. The condition is categorized as early-onset (within 24 hours after <u>birth</u>), classic (within 1 week), or late-onset (1 week to 6 months). Common bleeding sites include the <u>gastrointestinal tract</u>, umbilical stump, or nose, though life-threatening intracranial or subgaleal hemorrhages can occur. Treatment focuses on managing active bleeding with <u>vitamin K</u> administration and, in severe cases, <u>blood products</u> like four-factor <u>prothrombin complex concentrate</u> to restore <u>homeostasis</u>.

---

## Etiology

The underlying cause is always a deficiency of <u>vitamin K</u>, which can be due to various factors:




* <u>Vitamin K</u> deficiency in the mother

* Because of therapy with <u>anticonvulsants</u>, <u>antibiotics</u>, antituberculosis agents, or <u>warfarin</u>: most important in early-onset VKDB

* Maternal <u>malnutrition</u>

* Poor <u>placental</u> passage of <u>vitamin K</u>

* Underdeveloped intestinal flora (which produces <u>vitamin K</u>), e.g., due to <u>premature birth</u>

* <u>Exclusive breastfeeding</u>: low <u>vitamin K</u> levels in <u>breast milk</u> (most important in late-onset VKDB)

* <u>Chronic diarrhea</u> of the <u>newborn</u>

* <u>Cholestatic</u> diseases (e.g., <u>biliary atresia</u>)

* Long-term <u>antibiotic</u> treatment in <u>newborns</u>

## Classification

* Early onset: within 24 hours after <u>birth</u>

* Classic: : within 1 week after <u>birth</u>

* Late onset: between 1 week and 6 months after <u>birth</u>; highest risk of <u>intracranial bleeding</u>

---

## Clinical features

* Common bleeding sites

* Cutaneous: generalized <u>ecchymoses</u> and easy <u>bruising</u> (notably the face and neck)

* Gastrointestinal: <u>hematochezia</u>, <u>melena</u>, or <u>hematemesis</u>

* Umbilical: persistent oozing or frank bleeding from the <u>umbilical cord</u> stump

* <u>Mucosal</u>: <u>epistaxis</u> and bleeding from the <u>gums</u>

* Urogenital: <u>hematuria</u>

* Deep tissue: large intramuscular hemorrhages

* Life-threatening manifestations

* <u>Intracranial hemorrhage</u> (<u>ICH</u>): the most serious complication, often presenting with a bulging <u>fontanelle</u>, irritability, <u>seizures</u>, or altered consciousness

* <u>Signs of hypovolemia</u>: pale <u>skin</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, and poor <u>perfusion</u> due to acute blood loss

---

## Diagnosis

<u>Coagulation studies</u>:




* ↑ <u>Prothrombin</u> time (<u>PT</u>)

* Normal or ↑ activated <u>partial thromboplastin time</u> (<u>PTT</u>)

* Normal <u>bleeding time</u>

* ↓ Factors II, VII, IX, and X

---

## Treatment

* Nonmajor bleeding: PO or IM <u>vitamin K</u>

* <u>Major bleeding</u> (with <u>hemodynamic instability</u>)

* IV <u>vitamin K</u> (slow infusion)

* Four-factor <u>prothrombin complex concentrate</u> (4F <u>PCC</u>) for rapid <u>factor replacement</u>

* If 4F <u>PCC</u> is unavailable, <u>fresh frozen plasma</u> (<u>FFP</u>)

---

## Prevention

In the US, all <u>newborns</u> receive intramuscular <u>vitamin K</u> (0.5–1 mg) at <u>birth</u>.
