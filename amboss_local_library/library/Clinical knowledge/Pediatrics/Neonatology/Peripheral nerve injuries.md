# Peripheral nerve injuries

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Zi0ZJf)

## Summary

Peripheral nerve injuries result from systemic diseases (e.g., <u>diabetes</u>, autoimmune disease) or localized damage (e.g., trauma, compression, tumors) and manifest with neurological deficits <u>distal</u> to the level of the lesion. They occur as isolated neurological conditions or, more commonly, in association with <u>soft tissue</u>, vascular, and/or skeletal damage. Peripheral nerve injury can cause sensory deficits, loss of <u>motor function</u>, or a combination of both. Diagnosis is based on clinical evaluation, imaging techniques (<u>x-ray</u>, CT/<u>MRI</u>), and electrodiagnostic examination (e.g., <u>nerve conduction study</u>, <u>EMG</u>). Observation and <u>conservative treatment</u> (e.g., activity modification, splinting, electrical stimulation) are indicated in most closed injuries, which have a high rate of spontaneous recovery. Patients with open injuries or long disease courses may require surgical treatment. Recovery from peripheral nerve injury is often incomplete and patients may experience <u>chronic pain</u>.

## Types of nerve damage

Classification of peripheral nerve injury is useful for determining the prognosis and choosing a treatment strategy.




### Neurapraxia




* Compression injury causing temporary disruption of nerve conduction

* The whole nerve remains structurally intact.

* Good prognosis with complete recovery of nerve function





### Axonotmesis  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0RYelK)



* The <u>axon</u> is damaged but the <u>perineurium</u> and <u>epineurium</u> remain intact.




#### Leads to central chromatolysis




* Definition: the reaction of a neuronal cell body in response to an axonal injury

* Function: increase in <u>protein synthesis</u> to help restore the integrity of the damaged <u>axon</u>

* Characteristics

* Swelling of the neuronal body

* Dispersion of the <u>Nissl bodies</u>

* Displacement of the <u>nucleus</u> to the periphery





#### Results in Wallerian degeneration




* Definition: an active neuronal degeneration process in response to axonal injury

* Function

* To clear axonal debris and prevent scarring

* Facilitate targeted reinnervation and functional recovery of tissues previously innervated by that <u>axon</u> before injury

* Characteristics

* Initially retained electrical excitability of axonal membrane <u>distal</u> to the injury, lasting up to 36 hours

* Progressive degeneration of <u>distal</u> segment <u>cytoskeleton</u> with dissolution of axonal membrane

* Degradation of residual <u>myelin</u> sheath by <u>macrophages</u> and <u>Schwann cells</u>

* The <u>proximal</u> stump either stays in place or <u>retracts</u> slightly

* Ultimately, <u>the cell</u> body will sprout regenerative nerve fibers that, ideally, reinnervate the <u>distal</u> tissues.

* <u>Regeneration</u> is significantly more efficient in the <u>peripheral nervous system</u> than in the <u>central nervous system</u>.

* Good chance of at least partial recovery





### Neurotmesis [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0RYelK)




* Complete nerve transection

* Connective sheath damage

* The chances of recovery are very poor without surgical repair.





### Traumatic neuroma



* Benign, painful nodular thickening caused by nerve <u>regeneration</u> at the site of different forms of nerve injury

#### Erb palsy




* Injury to the upper trunk of the <u>brachial plexus</u> (C5–C6)

* Etiology
* Excessive <u>lateral</u> <u>flexion</u> of the neck  

* Trauma (e.g., falling on the head and shoulder in a motorcycle accident)

* <u>Birth injury</u>: excessive <u>lateral</u> <u>traction</u> on the neck during delivery and <u>shoulder dystocia</u>

* Clinical features

* <u>Weakness</u> of muscles in the C5 and C6 myotomes → flexed wrist with an extended forearm and <u>internally rotated</u> and <u>adducted</u> arm (waiter's tip posture) 

* Weak <u>biceps brachii</u>, <u>brachialis</u>, and <u>brachioradialis</u>

* Impaired <u>flexion</u> and <u>supination</u> of the forearm

* Absent <u>biceps reflex</u>

* Weak <u>infraspinatus</u> and <u>supraspinatus</u> → impaired <u>external rotation</u> of the arm

* Weak deltoid and <u>supraspinatus</u> → impaired arm <u>abduction</u>

* Weak wrist extensors → impaired wrist extension

* Asymmetric <u>Moro reflex</u> in <u>infants</u> (absent or impaired on the affected side)

* Sensory loss in the C5 and C6 <u>dermatomes</u> (thumb and <u>lateral</u> surface of the forearm and arm)

* Treatment

* <u>Immobilization</u> in <u>flexion</u> and <u>external rotation</u> with an <u>abduction</u> <u>brace</u>

* <u>Physiotherapy</u>

* <u>Surgery</u> for severe nerve damage or prolonged cases

#### Klumpke palsy




* Injury to the <u>lower trunk of the brachial plexus</u> (C8–T1)

* Etiology

* Hyperabduction of the arm

* Trauma (e.g., breaking a fall by grabbing a branch)

* <u>Birth injury</u>: excessive upward <u>traction</u> on the arm during delivery

* Compression of the lower trunk of <u>brachial plexus</u> (subacute to chronic onset)

* <u>Pancoast tumor</u>

* <u>Cervical rib</u>

* Clinical features

* <u>Weakness</u> of intrinsic hand muscles (thenar, <u>hypothenar</u>, <u>lumbricals</u>, <u>interossei</u>) → total claw hand (persistent <u>flexion</u> of the <u>interphalangeal joints</u> and extension of the <u>metacarpophalangeal joints</u> in the hand)

* <u>Preganglionic</u> <u>Horner syndrome</u> if injury occurs <u>proximal</u> to the <u>white ramus communicans</u>

* Decreased peripheral pulses if subclavian vessels are compressed by a <u>Pancoast tumor</u> or <u>cervical rib</u> (see “<u>Thoracic outlet syndrome</u>”)

* Absent <u>grasp reflex</u> in <u>infants</u>

* Sensory loss in the C8 and T1 <u>dermatomes</u> (little finger and <u>medial</u> surface of the forearm and arm)

* Treatment

* Splinting the hand to correct the <u>claw hand</u>

* <u>Physiotherapy</u>

* <u>Surgery</u> for severe nerve damage

### Peripheral nerve injuries in the upper extremity




* <u>Distal</u> nerve lesions are more likely to cause claw deformities (e.g., <u>ulnar claw</u> or <u>median claw</u>) because they result in a loss of <u>lumbrical</u> function with intact extrinsic flexors

* In <u>proximal</u> nerve lesions, hand distortions (e.g., <u>pope's blessing</u>) are only visible when the patient tries to flex the fingers or make a fist.

#### Phrenic nerve paralysis




* Anatomical course of the nerve

* Originates as a branch from the <u>cervical plexus</u> of C3–C5

* Passes ventrally on the <u>anterior scalene muscle</u> before descending into the <u>chest wall</u>

* Runs between <u>pleura</u> and <u>pericardium</u> accompanied by pericardiacophrenic <u>artery</u> and <u>vein</u>

* Supplies motor innervation of the <u>diaphragm</u> and sensory innervation of the <u>pericardium</u>, <u>parietal pleura</u> (<u>mediastinal</u> and diaphragmatic part), and <u>peritoneum</u>

* Etiology

* Unilateral

* Trauma

* <u>Iatrogenic</u> (e.g., cardiac <u>surgery</u>)

* Compression (e.g., <u>malignancy</u>)

* Bilateral

* Motor <u>neuron</u> diseases (e.g., <u>amyotrophic lateral sclerosis</u>)

* Neuropathies (e.g., <u>Guillain-Barré syndrome</u>, <u>post-polio syndrome</u>)

* <u>Cervical spine</u> <u>surgery</u>

* Trauma

* <u>Tumor</u>

* Clinical features

* Unilateral <u>paralysis</u>

* Often asymptomatic

* <u>Exertional dyspnea</u> possible

* Bilateral <u>paralysis</u>: severe <u>dyspnea</u>

* Diagnostics

* Unilateral <u>phrenic nerve paralysis</u>

* <u>Auscultation</u>

* Decreased respiratory movement

* Dull on percussion

* <u>CXR</u>

* <u>Ipsilateral</u> diaphragmatic elevation

* Possibly <u>mediastinal</u> shift

* <u>Compression atelectasis</u>

* <u>Fluoroscopy</u>: paradoxical elevation of the paralyzed hemidiaphragm on respiration or on asking the patient to sniff (sniff test)

* Bilateral <u>phrenic nerve paralysis</u>

* <u>Spirometry</u>: decreased <u>vital capacity</u>

* Diaphragmatic <u>electromyography</u>

* Treatment

* <u>Mechanical ventilation</u> may be required.

* Possible implantation of a diaphragmatic pacemaker

## Diagnosis

The diagnosis of peripheral nerve injuries is based on a thorough clinical history, <u>neurological examination</u>, and, in some cases, diagnostic tests (e.g., <u>x-ray</u> if <u>fracture</u> is suspected).




* Imaging

* Plain <u>x-ray</u>: detection of compression or transection due to <u>dislocated</u> bone or <u>fracture</u> segments

* CT/<u>MRI</u>: evaluation of causes like nerve tumors, avulsions, and focal <u>soft tissue</u> pathologies

* <u>Electrodiagnostic studies</u>: detect and grade nerve injury, nerve compression, and identify early stages of recovery

* <u>Electroneurography</u> (<u>nerve conduction study</u>): analysis of a muscle's electrical activity in response to stimulation of its supplying nerve

* Needle <u>electromyography</u> (<u>EMG</u>)

## Management

### <u>Conservative treatment</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eG0xyh)




* <u>Expectant management</u> (e.g., closed injuries of the nerve with a high rate of spontaneous recovery)

* Activity modification (e.g., avoid sports or activities that increase the likelihood of further nerve injury)

* Splinting [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fG0kAh)

* Prevents stiffness and <u>contractures</u> of <u>joints</u>

* Supports residual nerve functionality and reinnervation

* <u>Physiotherapy</u>

* Electrical stimulation: supports the <u>regeneration</u> of the <u>proximal</u> <u>axons</u> and reinnervation of the denervated muscles after surgical nerve repair [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gG0FAh)

* <u>Analgesia</u>

* <u>Nonopioid analgesics</u> (e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u>)

* Infiltration with <u>local anesthetics</u>

* Drug therapy

* Treatment of chronic <u>neuropathic pain</u> following peripheral nerve injury (e.g., <u>gabapentin</u>) [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hG0c_h)

* Used in combination with surgical treatment to enhance remyelination and motor <u>regeneration</u> (e.g., <u>lithium</u>) [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RG0l_h)





### Surgical repair




* Indications

* Open, non-contaminated, sharp injuries and concomitant vascular injuries → immediate surgical exploration and repair

* Open, contaminated injuries and postreduction palsy → early surgical exploration and repair (within 3 weeks)

* Patients without clinical or electromyographic signs of spontaneous recovery → delayed surgical exploration and repair (within 3 months)

* Procedures

* Nerve repair (neurorrhaphy): reconstruction of nerve continuity

* Nerve transfer: an intact healthy nerve is redirected towards a denervated nerve in order to restore the innervation of its target organ

* <u>Tendon</u> transfer: a <u>tendon</u> from a sufficiently powerful muscle is redirected towards another <u>tendon</u> in order to restore its motion and function
