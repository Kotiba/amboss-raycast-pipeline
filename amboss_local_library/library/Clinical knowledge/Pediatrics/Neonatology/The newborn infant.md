## Summary

<u>Infants</u> are usually <u>born at term</u> between 37 and 42 weeks of <u>gestation</u>. This time period can be further divided into <u>early term</u> (37 to 38 weeks), <u>full term</u> (39 to 40 weeks), and <u>late term</u> (41 weeks) deliveries. After 42 weeks of <u>gestation</u>, <u>birth</u> is considered <u>postterm</u>. Approximately 10% of births are preterm, occurring prior to 37 completed weeks of <u>pregnancy</u>. Most <u>infants</u> <u>born at term</u> require very little medical attention in order to successfully adapt to extrauterine life. Routine management of a newborn <u>infant</u> immediately after <u>birth</u> consists of removing <u>airway</u> secretions, drying the newborn, and providing him or her with warmth. <u>Health care providers</u> also clamp and cut the <u>umbilical cord</u>. The <u>Apgar score</u> is typically used to gauge the clinical status of newborn <u>infants</u> at one and five minutes after <u>birth</u> using the following parameters: <u>heart rate</u>, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability to tactile stimulation, and <u>skin</u> color. <u>Infants</u> who are <u>born at term</u> or late preterm and are breathing and moving satisfactorily should immediately be given to their mother for <u>skin</u>-to-<u>skin</u> contact and initiation of <u>breastfeeding</u>. <u>Infants</u> who are born prematurely, lack muscle tone, or are not breathing or crying may require <u>supplemental oxygen</u> or <u>neonatal resuscitation</u>.




Preventive measures in the delivery room include the administration of ophthalmic <u>antibiotics</u> and <u>vitamin K</u>. Within 24 hours of <u>birth</u>, a detailed <u>assessment of the newborn</u> should take place. This typically includes a history of the <u>pregnancy</u> and a <u>physical exam</u> from head to toe, as well as measurements of length and weight.




This article provides an overview of the initial post-delivery care of newborns. Newborn care after this time is covered in “<u>Well-child visits</u>.”




See also “<u>Overview of neonatal conditions</u>.”

## Newborn terminology

### General




* Infant: a child under 1 year of age [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZJXZs_)

* Newborn: a child under 28 days of age [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0JXes_)

* Perinatal period: the period from the 22<sup>nd</sup> week of <u>gestation</u> to the 7<sup>th</sup> day after <u>birth</u>  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gYYFon)

* <u>Postpartum period</u>: first 6–8 weeks after <u>birth</u>

* Live birth: postnatal presence of <u>vital signs</u> (e.g., respiration, <u>pulse</u>, <u>umbilical cord</u> <u>pulse</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JpXsq_)





### Timing of <u>birth</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TYY6on)




* Term birth: umbrella term for <u>live births</u> between 37 and 42 weeks of <u>gestation</u> 

* Early term infant: <u>live birth</u> between 37 0/7 weeks and 38 6/7 weeks of <u>gestation</u>

* Full term infant: <u>live birth</u> between 39 0/7 weeks and 40 6/7 <u>weeks of gestation</u>

* Late term infant: <u>live birth</u> between 41 0/7 weeks and 41 6/7 <u>weeks of gestation</u>

* <u>Preterm infant</u>: <u>live birth</u> between 20 0/7 weeks and 36 6/7 weeks of <u>gestation</u>  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IpXYI_)

* <u>Postterm infant</u>: <u>live birth</u> after the 42<sup>nd</sup> week of <u>gestation</u>





### Evaluation of <u>birth</u> weight




* Appropriate-for-gestational-age infant (<u>AGA</u>): <u>birth</u> weight 10<sup>th</sup>–90<sup>th</sup> <u>percentile</u> for <u>gestational age</u>

* Small-for-gestational-age infant (<u>SGA</u>): <u>birth</u> weight < 10<sup>th</sup> <u>percentile</u> for <u>gestational age</u>  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YJXns_)

* Large-for-gestational-age infant (<u>LGA</u>): <u>birth</u> weight > 90<sup>th</sup> <u>percentile</u> for <u>gestational age</u>

* Low birth weight [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aJXQs_)

* <u>Birth</u> weight < 2,500 g regardless of the <u>gestational age</u>

* Occurs in <u>early term infants</u> and <u>infants</u> with <u>intrauterine growth restriction</u>

* Associated with increased mortality, particularly due to <u>sudden infant death syndrome</u>

* Very low birth weight: birthweight between 1000 g–1499 g regardless of the <u>gestational age</u>

* Extremely low birth weight: birthweight < 1000 g regardless of the <u>gestational age</u>

## Postdelivery care and Apgar score

### Postdelivery care of the newborn [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ynXdDA)




* Perform <u>initial neonatal assessment</u>.

* Initiate <u>skin</u>-to-<u>skin</u> contact between newborn and birthing parent.

* Encourage early initiation of <u>breastfeeding</u>.

* Consider delaying <u>umbilical cord clamping</u> for ≥ 30 seconds. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XyW9dL0)

* Clamp and cut the <u>umbilical cord</u>.

* Provide additional interventions as needed, e.g.:

* Clear <u>airway</u> secretions by wiping the newborn's mouth and nose.

* Dry and stimulate the newborn.

* Provide warmth.

* Assess the <u>Apgar score</u> 1 and 5 minutes after <u>birth</u>.





### Apgar score [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4qa3Al)




* Used for standardized clinical assessment of newborns at 1 and 5 minutes after <u>birth</u> 

* Five components: <u>skin</u> color, <u>heart rate</u>, reflex irritability to tactile stimulation, muscle tone, respiratory effort

* Each component is given 0–2 points, depending on the status of the newborn.

* The total <u>Apgar score</u> is the sum of all five components.

* Assessing the need for and beginning <u>neonatal resuscitation</u> should be done independently of and before the <u>Apgar score</u> is determined.

* Assessment of the <u>Apgar score</u> at 5 minutes: <u>Infants</u> with scores < 7 may require further intervention.

* Reassuring: 7–10

* Moderately abnormal: 4–6

* Low: 0–3

* In <u>infants</u> with a score below 7, the <u>Apgar</u> assessment is performed at 5-minute intervals for an additional 20 minutes.

* Persistently low <u>Apgar scores</u> are associated with long-term neurologic sequelae.





| Calculation of the <u>Apgar score</u> |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
| Appearance (<u>skin</u> color) | Blue (<u>cyanotic</u>) or pale | Pink trunk, blue extremities (<u>acrocyanosis</u>) | Pink body and extremities |
| <u>Pulse</u> (<u>heart rate</u>) | None | < 100 beats/min | ≥ 100 beats/min |
| Grimace (reflex irritability upon tactile stimulation) | None | Grimace | Cry or active withdrawal |
| Activity (muscle tone, movement) | No movement, limp body | Some <u>flexion</u> | Active motion, <u>flexion</u> |
| Respirations | None | Weak cry, irregular/slow/weak breathing or <u>gasping</u> | Regular breathing, strong cry |





> [!NOTE]
> APGAR: Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, Respirations




> [!TIP]
> The <u>Apgar score</u> is useful for evaluating the status of a newborn <u>infant</u>, but it should not be used to draw conclusions about individual neonatal <u>morbidity</u> or mortality, and it should not be used as a long-term prognostic tool.

### Preventive measures directly after <u>birth</u>




* Ophthalmic <u>antibiotics</u>: to prevent <u>gonococcal conjunctivitis</u> (<u>erythromycin</u> ophthalmic ointment)

* <u>Vitamin K</u>: to prevent <u>vitamin K deficiency bleeding of the newborn</u> (<u>VKDB</u>)

## Assessment of the newborn

Measurement and a detailed <u>examination of the newborn</u> should take place within the first 24 hours of life.




* Measurements  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rJXfv_)

* Normal range (10<sup>th</sup> to 90<sup>th</sup><u>percentile</u> at 40 weeks <u>gestation</u>)

* Length: ∼ 50 cm (19–21 in)

* Weight: ∼ 7.5 lb (6 lb 6 oz to 8 lb 9 oz)

* Boys: 6 lb 6 oz to 8 lb 9 oz

* Girls: 6 lb 2 oz to 8 lb 6 oz

* Head circumference: ∼ 35 cm (13–14.6 in)

* <u>Vital signs</u> [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XJX9s_)

* <u>Respiratory rate</u>: 40–60 breaths per minute

* <u>Heart rate</u>: 120–160 beats per minute

* <u>Bilirubin</u>: see ”<u>Neonatal jaundice</u>”

* <u>pH</u>: ≥ 7.2 (slightly more acidic than adults) [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cJXaG_)

* Urine and <u>meconium</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1JX2G_)

* First passage of urine within 24 hours of <u>birth</u>

* First passage of meconium;  (a black-green, tarry substance that forms the newborn's feces) within 48 hours after <u>birth</u>

* Feeding: encourage and provide counseling regarding <u>breastfeeding</u>

* Losing weight after <u>birth</u> [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WJXPG_)

* Loss of up to 7% of <u>birth</u> weight in first five days of life is normal and no specific treatment is required.

* Newborns normally regain their <u>birth</u> weight by the time they are 10–14 days old.

* For causes of <u>small-for-gestational-age infants</u>, see “<u>Intrauterine growth restriction</u>.”

* Consequences of intrauterine <u>estrogen</u> exposure

* <u>Breast</u> bud development is normal in newborns, independent of sex.

* Newborn girls may have bloody mucoid <u>vaginal discharge</u>.





> [!TIP]
> The physiological <u>respiratory rate</u> and <u>heart rate</u> of newborns are substantially higher than in adults and older pediatric patients.




> [!TIP]
> Healthy newborns normally lose up to 7% of their original <u>birth</u> weight in the first 5 days of life. This weight is then gained back through drinking <u>breast milk</u> and/formula by age 10–14 days. No treatment is necessary.




### External signs of maturity




<u>Gestational age</u> is estimated at <u>birth</u> with the <u>Ballard score</u>, which combines neuromuscular assessment with the following physical signs of maturity:  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UcWbb40)




* <u>Skin</u> color and texture: rosy

* Body <u>hair</u>: <u>lanugo</u> may be present, thinning, or mostly absent

* Eyes: open

* <u>Ears</u>: well-formed <u>pinna</u> (auricular <u>cartilage</u>) that instantly recoils

* <u>Breast</u>: clearly discernible <u>areola</u>

* <u>Testicles</u>: descended

* Labia: <u>labia minora</u> covered by <u>labia majora</u>

* <u>Plantar</u> creases: cover the entire soles of the feet





### Examination for abnormalities




For an overview of potential abnormal examination findings, see “<u>Overview of neonatal conditions</u>.”




#### Face and head [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UcWbb40)




* Look for dysmorphic facial features.

* Check head shape (including <u>cranial sutures</u> and <u>fontanelles</u>).

* Perform an <u>eye examination</u> including the <u>Bruckner test</u> for <u>red reflex</u>. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qw1uiQ0)

* Visualize and palpate the oral <u>palate</u> for <u>cleft palate</u>.

* Check the <u>tongue</u> for <u>ankyloglossia</u>.

* Check for <u>ear</u> abnormalities; low-set <u>ears</u> are often associated with genetic disorders.

* Assess <u>hearing</u> function (see “<u>Newborn hearing screening</u>”).





> [!TIP]
> Intermittent <u>strabismus</u> is a normal finding in children < 4 months of age. [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5E1iwi0)




#### <u>Spine</u> and nervous system [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Assess the neck.

* Look for webbing, cysts, pits, masses, and <u>branchial clefts</u>.

* Ensure there is full <u>range of movement</u>.

* Examine the <u>spine</u>.

* Perform a full <u>neurological examination</u> including assessment of tone.





#### Thorax [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UcWbb40)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Palpate clavicles to exclude <u>neonatal clavicle fracture</u>.

* Look for skeletal deformities (e.g., <u>pectus excavatum</u>) and check for <u>neonatal gynecomastia</u>.

* Look for symmetric movement, and <u>auscultate</u> the chest to detect neonatal <u>pneumothorax</u>.

* Assess for congenital cardiac defects (see also “<u>Newborn screening</u>”).

* <u>Auscultate</u> for <u>heart sounds</u>.

* Perform <u>pulse oximetry</u> to exclude <u>cyanotic</u> cardiac defects.

* Palpate the brachial and femoral pulses to evaluate for <u>coarctation of the aorta</u>.  [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lw1vQQ0)





#### Abdomen [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Check that the abdomen is not distended or <u>scaphoid</u>.

* Palpate the abdomen for masses and organomegaly.

* <u>Auscultate</u> for <u>bowel sounds</u>.

* Check the <u>umbilical cord</u>.

* Ensure there are two <u>arteries</u> and one <u>vein</u>; a single <u>umbilical artery</u> can be associated with other congenital abnormalities.

* Look for signs of bleeding/infection.





#### <u>Genitourinary examination</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Assess for an <u>imperforate anus</u>.

* Check the inguinal region for <u>hernias</u>.

* In males <u>infants</u>: Rule out <u>cryptorchidism</u>, congenital <u>hydrocele</u>, and <u>hypospadias</u>.

* In female <u>infants</u>: Assess for <u>labial adhesions</u> and abnormalities of the <u>hymen</u>.





#### Extremities [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Check the fingers and toes for supernumerary, absent, or deformed digits.

* Assess the <u>range of motion</u> of all extremities.

* Perform an <u>infant hip examination</u> (e.g., with <u>Ortolani maneuver</u>) to rule out <u>developmental dysplasia of the hip</u>.

* Look for signs of neonatal <u>brachial plexus injury</u>.





#### <u>Skin</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ecWxY40)




* Look for signs of <u>neonatal jaundice</u>.

* Carefully inspect all of the <u>skin</u> for <u>neonatal skin lesions</u> (including birthmarks).

## Newborn screening

* Before leaving the hospital, newborns should be screened for serious and life-threatening conditions.

* The optimal time for screening is 24–48 hours after <u>birth</u>. [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gcWFb40)

* Screening usually involves assessment for:

* Critical congenital cardiac defects

* <u>Hyperbilirubinemia</u>

* Congenital <u>deafness</u>

* Various genetic disorders, e.g., endocrine, metabolic, and hematological disorders





> [!WARNING]
> Which genetic disorders to screen for varies according to national and state law; check local guidelines.

### Examples of commonly screened genetic conditions




* Endocrine conditions

* <u>Congenital hypothyroidism</u>

* <u>Congenital adrenal hyperplasia</u>

* Metabolic conditions

* <u>Disorders of fatty acid metabolism</u>

* Disorders of organic acid metabolism (e.g., <u>isovaleric acidemia</u>, <u>methylmalonic acidemia</u>, <u>propionic acidemia</u>)

* <u>Disorders of amino acid metabolism</u>;  (e.g., <u>phenylketonuria</u>, <u>homocystinuria</u>, <u>maple syrup urine disease</u>, <u>citrullinemia type I</u>)

* <u>Hemoglobinopathies</u> (e.g., <u>sickle cell disease</u>, <u>beta thalassemia</u>)

* Other conditions

* <u>Cystic fibrosis</u>

* Classical <u>galactosemia</u>

* <u>Severe combined immunodeficiencies</u> (<u>SCID</u>)

* <u>Glycogen storage disease type II</u> (<u>Pompe disease</u>)

* <u>Mucopolysaccharidosis type I</u>

* <u>X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy</u>

* <u>Spinal muscular atrophy</u>

* Biotinidase deficiency

* Etiology: metabolic disorder that leads to <u>biotin deficiency</u>

* Clinical features: dermatitis, <u>CNS</u> damage

* Treatment: <u>biotin</u> substitution
