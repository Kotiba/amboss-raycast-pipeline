# Sedative-hypnotic drugs

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Anesthesiology > Drugs > Sedative-hypnotic drugs*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CN0qWg)

---

## Summary

Sedative-hypnotics are a class of drugs that depress <u>CNS</u> function, inducing sedation, <u>sleep</u>, and unconsciousness with increasing dose. Agents in this drug class include <u>benzodiazepines</u>, <u>nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics</u> (<u>Z-drugs</u>), <u>barbiturates</u>, and <u>melatonin agonists</u>. Most sedative-hypnotic drugs affect <u>GABAergic</u> transmission, increasing the inhibition of neuronal excitability, except for <u>melatonin agonists</u>, which act on <u>hypothalamic</u> <u>melatonin</u> <u>receptors</u>. Sedative-hypnotic drugs are used as <u>anxiolytics</u>, sedatives, <u>muscle relaxants</u>, <u>anesthetics</u>, and <u>anticonvulsants</u>. Common side effects result from excessive <u>CNS depression</u> and include confusion, <u>somnolence</u>, and respiratory <u>depression</u>. Long-term use of sedative-hypnotics is associated with a risk of tolerance and withdrawal.




See also “<u>Sedative-hypnotic drug overdose</u>.”

## Benzodiazepines

### Agents [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xFXE4-)




* Short-acting:  (<u>half-life</u> 1–12 hours): midazolam, triazolam

* Intermediate-acting:  (<u>half-life</u> 12–40 hours): lorazepam; , temazepam, oxazepam, lormetazepam, alprazolam

* Long-acting:  (<u>half-life</u> > 40 hours): diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam, tetrazepam, flurazepam





### Mechanism of action [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* <u>Benzodiazepines</u> are indirect <u>GABA<sub>A</sub> receptor</u> <u>agonists</u>;  that bind to GABA-A receptors → ↑ affinity of <u>GABA</u> to bind to <u>GABA</u><sub>A</sub> <u>receptors</u> → ↑ <u>GABA</u> action → ↑ opening frequency of <u>chloride</u> channels → <u>hyperpolarization</u> of the postsynaptic neuronal membrane → ↓ neuronal excitability

* <u>Benzodiazepines</u> decrease the duration of N3 phase in <u>NREM sleep</u>, thereby reducing the occurrence of <u>sleepwalking</u> and <u>night terrors</u>





> [!NOTE]
> Benzo increases the frequenzo of <u>Cl-</u>channel opening.




### Effects [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H1YKRL)




* <u>Anxiolysis</u>

* Sedation

* Hypnotic action

* Muscle relaxation

* <u>Anticonvulsant</u> action

* <u>Amnesia</u>





### Indications




* <u>Anxiety disorders</u>: <u>chlordiazepoxide</u>, <u>diazepam</u>, <u>clonazepam</u>, <u>lorazepam</u>, <u>oxazepam</u>, <u>alprazolam</u>

* <u>Insomnia</u>: <u>flurazepam</u>, <u>temazepam</u>, <u>triazolam</u>, <u>lormetazepam</u>

* <u>Alcohol withdrawal</u>: <u>chlordiazepoxide</u>, <u>diazepam</u>, <u>oxazepam</u>

* <u>Seizures</u> and <u>status epilepticus</u> : <u>diazepam</u>, <u>lorazepam</u>, <u>midazolam</u>, <u>clonazepam</u>

* Preoperative and <u>procedural sedation</u>: <u>diazepam</u>, <u>lorazepam</u>, <u>midazolam</u>

* Other indications include <u>skeletal muscle</u> <u>spasticity</u>, <u>catatonia</u>, <u>eclampsia</u>, <u>sleepwalking</u>, <u>night terrors</u>, and <u>chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting</u>.





> [!NOTE]
> Lorazepam, Oxazepam, and Temazepam are preferred for individuals who drink a LOT (have <u>alcoholic liver disease</u>), because hepatic dysfunction does not have a strong effect on their metabolism. All <u>benzodiazepines</u> are metabolized by the <u>liver</u>, but these three undergo <u>biotransformation</u> through <u>glucuronidation</u>, not <u>CYP450</u> activation, and are less affected by <u>liver</u> disease. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




### Adverse effects [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H1YKRL)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




#### General adverse effects




* <u>Anterograde amnesia</u>

* Confusion

* Blunted affect

* Residual sedation: sedative effects (e.g., drowsiness) persist beyond the intended time span  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dw1o3Q0)

* Reduced coordination

* Increased risk of injury  (especially with <u>benzodiazepine</u> use in older adults) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s1YtRL)

* Paradoxical reactions to benzodiazepines [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/27YTOq)

* Includes restlessness, irritability, impulsivity, and disinhibition.

* Risk groups: older adults and children, individuals with a history of a <u>substance use disorder</u> or another psychiatric condition.

* Treatment: discontinuation of the drug

* Rebound phenomenon

* Reemergence of symptoms (e.g., <u>depression</u>, <u>insomnia</u>, and <u>anxiety</u>) upon discontinuation of <u>benzodiazepine</u> therapy, often with increased intensity

* Especially common in the treatment of <u>sleep disorders</u>

* Risk of overdose

* <u>Benzodiazepines</u> combined with other <u>CNS depressants</u> (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, <u>opioids</u>, <u>barbiturates</u>, <u>antihistamines</u>) can increase the risk of life-threatening respiratory <u>depression</u>.

* See “<u>Benzodiazepine overdose</u>” for details.

* Tolerance and withdrawal; short-acting agents are associated with a higher risk of dependence [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H1YKRL)

* Risk of developing <u>substance use disorder</u>

* Interactions: <u>cytochrome P450</u> induction





> [!WARNING]
> In older adults, <u>benzodiazepines</u> should be used with extreme caution because they can worsen cognitive impairment, increase the risk of falls, and lead to <u>paradoxical reactions</u>.




#### Benzodiazepine dependence [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H1YKRL)




* Definition: The physical adaptation to sustained use of a substance with the development of tolerance and withdrawal.

* <u>Withdrawal symptoms</u> 

* <u>Autonomic nervous system</u>

* <u>Diaphoresis</u>

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, and <u>anorexia</u>

* <u>Tachycardia</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* Neurological

* <u>Seizures</u>

* <u>Tremors</u>

* <u>Memory</u> impairment

* Psychiatric

* <u>Anxiety</u>

* Depressive mood

* <u>Insomnia</u>

* Withdrawal <u>psychosis</u> with transient optic, auditory, or <u>tactile hallucinations</u>

* Treatment [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lscvEd0)

* Dose tapering 

* Withdrawal management should be done in an inpatient setting.

* Switch from multiple agents to one, preferably <u>diazepam</u>.

* Discontinue gradually over a period of 4–8 weeks.

* Reduce dose no more than once a week

* <u>Seizure</u> prophylaxis (e.g., <u>carbamazepine</u>)

* <u>Acute seizure management</u>





> [!TIP]
> Prolonged <u>benzodiazepine</u> use causes dependence and, potentially, <u>substance use disorder</u>. Treatment involves <u>cognitive-behavioral therapy</u> and psychosocial interventions to facilitate withdrawal and continued abstinence (i.e., <u>psychoeducation</u>, <u>motivational interviewing</u>, <u>cognitive-behavioral therapy</u>).




> [!TIP]
> <u>Benzodiazepine dependence</u> can already develop after a few weeks of use. Therefore, <u>benzodiazepines</u> should only be prescribed when strongly indicated.




> [!TIP]
> <u>Benzodiazepine dependence</u> in pregnant individuals may lead to <u>withdrawal symptoms</u> in the <u>newborn</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> Acute withdrawal and <u>seizures</u> can be precipitated by using <u>flumazenil</u> to treat <u>benzodiazepine overdose</u> in patients with <u>benzodiazepine dependence</u>.




### Contraindications for benzodiazepines [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s1YtRL)




* Hypersensitivity to <u>benzodiazepines</u>

* <u>Narrow-angle glaucoma</u>

* Respiratory <u>depression</u> (<u>COPD</u>, <u>respiratory failure</u>)

* <u>Myasthenia gravis</u>

* History of a <u>substance use disorder</u> (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, <u>recreational drugs</u>, prescription medications), except in the treatment of acute <u>alcohol</u> or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal

* <u>Pregnancy</u> (except for the <u>management of eclampsia</u> following unsuccessful <u>magnesium sulfate</u> therapy)  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)

## Nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics (Z-drugs)

### Agents [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




* Zolpidem (imidazopyridine): <u>half-life</u> ∼ 2 hours

* Zaleplon (pyrazolopyrimidine): <u>half-life</u> ∼ 1 hour

* Eszopiclone: <u>half-life</u> ∼ 6 hours





### Mechanism of action [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




* Similar to <u>benzodiazepines</u>

* Selectivity for <u>GABA<sub>A</sub> receptors</u> with α1 subunits (corresponding to the BZ<sub>1</sub> subtype)

* Short-acting due to fast metabolization by <u>liver</u> enzymes





### Effects [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




* Sedation

* Hypnotic action

* Less effect on <u>sleep</u> architecture than <u>benzodiazepines</u>

* Less effective than <u>benzodiazepines</u> as an <u>anticonvulsant</u> and an <u>anxiolytic</u>





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Z-drugs</u> give you the Zzzz's (make you sleepy).




### Indications



* <u>Insomnia</u>




### Adverse effects [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6NYjXp)




* <u>Ataxia</u>

* <u>Headaches</u>

* Confusion

* Psychomotor <u>depression</u>

* <u>Amnesia</u>

* <u>Residual sedation</u>

* Impaired cognitive functions

* Risk of overdose: See “<u>Nonbenzodiazepine hypnotic overdose</u>.”

* Tolerance and withdrawal [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rE1fCi0)

* Risk profile is similar to that of <u>benzodiazepines</u>

* After long-term use, the daily dose should be tapered gradually (there is a risk of developing <u>withdrawal symptoms</u> and <u>memory</u> impairment upon sudden cessation of therapy).

* Risk of developing <u>substance use disorder</u>





### Contraindications



* Similar to <u>contraindications for benzodiazepines</u> (see the corresponding section above)

## Barbiturates

### Agents [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sBatXM)




* Ultra-short acting:  (<u>half-life</u> 15 minutes–3 hours): thiopental, methohexital

* Short-acting:  (<u>half-life</u> 3–6 hours): pentobarbital, secobarbital

* Intermediate-acting (<u>half-life</u> 6–12 hours): amobarbital, butalbital

* Long-acting:  (<u>half-life</u> 12–24 hours): phenobarbital, primidone





### Mechanism of action




* Bind to <u>GABA<sub>A</sub> receptors</u>;   → ↑ duration of the <u>GABA</u>-gated <u>chloride</u> channel opening → ↑ intracellular <u>Cl<sup>-</sup></u>-flow → <u>hyperpolarization</u> of postsynaptic <u>neurons</u> → ↓ neuronal excitability

* Additional, non-<u>GABA</u>-dependent, mechanisms of action

* ↓ <u>Glutamate</u> signaling

* Membrane effects similar to those of <u>inhalational anesthetics</u>

* High lipid solubility of <u>barbiturates</u> leads to:

* Rapid onset of action

* Accumulation in skeletal and adipose tissue → prolonged duration of action





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Benzodiazepines</u> increase the frequency of <u>chloride</u> channel opening, whereas barbiDURATes increase the DURATion of <u>chloride</u> channel opening.




### Effects




* Dose-dependent effects

* Low dose: sedative

* Medium dose: hypnotic

* High dose: general <u>anesthetic</u>

* Decreased <u>intracranial pressure</u> due to reduced <u>cerebral blood flow</u>

* <u>Antiepileptic</u>

* Little to no <u>analgesic</u> or <u>muscle relaxant</u> effects





### Indications




* Ultra-short acting

* <u>General anesthesia</u> (see “<u>Intravenous anesthetics</u>”)

* <u>Status epilepticus</u>

* Sedation;  for <u>electroconvulsive therapy</u> (<u>methohexital</u>)

* Reduction of <u>intracranial pressure</u> for <u>brain edema</u> following trauma or <u>surgery</u>

* Short-acting and intermediate-acting

* Preanesthetic sedation

* Short-term treatment for <u>insomnia</u> or <u>anxiety disorders</u>

* <u>Tension-type headache</u>

* Long-acting

* Generalized-onset and focal-onset <u>seizures</u> (see “<u>Anticonvulsant drugs</u>”)

* <u>Phenobarbital</u>

* <u>Status epilepticus</u>

* <u>Neonatal seizures</u>

* Preanesthetic sedation

* <u>Alcohol withdrawal</u>

* <u>Gilbert syndrome</u> (to reduce <u>hyperbilirubinemia</u>)

* <u>Primidone</u>: <u>essential tremor</u>





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Barbiturates</u> are no longer used for sedation or long-term treatment of <u>insomnia</u> due to their low safety margin. They have been replaced by more effective drugs with fewer side effects (e.g., <u>benzodiazepines</u>).




### Adverse effects




For overdose and <u>poisoning</u>, see “<u>Barbiturate overdose</u>.”




* <u>Hypotension</u> (dose-dependent)

* Respiratory <u>depression</u>;  and/or <u>apnea</u> (dose-dependent): <u>Barbiturates</u> have a narrower margin of safety than <u>benzodiazepines</u>.

* Tolerance and withdrawal

* <u>CNS depression</u>, especially when used with other <u>CNS depressants</u> (e.g., <u>benzodiazepines</u>, <u>alcohol</u>)

* Toxicity

* <u>Laryngospasm</u>, <u>bronchospasm</u> (due to <u>histamine</u> release)

* <u>Myoclonus</u>

* Interactions: <u>cytochrome P450</u> induction (leads to a variety of possible <u>drug interactions</u>)





#### Accidental intraarterial injection of barbiturates




* Etiology: incorrect injection of <u>barbiturates</u>

* Clinical features: tissue <u>necrosis</u>; <u>gangrene</u> (through vessel injury, spasm, and <u>thrombosis</u>)

* Treatment

* Intraarterial dilution with NaCl 0.9% and injection of <u>heparin</u> to prevent <u>thrombosis</u>

* Termination of vessel spasm

* Intraarterial administration of 5–10 mL of 1% <u>lidocaine</u>

* Axillary plexus or <u>stellate ganglion block</u> to block <u>sympathetic</u> nerve fibers that uphold the vessel spasm





#### Barbiturate dependence




* The physical adaptation to sustained use of <u>barbiturates</u> with the development of tolerance and withdrawal.

* <u>Withdrawal symptoms</u>

* <u>CNS</u> stimulation (e.g., <u>insomnia</u>, irritability)

* <u>Autonomic dysfunction</u> (e.g., <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>)

* <u>Dysphoria</u>, <u>delirium</u>

* <u>Tremors</u>, <u>seizures</u>

* Life-threatening cardiovascular dysfunction





### Contraindications




* <u>Porphyria</u>

* <u>Severe anemia</u>

* <u>Myasthenia gravis</u>

* <u>Addison disease</u>

* <u>Liver</u> or <u>kidney</u> disease

* <u>Thyroid disorders</u>

* <u>Asthma</u>

## Melatonin agonists

### Agents




* Ramelteon

* Tasimelteon

* Agomelatine





> [!NOTE]
> Ramelteon, tasimelteon, and agomelatine are melatonin <u>receptor</u> <u>agonists</u>.




### Mechanism of action




* Activation of MT1 and MT2 <u>receptors</u> in <u>suprachiasmatic nuclei</u> of the <u>hypothalamus</u> → quicker <u>sleep</u> onset

* Do not cause dependence





### Indications




* <u>Insomnia</u>, especially in older adults

* <u>Circadian rhythm</u> disorders





### Adverse effects




* <u>Headache</u>

* <u>Dizziness</u>

* Fatigue

* <u>Nausea</u>

* <u>Arthralgias</u>

* <u>Angioedema</u> (rare)

* Interactions: <u>CYP1A2</u> inhibitors (e.g., <u>fluvoxamine</u>) increase blood concentration.





### Contraindications [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6WcjmY0)



* <u>Hypersensitivity reactions</u>

## Orexin antagonists

### Agents



* Suvorexant




### Mechanism of action



* <u>Antagonism</u> of <u>orexin</u> (<u>hypocretin</u>) <u>receptors</u> → inhibits the binding of neuropeptides <u>orexin</u> A and B → ↓ wakefulness and arousal




> [!NOTE]
> Suvorex-ant is an orexin ant<u>agonist</u>.




### Indications



* <u>Insomnia</u>




### Adverse effects [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fXbkxH)




* <u>Headache</u>

* <u>Sleep</u> disturbances: abnormal dreams, nightmares, <u>sleep terrors</u>

* <u>CNS depression</u> (e.g., <u>somnolence</u>)

* Tolerance and withdrawal (rare)

* <u>Suicidal ideation</u>

* Interactions: <u>CYP3A4</u> inhibitors





### Contraindications




* <u>Liver</u> disease

* <u>Narcolepsy</u>
