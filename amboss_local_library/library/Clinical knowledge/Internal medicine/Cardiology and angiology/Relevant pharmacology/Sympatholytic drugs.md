# Sympatholytic drugs

## Summary

Sympatholytic agents inhibit the activity of the <u>sympathetic nervous system</u>, which is mediated by <u>epinephrine</u> and <u>norepinephrine</u>. They act primarily by blocking the postsynaptic <u>adrenergic receptors</u> (alpha and <u>beta receptor</u> <u>antagonism</u>) in target organs or by inhibiting the synthesis and storage of <u>endogenous</u> <u>catecholamines</u> (mainly <u>norepinephrine</u>). A sympatholytic effect can also be achieved via stimulation of the presynaptic <u>alpha-2 receptor</u> with an <u>alpha-2 agonist</u>, which inhibits the release of <u>catecholamines</u>. Sympatholytic drugs are most commonly used in the treatment of <u>ischemic heart disease</u> and <u>hypertension</u> but may also be used for <u>urinary retention</u> secondary to <u>benign prostatic hyperplasia</u> and for psychiatric conditions such as <u>anxiety disorders</u> and <u>posttraumatic stress disorder</u>.




<u>Beta receptor</u> <u>antagonists</u> are discussed in detail in a separate article.

---

## Overview of sympatholytic drugs

All sympatholytic drugs inhibit the activity of the <u>sympathetic nervous system</u> via one of the following mechanisms:




* Inhibition of <u>catecholamine</u> <u>receptors</u>

* Inhibition of the production, storage, and/or release of <u>catecholamines</u> (especially <u>norepinephrine</u>)

> [!TIP]
> All drug groups that directly inhibit the <u>sympathetic nervous system</u> (i.e., <u>alpha receptor antagonists</u>, <u>beta receptor</u> <u>antagonists</u>, and drugs that reduce <u>sympathetic</u> tone) are treatment options for <u>arterial hypertension</u>.
