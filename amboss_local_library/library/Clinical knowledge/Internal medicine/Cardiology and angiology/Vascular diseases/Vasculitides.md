## Summary

Vasculitides are a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune diseases characterized by blood vessel inflammation (vasculitis). Inflammation can lead to <u>ischemia</u>, <u>necrosis</u>, and/or hemorrhage, with subsequent <u>end-organ damage</u>. Vasculitides are either primary (<u>idiopathic</u>) or secondary to an underlying disease (e.g., <u>HBV infection</u>, cancer, <u>systemic lupus erythematosus</u>) or drug use. Vasculitides are further classified based on the size of the affected vessels: small-, medium-, or <u>large-vessel vasculitis</u>, or variable vessel vasculitis. Diagnosing vasculitides is often challenging, as symptoms are usually nonspecific; vasculitides should be considered in patients presenting with <u>constitutional symptoms</u> and signs of multisystem disease (e.g., palpable <u>purpura</u>, pulmonary infiltrates, unexplained <u>ischemic</u> events). <u>Laboratory studies</u>, imaging, and <u>histopathology</u> are often required to confirm the diagnosis. Management should involve a multidisciplinary team (e.g., rheumatology, ophthalmology, <u>neurology</u>) and aims to promptly prevent the progression of vascular inflammation with <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u> to avoid organ damage and <u>death</u>. Treatment of the underlying cause (e.g., with <u>antiviral drugs</u>) and/or symptomatic management (e.g., with <u>NSAIDs</u>) is often necessary.

## General principles

### Etiology [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F51gNh0)




* Primary (<u>idiopathic</u>)

* Secondary to another disease or drug use, e.g.:

* Infectious diseases

* Viral infection: e.g., <u>HBV</u>, <u>HCV</u>, <u>HIV</u>

* <u>Infectious endocarditis</u>

* <u>Tuberculosis</u>

* <u>Syphilis</u>

* Drugs: e.g., <u>hydralazine</u>, <u>cocaine</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u>: e.g., <u>multiple myeloma</u>, <u>lymphoproliferative disorders</u>

* Autoimmune diseases: e.g., <u>systemic lupus erythematosus</u> (<u>SLE</u>), <u>Sjogren syndrome</u>, <u>sarcoidosis</u>, <u>IBD</u>





### Classification [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6tXje-)




* Based on the 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Nomenclature

* Classification is based on the size of the vessel predominantly affected:  

* <u>Large-vessel vasculitis</u>

* <u>Medium-vessel vasculitis</u>

* <u>Small-vessel vasculitis</u>

* <u>ANCA-associated vasculitis of small vessels</u>

* <u>Non-ANCA-associated vasculitis of small vessels</u>

* Variable vessel vasculitis

### Approach to management [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F51gNh0)




Vasculitis should be suspected in patients with <u>constitutional symptoms</u> and multisystemic inflammatory disease. Specific manifestations depend on the vessels affected.




* Tailor the approach to the suspected disease and/or affected organ or system.

* Rule out other (more common) diagnoses (e.g., infections, cancer, other autoimmune diseases).

* Assess for secondary causes of vasculitis (see “Etiology”).

* Consult a rheumatologist and/or other specialists as required.

* Start management based on disease severity and the affected organ or system.





> [!TIP]
> Most patients with vasculitis present with nonspecific features (e.g., <u>constitutional symptoms</u>, elevated <u>inflammatory markers</u>).




#### Initial evaluation




* Perform a thorough history and <u>physical examination</u>; ≥ 1 of the following clinical features are usually found in patients with vasculitis :

* Typical cutaneous lesions (e.g., palpable <u>purpura</u>, <u>livedo reticularis</u>, digital <u>gangrene</u>)

* <u>Pulmonary-renal syndromes</u> (e.g., <u>asthma</u>, <u>hemoptysis</u>, <u>glomerulonephritis</u>)

* Limb <u>claudication</u>, asymmetric pulses and/or blood pressure measurements, <u>bruits</u>

* Temporal <u>headache</u>, visual loss, <u>mononeuritis multiplex</u>

* Findings suggestive of vasculitis on routine studies include:

* <u>CBC</u>: <u>anemia of chronic disease</u>, <u>thrombocytosis</u>, <u>eosinophilia</u>

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u>: ↑ <u>ESR</u>, ↑ <u>CRP</u>

* Infectious diseases workup: positive <u>hepatitis B testing</u> or <u>hepatitis C testing</u>

* Autoimmune diseases workup: negative <u>ANAs</u> and <u>aPL antibodies</u>

* <u>Chest x-ray</u>: <u>lung</u> involvement (e.g., <u>ground glass opacity</u>, nodular lesions)





> [!TIP]
> Vasculitis has a wide variety of signs and symptoms; there is no single typical presentation.




#### Additional evaluation




Consider further diagnostics guided by clinical suspicion to:




* Determine the extent of the disease, e.g.:

* Urine studies and chest CT to evaluate for <u>kidney</u> and <u>lung</u> involvement

* <u>Nerve conduction studies</u>, <u>electromyography</u>, and <u>creatine kinase</u> levels

* Establish the type of vasculitis: e.g., ↓ <u>ANCAs</u> and ↑ <u>cryoglobulins</u> indicates <u>cryoglobulinemic vasculitis</u>

* Confirm the diagnosis: with imaging (e.g., <u>MRA</u>, <u>CTA</u>) or histological studies





#### Management




* <u>Immunosuppressive therapy</u> is generally indicated.

* Surgical therapy may be required in specific cases (e.g., those with limb <u>ischemia</u>).

* <u>Supportive care</u>

* Provide <u>pain management</u>.

* <u>Prevent complications of glucocorticoid therapy</u>.

* Monitor <u>adverse effects of immunosuppressants</u>.

* Consider <u>PCP prophylaxis</u>.
