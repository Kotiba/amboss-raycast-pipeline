## Summary

The heart is a muscular organ located in the <u>middle mediastinum</u> that pumps blood through the <u>circulatory system</u>. The heart is surrounded by the <u>pericardium</u> and is divided into four chambers: two <u>atria</u> and two ventricles. The <u>right atrium</u> and ventricle are often referred to as the right heart while the <u>left atrium</u> and ventricle are often referred to as the left heart. The <u>atria</u> and ventricles are separated by the <u>atrioventricular valves</u>, while the ventricles and the arterial outflow tracts of the heart (namely the <u>pulmonary trunk</u> and the aorta) are separated by the <u>semilunar valves</u>. The heart wall consists of the <u>endocardium</u> (innermost), the <u>myocardium</u>, and the <u>epicardium</u>. The <u>conduction system of the heart</u> is composed of specialized nodes and pacemaker cells that initiate and coordinate the contraction of the heart.
The right heart receives deoxygenated blood from the <u>systemic circulation</u> and pumps it through the <u>pulmonary circulation</u>, where it becomes oxygenated. The left heart then receives the oxygenated blood from the <u>pulmonary circulation</u> and pumps it through the <u>blood vessels</u> of the <u>systemic circulation</u>. The <u>coronary arteries</u>, namely the <u>right coronary artery</u> and the <u>left coronary artery</u>, arise in the root of the aorta and supply the <u>myocardium</u> and <u>endocardium</u>. The heart develops embryologically from the heart tube, which undergoes looping and septation to separate it into the four chambers.

## Gross anatomy

### Overview




* Characteristics

* Two ventricles and two <u>atria</u>, which connect the <u>pulmonary circulation</u> with the <u>systemic circulation</u>

* Four valves, which ensure that blood flow occurs in only one direction

* Roughly the size of a fist

* Weighs approx. 300–500 g

* Surrounded by <u>pericardium</u> (a fibroserous, fluid-filled sac)

* Location: in the <u>middle mediastinum</u> between the <u>lungs</u> 

* <u>Anterior</u> to the heart: <u>sternum</u> and <u>rib</u> <u>cartilage</u>

* <u>Posterior</u> to the heart 

* <u>Vertebral column</u> (T5–T8)

* <u>Esophagus</u>

* <u>Carina</u> and primary <u>bronchi</u>

* The upper part of the heart is at the level of the third costal <u>cartilage</u>.
* The site of attachment for the venae cavae, aorta, and <u>pulmonary trunk</u>

* The lower part of the heart (cardiac apex) lies left of the <u>sternum</u> at the level between the fourth and fifth <u>ribs</u>.

* Function

* Pumps blood through the body via the <u>circulatory system</u>

* <u>ANP</u> synthesis





> [!NOTE]
> The <u>left atrium</u> is the posteriormost part of the heart, located directly in front of the <u>esophagus</u>. It can be visualized using <u>TEE</u>. The <u>right ventricle</u> is the anteriormost part of the heart and is at greatest risk of injury following chest trauma.




> [!NOTE]
> The cardiac apex beat can typically be palpated to the left of the <u>sternum</u>, <u>medial</u> to the <u>midclavicular line</u> at the 4<sup>th</sup>–5<sup>th</sup> <u>intercostal space</u>. In patients with <u>dextrocardia</u>, the orientation of the heart is inverse so the apex is located to the right of the <u>mediastinum</u> rather than the left.

### Heart chambers




#### Two atria




Separated by the interatrial septum (The fossa ovalis is visible on the septum as a small oval-shaped <u>depression</u> in the <u>interatrial septum</u>.)




* Right atrium

* Receives deoxygenated blood via the <u>superior vena cava</u> (<u>SVC</u>), <u>inferior vena cava</u> (<u>IVC</u>), and the <u>coronary veins</u>

* Pumps deoxygenated blood into the <u>right ventricle</u>

* Right auricle (<u>right atrial appendage</u>)

* Muscular pouch that acts to increase the capacity of the <u>atrium</u>

* Located close to the <u>ascending aorta</u>

* Separated from the <u>atrium</u> by the terminal sulcus (sulcus terminalis) and terminal crest (crista terminalis).

* Cavotricuspid isthmus, a region of fibrous tissue between the opening of the <u>inferior vena cava</u> and the <u>tricuspid valve</u>, is a common location of the re-entry circuit in <u>atrial flutter</u>.

* Common site for the development of <u>thrombi</u> in patients with <u>atrial fibrillation</u>

* Left atrium

* Receives oxygenated blood from the four <u>pulmonary veins</u>

* Pumps oxygenated blood into the <u>left ventricle</u> to enter the <u>systemic circulation</u>

* Left auricle (<u>left atrial appendage</u>)

* Muscular pouch that acts to increase the capacity of the <u>atrium</u>

* Extends from superior aspect of the chamber in close proximity to the root of the <u>pulmonary trunk</u>

* Common site for the development of <u>thrombi</u> in patients with <u>atrial fibrillation</u>





### Two ventricles of the heart




* Right ventricle

* Receives deoxygenated blood from the <u>right atrium</u>

* Pumps deoxygenated blood into the <u>pulmonary trunk</u> as part of the <u>pulmonary circulation</u>

* Moderator band: a muscular structure located in the <u>right ventricle</u> that extends from the base of the <u>anterior</u> <u>papillary muscle</u> to the ventricular septum; acts as part of the electrical conduction pathway

* Left ventricle

* Receives oxygenated blood from the <u>left atrium</u>

* Pumps oxygenated blood into the aorta via the left ventricular outflow tract as part of the <u>systemic circulation</u>

### Heart valves




* Two types of cardiac valves that differ in location and morphology

* Cardiac skeleton 

* Consists of four fibrous rings (annuli fibrosi <u>cordis</u>)  that surround the atrioventricular and arterial orifices

* Function

* Separates <u>atria</u> and ventricles

* Provides anchor and structural support for the valves

* Provides electrical insulation layer between <u>atria</u> and ventricles

* Closure of <u>heart valves</u> produces <u>heart sounds</u> (See <u>auscultation of the heart</u> in <u>cardiovascular examination</u> for details.)





> [!NOTE]
> “Try <u>PUL</u>ling My AORTA“: Tricuspidal, Pulmonary, Mitral, Aortic is the order in which blood flows through the <u>heart valves</u>.




![Overview of the cardiac skeleton](../../../../../media/axba5l6rx6miojyev3oj9tta.jpg)

Overview of the cardiac skeleton




#### Atrioventricular valves




* Structure: leaflets supported by subvalvular apparatus

* Valves

* Tricuspid valve

* Consists of three leaflets

* Located between <u>right atrium</u> and <u>right ventricle</u>

* Mitral valve (<u>bicuspid valve</u>)

* Consists of two leaflets

* Located between <u>left atrium</u> and <u>left ventricle</u>

* Subvalvular apparatus

* Chordae tendineae: fibrous cords that support the <u>AV valves</u> and connect them to the <u>papillary muscles</u>

* Papillary muscles (two in the <u>left ventricle</u>; three in the <u>right ventricle</u>)

* Derive from the <u>myocardium</u>

* Extend from the <u>anterior</u> and <u>posterior</u> ventricular walls and the septum

* Have apices that are attached to the <u>chordae tendineae</u>

* Contract during <u>systole</u> and thereby tighten the <u>chordae tendineae</u>: prevent prolapse of valve leaflets and <u>regurgitation</u> into the <u>atria</u> when pressure rises during ventricular contraction





> [!NOTE]
> The tricuspid valve has three leaflets and is located on the right side, as is the three-lobed right <u>lung</u>. The bicuspid (mitral) valve has two leaflets and is located on the left side, as is the two-lobed left <u>lung</u>.




#### Semilunar valves




* Structure: three crescent-shaped cusps without subvalvular apparatus

* Valves

* Pulmonary valve: located between <u>right ventricle</u> and <u>pulmonary trunk</u>

* Aortic valve

* Located between <u>left ventricle</u> and aorta

* Consists of three leaflets and the aortic sinuses (a space immediately above the <u>aortic valve</u>, between an <u>aortic valve</u> leaflet and the wall of the <u>ascending aorta</u>)

* There are three <u>aortic sinuses</u> (one above each aortic leaflet): the left, right, and <u>posterior</u> <u>aortic sinuses</u>.

* The left and right <u>aortic sinus</u> give rise to the left and <u>right coronary artery</u>.

* The three <u>aortic sinuses</u> together with the <u>aortic valve</u> leaflets form a dilation of the root of the aorta called the aortic bulb.

### Coronary arteries




* The left and right <u>coronary arteries</u> arise from the root of the aorta and supply the <u>heart muscle</u> with arterial blood.

* Coronary arterial dominance 

* Right-dominant (∼ 85% of the population): <u>posterior descending artery</u> (<u>PDA</u>) supplied by the <u>RCA</u>

* Left-dominant (∼ 8% of the population): <u>PDA</u> supplied by the <u>left circumflex artery</u> (<u>LCX</u>)

* <u>Codominant</u> (balanced; ∼ 7% of people): <u>PDA</u> supplied by both <u>RCA</u> and <u>LCX</u>

* Coronary blood flow peaks during early <u>diastole</u> at a point when the pressure differential between the aorta and the ventricle is the greatest (see <u>left ventricular</u> <u>pressure-volume diagram</u>.)

#### Venous drainage



* Coronary sinus

* Largest <u>vein</u> of the heart, into which all other <u>coronary veins</u> drain

* Lies in the left <u>posterior</u> atrioventricular groove

* Drains into the <u>right atrium</u> between the <u>IVC</u> orifice and the right atrioventricular orifice

* <u>Veins draining into coronary sinus</u> (coronary veins):

* Left side of the heart: great cardiac <u>vein</u> and <u>posterior</u> cardiac <u>vein</u>(s)

* Right side of the heart: small cardiac <u>vein</u> and middle cardiac <u>vein</u>

#### Structures of cardiac conduction system




* Definition: collection of nodes and specialized conduction cells that initiate and coordinate contraction of the <u>heart muscle</u>.

* Components

* <u>Sinoatrial node</u> (<u>SA node</u>): in the upper wall of the <u>right atrium</u> (at the junction where the <u>SVC</u> enters)

* <u>Atrioventricular node</u> (<u>AV node</u>): within the AV septum (superior and <u>medial</u> to the opening of the <u>coronary sinus</u> in the <u>right atrium</u>)

* <u>Atrioventricular bundle</u> (<u>bundle of His</u>): directly below the <u>cardiac skeleton</u> within the membranous part of the interventricular septum

* <u>Purkinje fibers</u> (modified <u>myocytes</u>): terminal conducting fibers in the subendocardium





See “<u>Conducting system of the heart</u>“ in “<u>Cardiac physiology</u>“ for more details.

## Microscopic anatomy

### Layers of the heart




* The heart wall itself consists of three layers (from inside to outside):

* <u>Endocardium</u>

* <u>Myocardium</u>

* <u>Epicardium</u>: <u>connective tissue</u> layer attached to the outside of the <u>myocardium</u>, i.e., <u>visceral</u> layer of serous <u>pericardium</u>

* <u>Pericardium</u>: membrane that directly surrounds the heart

#### Endocardium



* Description: innermost layer of heart tissue, consisting of the following three sublayers:

* <u>Endothelium</u> (innermost): simple <u>squamous epithelium</u>

* A layer of <u>loose connective tissue</u>

* Subendocardium (outermost): <u>loose connective tissue</u> containing

* Cardiac Purkinje cells

* Specialized/modified <u>cardiomyocytes</u> that are part of the <u>conducting system of the heart</u>

* Contain fewer contractile <u>myofibrils</u> and more <u>mitochondria</u>, <u>glycogen</u>, and <u>gap junctions</u> than normal <u>cardiomyocytes</u>.

* <u>Veins</u> and nerves




![Endocardium and subendocardial tissue](../../../../../media/fnqmnbts2z15c1nxrhua0udc.jpg)

Endocardium and subendocardial tissue




#### Myocardium



* Description: thick <u>myocardial</u> layer composed of the following:

* Cardiomyocytes: <u>striated muscle</u> cells containing a single, centrally located <u>nucleus</u>  

* Contain many <u>mitochondria</u>, which produce <u>ATP</u> for contraction.

* <u>Myofibrils</u> within <u>cardiomyocytes</u> are organized into <u>sarcomeres</u> (smallest functional contractile unit of <u>cardiac muscle</u>).

* Connected by intercalated discs to form long fibers.

* <u>Intercalated discs</u> connect individual <u>cardiomyocytes</u> into a <u>functional syncytium</u> and force transmission during muscle contraction.

* They contain adherent junctions (transmit mechanical stimuli) and <u>gap junctions</u> (transmit electrical stimuli)

* They appear as slightly darker-staining lines between <u>cardiac muscle cells</u> under <u>light microscopy</u> and <u>electron microscopy</u>.

* See also ”<u>Cardiac muscle</u>” in the article on <u>muscle tissue</u>.

* Atrial <u>cardiomyocytes</u> release <u>atrial natriuretic peptide</u> (<u>ANP</u>) when stretched (i.e., at higher BPs) → ↑ water and <u>sodium</u> excretion by the <u>kidneys</u> → ↓ BP

* <u>Fibroblasts</u> (these become <u>myofibroblasts</u> after injury)

* <u>Extracellular matrix</u>: <u>collagen</u>, <u>elastin</u>, and <u>glycosaminoglycans</u>




> [!NOTE]
> Damaged <u>myocardial</u> tissue is replaced by noncontractile <u>scar</u> tissue (<u>fibrosis</u>) that does not conduct electrical impulses well and, thus, predisposes to <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>.

#### Pericardium




* Description

* Fibroserous sac enclosing the heart

* Defines the <u>pericardial cavity</u>

* <u>Pericardial</u> layers

* Serous <u>pericardium</u> (innermost)

* <u>Visceral</u> layer of serous <u>pericardium</u> (epicardium)

* Outermost layer of the heart wall

* Separated from the parietal layer of the serous <u>pericardium</u> by the <u>pericardial cavity</u>.

* Secretes lubricating serous fluid into the <u>pericardial cavity</u>

* Contains <u>blood vessels</u>, <u>lymphatics</u>, and <u>adipose tissue</u>

* Parietal layer of serous <u>pericardium</u>

* Fibrous <u>pericardium</u> (outermost)

* Pericardial cavity: space between the <u>visceral</u> and parietal layers of the serous <u>pericardium</u> that contains serous, <u>pericardial</u> fluid

* Innervation: innervated by the <u>phrenic nerve</u>





> [!NOTE]
> The capacity of the <u>pericardial cavity</u> is limited by the stiff, fibrous <u>pericardium</u>. If fluid abnormally accumulates in the <u>pericardial space</u> (<u>pericardial effusion</u>), intrapericardial pressure increases and may impair cardiac function → <u>pericardial tamponade</u>.




> [!NOTE]
> Because of the sensory innervation of the <u>pericardium</u> by the <u>phrenic nerve</u>, <u>pericarditis</u> can result in <u>referred pain</u> to the neck, arms, or shoulders (often the left side).

### Microscopy of the <u>heart valves</u>




* Composed of <u>connective tissue</u> and <u>endocardium</u>

* Mostly vessel-free, with nutrition derived from the surrounding blood (This makes valvular involvement in <u>endocarditis</u> difficult to treat because both the cells of the <u>immune system</u> and <u>antibiotics</u> typically reach sites of infection via the <u>circulatory system</u>.)

## Embryology

### Overview




* Mesodermal origin

* <u>Mesoderm</u> → <u>myocardium</u>

* <u>Mesoderm</u> → <u>mesothelium</u> → <u>pericardium</u>

* <u>Mesoderm</u> → <u>endothelium</u> → <u>endocardium</u>, <u>blood vessels</u>, <u>lymphatics</u>

* Steps of heart development
1. * Two single endocardial tubes merge to form the heart tube.
2. * The straight heart tube transforms into an S-shaped heart loop.
3. * <u>Endocardial cushions</u> grow towards each other and fuse to separate the <u>atria</u> and ventricles.
4. * Two <u>atria</u> and two ventricles form and one common outflow tract divides into an aortic trunk and a <u>pulmonary trunk</u>.
5. * Valves form from the <u>endocardial cushion</u>.

* <u>Fetal circulation</u> is covered in the corresponding section of <u>prenatal and postnatal physiology</u>.

* Postnatal derivatives of fetal vascular structures are covered in the section on postnatal adaptation of the circulatory and respiratory system in the <u>prenatal and postnatal physiology</u> article.

##### Atrial septation



1. * Development of the <u>septum primum</u> and <u>foramen primum</u>

* Initially, the two <u>atria</u> communicate via the foramen primum.

* The septum primum, located cranially, grows <u>caudally</u> towards the <u>dorsal</u> <u>endocardial cushion</u>.
2. * Narrowing of the <u>foramen primum</u> occurs as the <u>septum primum</u> grows towards the <u>endocardial cushion</u>.
3. * Development of the foramen secundum

* Before fusion of the <u>septum primum</u> with the <u>endocardial cushion</u> is complete, an opening appears in the <u>cranial</u> end of the <u>septum primum</u>, known as the <u>foramen secundum</u>, through which interatrial communication continues.

* Ensures right-to-left shunting of the blood
4. * Development of the septum secundum
* A second muscular septum begins to grow on the right side of the <u>septum primum</u> in two segments:

* The superior part grows <u>caudally</u> from the roof of the primitive <u>right atrium</u>.

* The inferior part grows cranially from the <u>endocardial cushion</u>.
5. * Development of the <u>foramen ovale cordis</u>

* The space between the two parts of the <u>septum secundum</u> is known as the foramen ovale.

* The superior portion of the <u>septum primum</u> regresses.

* The inferior portion of the <u>septum primum</u> persists and acts as a one-way valve, allowing blood to flow from the <u>right atrium</u> into the <u>left atrium</u>.
6. * Formation of the atrial septum (no further interatrial communication)

* <u>Closure of the foramen ovale</u> occurs shortly after <u>birth</u>, when the left atrial pressure increases (due to loss of low resistance <u>placental</u> circulation) and the <u>right atrial pressure</u> decreases (due to increased <u>pulmonary circulation</u> upon <u>lung</u> inflation).

* The <u>septum secundum</u> completely fuses with the remnant of the <u>septum primum</u> to form the atrial septum during <u>infancy</u>.




> [!NOTE]
> If the <u>septum primum</u> and <u>septum secundum</u> fail to fuse after <u>birth</u>, a <u>patent foramen ovale</u> (<u>PFO</u>) remains. Later in life, a <u>PFO</u> can result in a <u>paradoxical embolus</u>, in which a <u>venous thrombus</u> may travel via the <u>PFO</u> from the venous to the arterial circulation and cause end-organ <u>infarction</u> (e.g., <u>stroke</u>).

##### Ventricular septation



1. * A <u>caudally</u> located muscular interventricular septum forms with an <u>interventricular foramen</u> between the two ventricles.
2. * The cranially developing <u>aorticopulmonary septum</u> rotates and <u>caudally</u> fuses with the <u>muscular interventricular septum</u>, and this fusion forms the membranous interventricular septum and closes the <u>interventricular foramen (heart)</u>.
3. * <u>Endocardial cushions</u> grow to further separate the ventricles and the <u>atria</u>.




> [!NOTE]
> Abnormal development of the <u>membranous interventricular septum</u> results in a <u>ventricular septal defect</u>, the most common congenital cardiac anomaly.

#### Development of the outflow tract




* Sequence of events
1. * Development of the aorticopulmonary septum (<u>AP septum</u>)

* <u>Neural crest</u> and endocardial cells migrate to form truncal ridges and bulbar ridges from the <u>truncus arteriosus</u> and <u>bulbus cordis</u>, respectively.

* Truncal and <u>bulbar ridges</u> spiral and fuse to form the <u>AP septum</u>.
2. * Division of the ventricular outflow tract
* The <u>aorticopulmonary septum</u> fuses and rotates 180°, dividing the outflow tract into the aorta (from the <u>left ventricle</u>) and <u>pulmonary trunk</u> (from the <u>right ventricle</u>).

* Associated <u>conotruncal abnormalities</u> 

* Failure to spiral: <u>transposition of the great vessels</u>

* Malaligned <u>AP septum</u>: <u>tetralogy of Fallot</u>

* Partial <u>AP septum</u> development: <u>persistent truncus arteriosus</u>
