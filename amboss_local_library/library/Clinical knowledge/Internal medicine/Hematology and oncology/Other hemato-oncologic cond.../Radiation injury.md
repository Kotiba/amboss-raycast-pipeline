# Radiation injury

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Hematology and oncology > Other hemato-oncologic conditions > Radiation injury*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O80I53)

---

## Summary

Radiation injury can be caused by a single whole-body exposure to a high dose of <u>ionizing radiation</u> (<u>acute radiation syndrome</u>; <u>ARS</u>) or single or multiple exposures of a small area of the body to concentrated radiation, e.g., <u>radiotherapy</u> (RT).




<u>ARS</u> is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition caused by unintentional exposure to radioactive material or a nuclear explosion. Initial symptoms are nonspecific and may subsequently coalesce into one or more <u>ARS subsyndromes</u>. Management includes prompt treatment of concomitant injuries, screening for nuclear contamination, assessing the level of <u>ionizing radiation</u> exposure, monitoring for <u>bone marrow suppression</u> and impaired gastrointestinal function, and coordinating multiple specialty consultations (e.g., <u>hematology</u>, gastroenterology, burn specialists).




Radiation injuries following RT are common. Radiation injuries are categorized by the anatomical site of the RT, time elapsed since RT, and patient <u>risk factors</u>. Early adverse effects of RT are caused by radiation-induced proinflammatory states and include <u>radiation pneumonitis</u> and <u>acute radiation proctitis</u>. Late adverse effects are commonly related to <u>fibrosis</u> (<u>radiation fibrosis syndrome</u>), secondary <u>malignancy</u>, and/or tissue <u>ischemia</u> caused by accelerated vascular disease. Management of <u>local radiation injuries</u> is injury specific.




Although radiation injuries to <u>health care providers</u> are rare, providers should be aware of <u>radiation safety</u> recommendations, such as <u>occupational radiological protection</u> and <u>radiological incident protection</u>.

## Overview

### Applied physics [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/trWXQ50)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)




* Definitions

* Radiation: energy that moves through space as a particle or wave

* <u>Nonionizing radiation</u>: low energy radiation that can cause <u>thermal injuries</u>, e.g., infrared, microwave, <u>radio</u> waves

* <u>Ionizing radiation</u>: radiation capable of ionizing atoms, e.g., <u>x-rays</u>, gamma rays, <u>alpha particles</u>, beta particles

* Radiation <u>absorbed dose</u>

* Amount of <u>ionizing radiation</u> deposited in the body per unit mass

* Measured in gray (Gy) or radiation <u>absorbed dose</u> (rad)

* Radiation <u>equivalent dose</u>

* Derivative of the radiation <u>absorbed dose</u> that factors in the potential damage to biological tissue based on the type of radiation

* Measured in sievert (Sv) or roentgen equivalent man (rem)

* Measurements

* Geiger counter: a device used to detect the presence of <u>ionizing radiation</u> (e.g., contamination with radioactive material) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)

* Radiation dosimeter: measures an individual's total accumulated dose of <u>ionizing radiation</u>; typically worn in the form of a badge





> [!TIP]
> The radiation <u>absorbed dose</u> and radiation <u>equivalent dose</u> are equal for medical imaging (e.g., <u>x-rays</u>, <u>CT scans</u>).




### Types of radiation injury [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/trWXQ50)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)




* <u>Irradiation</u>: All or part of the body has been exposed to <u>ionizing radiation</u>, but there has been no transfer of radioactive material to the patient.

* Contamination: Radioactive materials are present on the surface of the body, in a wound, and/or have been ingested.

* Incorporation: Radioactive material has been absorbed into cells and/or tissues following contamination.





### Pathophysiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)




* High doses of <u>ionizing radiation</u> (e.g., <u>radiotherapy</u>, nuclear accidents) can break <u>DNA</u> strands (direct effect) or generate <u>free radicals</u> that cause cellular damage (indirect effect), leading to inflammation and/or progressive tissue damage.

* Cancer cells have an increased susceptibility to radiation because of high replication rates and dysfunctional <u>DNA repair mechanisms</u>.

* Rapidly regenerating tissues (e.g., <u>bone marrow</u>, GI <u>mucosa</u>, <u>skin</u>) have an increased susceptibility to radiation because of the depletion of <u>stem cells</u>.





### Radiation safety




#### Occupational radiological protection [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FHWgH50)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RHWlJ50)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iHWJJ50)




* Keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable (<u>ALARA</u>).

* Wear a <u>radiation dosimeter</u> and avoid exceeding the recommended cumulative exposure.

* No more than 50 mSv in 1 year [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FHWgH50)

* No more than 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FHWgH50)

* <u>Pregnancy</u>: < 1 mSv for the entire <u>gestation</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FHWgH50)

* Maintain a distance of 2 m from the radiation source.  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tHWXH50)

* Wear a lead apron with ≥ 0.5 mm lead thickness or stay behind an equivalent shield.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FHWgH50)





#### Radiological incident protection [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)




* Precautions for patients with <u>ionizing radiation</u> injuries [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)

* An <u>ionizing radiation</u> injury without contamination does not pose a risk to providers.

* Use <u>standard precautions</u> suitable for coexisting injuries.

* Precautions for patients with radioactive contamination [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)

* <u>Don PPE</u> that includes isolation gown, <u>surgical mask</u>, protective eyewear or face shield, head cover, and shoe covers.

* Wear 2 sets of nitrile gloves.

* Consider N95 or negative pressure <u>respirator</u> with <u>HEPA filter</u> if there is a risk of air dispersion of contaminated material.

* Frequently test gloves and clothing for contamination using a <u>Geiger counter</u> and replace as indicated. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)

## Acute radiation syndrome

### Definition [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




<u>Acute radiation syndrome</u> (<u>ARS</u>) is a collection of signs and symptoms that occur following whole-body (or significant partial-body) exposure to high-dose <u>ionizing radiation</u> over a short period of time.




### Etiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)




* Radiological exposure device

* Radiological dispersal device (“dirty bomb”)

* Nuclear power plant incident

* Nuclear weapon





### Clinical features [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nrW7350)




The onset and severity of symptoms depend on the level of exposure and type of radiation. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)




* <u>Prodromal</u>

* Nonspecific symptoms, e.g., <u>anorexia</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* Typical onset: 0–2 days after exposure [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)

* Latent

* Asymptomatic period following resolution of the <u>prodromal</u> phase

* Typically lasts up to 20 days after exposure [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)

* Manifest illness

* Symptoms depend on the severity and location of radiation exposure and typically coalesce into one or more <u>ARS subsyndromes</u>.

* Typical onset: 21–60 days after exposure





> [!TIP]
> Early symptom manifestation typically suggests significant radiation exposure and a poorer prognosis. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




### Initial management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/trWXQ50)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)




* <u>Don PPE</u>; see “<u>Radiological incident protection</u>.”

* Perform a <u>primary survey</u>: Do not delay life-saving care to decontaminate the patient.

* Assess for radiation contamination.

* Obtain medical and incident history.

* Perform a <u>radioactive contamination assessment</u>.

* Begin <u>radiation decontamination</u> if necessary.

* Alert radiation experts, e.g.:

* Hospital <u>radiation safety</u> personnel

* Poison Control Center: 1-800-222-1222

* Radiation Emergency Assistance Center: 865-576-1005

* <u>Estimate the level of radiation exposure</u>.

* Begin management of <u>ARS subsyndromes</u> in consultation with radiation experts.





> [!WARNING]
> Treat life-threatening emergencies before beginning decontamination; provider exposure to dangerous levels of radiation is highly unlikely. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)




> [!TIP]
> Treatment of <u>ARS</u> is typically not needed for absorption of < 1 Gy. Survival is unlikely if the <u>absorbed dose</u> is > 10 Gy. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




### Diagnostics [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/trWXQ50)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)




For concomitant traumatic injuries, see “<u>Urgent diagnostics for trauma patients</u>.”




#### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)




* Initial studies: to establish baseline organ function and assess for acute abnormalities

* <u>CBC with differential</u>: to determine absolute <u>lymphocyte</u> count

* <u>Type and screen</u>: <u>Transfusion</u> may be required.

* Other studies: <u>BMP</u>, <u>LFTs</u>, <u>lipase</u>, <u>CRP</u>, <u>coagulation panel</u>

* Serial studies: to estimate the severity of radiation exposure [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FrWgQ50)

* <u>CBC with differential</u>: every 6 hours for 48 hours

* Serum <u>amylase</u>: daily for 3 days

* <u>CRP</u>: daily for 3 days





#### Radioactive contamination assessment [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)




* Perform a whole-body radiation scan using a <u>Geiger counter</u>.

* Scan swabs obtained from facial orifices (e.g., nose, mouth) and <u>open wounds</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)

* If internal contamination is suspected, consider scanning 24-hour collections of urine and feces.





#### Radiation dose estimation [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nrW7350)




* Purpose

* Predicts the likelihood of developing <u>ARS</u> and clinical severity

* Guides subsequent management, counseling, and disposition decisions

* Methods

* Time to onset of <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Lymphocyte</u> depletion kinetics

* <u>Dicentric chromosome assay</u>





### Radiation decontamination [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/trWXQ50)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YIWnY50)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nrW7350)




* External decontamination

* Perform <u>body surface decontamination</u>.

* Protect uncontaminated wounds with waterproof dressings.

* If necessary and feasible, remove embedded shrapnel.

* Repeat decontamination up to 2–3 times until surface radiation is less than twice the level of background radiation. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bIWHY50)

* Follow local biohazard protocols for the collection of water and removed material.

* Internal decontamination [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* May be initiated under the guidance of radiation experts [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Treatment varies based on the radioactive isotope and includes:

* <u>Iodine</u>-125 or <u>iodine</u>-131: <u>potassium iodide</u>

* Cesium-137: <u>Prussian blue</u>

* Plutonium-239 or americium-241: diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (DPTA)





### ARS subsyndromes [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SrWyg50)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




<u>ARS</u> is classically divided into four system-based subsyndromes. Management of <u>ARS subsyndromes</u> requires a multidisciplinary approach tailored to the patient's symptoms. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




#### <u>Hematopoietic</u> syndrome [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




<u>Pancytopenia</u> due to <u>bone marrow</u> damage may occur after absorption of approx. 1 Gy.




* Clinical features

* Early: <u>neutropenia</u>, <u>signs of thrombocytopenia</u>

* Late: <u>signs of anemia</u>

* Management may include: [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mJWVum0)

* <u>Neutropenic precautions</u>

* <u>Antibiotic prophylaxis</u>

* <u>Management of neutropenic fever</u>

* <u>Transfusions</u> (e.g., <u>pRBCs</u>, <u>platelet transfusion</u>)

* <u>Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor</u>

* <u>Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation</u> (<u>HSCT</u>)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Red blood cells</u> and <u>platelets</u> are radioresistant, since both are terminally differentiated and do not have a <u>nucleus</u>. However, their precursor cells are radiosensitive, resulting in delayed <u>anemia</u> and <u>thrombocytopenia</u>. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




#### Gastrointestinal syndrome [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




<u>Prodromal</u> symptoms may occur after absorption of < 1.5 Gy; severe symptoms typically occur after approx. 5–6 Gy.




* Clinical features

* <u>Prodromal</u> symptoms: <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, gastric atony

* Severe symptoms: abdominal <u>pain</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>GI bleeding</u>, <u>signs of significant dehydration</u>

* Management may include:

* <u>Antiemetics</u>

* <u>Antidiarrheals</u>

* <u>IV fluid therapy</u>

* <u>Nutritional support</u> (e.g., <u>parenteral nutrition</u>)

* <u>Antibiotic prophylaxis</u>  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lJWv8m0)





#### Cutaneous syndrome [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)




Symptoms occur after absorption of approx. 3 Gy.




* Clinical features

* Early: <u>erythema</u>, <u>edema</u>, <u>hair</u> loss

* Late: desquamation, <u>ulceration</u>, <u>necrosis</u> [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Management may include: [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lJWv8m0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jJW_Fm0)

* <u>Topical steroids</u>

* Topical <u>antibiotics</u>

* <u>Burn management</u>

* Referral to a <u>burn center</u>





#### Neurovascular syndrome [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PJWW8m0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)




Symptoms occur after absorption of approx. 10–20 Gy ; neurovascular syndrome and is universally fatal.




* Clinical features

* <u>Altered mental status</u> (e.g., confusion, <u>disorientation</u>, decreased <u>level of consciousness</u>)

* <u>Headaches</u>

* <u>Seizures</u>

* <u>Ataxia</u>

* <u>Papilledema</u>

* Management may include: [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lJWv8m0)

* <u>Palliative care</u>

* <u>Antiepileptics</u>





### Disposition [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QJWuFm0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Disposition decisions should be made in consultation with radiation experts and include the following factors:

* Severity of symptoms

* Radiation exposure

* Community contamination risk

* Consider transfer to specialized centers as indicated (e.g., <u>trauma center</u>, <u>burn center</u>, <u>HSCT</u> center).

## Local radiation injuries

<u>Local radiation injuries</u> typically result from <u>radiation therapy</u> (RT) administered in high doses (e.g., 40–80 Gy) to a small area of the body as opposed to whole-body radiation that causes <u>ARS</u>.




### Overview [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BrWzj50)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XIW9Y50)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cIWab50)




* The pathophysiology and treatment of <u>local radiation injuries</u> varies by the type, dose, and location of RT.

* <u>Local radiation injuries</u> are classified as early (occurring within weeks of RT) or late (occurring months to years after RT). [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BrWzj50)

* Early injuries are typically caused by a proinflammatory state leading to organ dysfunction.

* Late injuries are often related to radiation-induced <u>fibrosis</u> (<u>radiation fibrosis syndrome</u>), secondary <u>malignancy</u>, and/or accelerated vascular disease.

## Radiation-induced lung injury

<u>Radiation-induced lung injury</u> (RILI) is a complication of thoracic RT, which is commonly used in the <u>treatment of lung cancer</u>, <u>mediastinal</u> <u>lymphoma</u>, and <u>breast cancer</u>. RILI may manifest acutely as <u>radiation pneumonitis</u> or later as <u>radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis</u>. [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJWMum0)




### Radiation pneumonitis [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJWMum0)[[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5JWium0)




* Definition: acute or subacute <u>lung tissue</u> inflammation caused by RT

* Onset: within 3–12 weeks of radiation. [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJWMum0)

* Mechanism of injury: rapid release of <u>proinflammatory cytokines</u> (e.g., <u>IL-1</u>, <u>IL-6</u>) from alveolar cells, resulting in a proliferative reaction that impairs <u>gas exchange</u>

* Clinical features

* Most common: <u>dyspnea</u>, dry <u>cough</u>

* Less common: <u>hemoptysis</u>, <u>fever</u>, asymptomatic

* Diagnostics

* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u> after ruling out alternative diagnoses (e.g., <u>pneumonia</u>, <u>pulmonary embolism</u>) [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5JWium0)

* <u>CXR</u> and CT chest findings vary by phase of injury from normal to <u>ground glass opacities</u>.

* Management: <u>systemic corticosteroids</u>

* Complications

* <u>Respiratory insufficiency</u>

* <u>Radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis</u>





### Radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5JWium0)




* Definition: chronic <u>pulmonary tissue</u> damage caused by <u>sclerotic</u> <u>fibrosis</u> [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJWMum0)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uwWpkn0)

* Onset: typically 6–12 months after RT [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cIWab50)

* Clinical features: progressive <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>cyanosis</u>

* Diagnostics

* CT chest is preferred to <u>CXR</u>

* CT findings include linear scarring with consolidation and volume loss. [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5JWium0)

* Management: <u>supportive care</u>, e.g., <u>oxygen therapy</u>, <u>pulmonary rehabilitation</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Always rule out infection, <u>thrombotic events</u>, and/or cancer recurrence before diagnosing <u>radiation-induced lung injury</u> in patients with a history of thoracic RT. [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5JWium0)

## Radiation-induced gastrointestinal injury

### Radiation-induced esophagitis [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F0dg3o0)




* Onset: most commonly 2–3 weeks after initiation of RT initiated

* Clinical features

* Early: <u>dysphagia</u>, <u>odynophagia</u>, <u>anorexia</u>, <u>nausea</u>, substernal discomfort

* Late: <u>mechanical dysphagia</u>, impaired esophageal motility

* Diagnostics

* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u>

* Endoscopy is used to differentiate radiation injury from <u>infectious esophagitis</u>.

* Management

* Dietary modifications (e.g., soft, bland diet)

* Symptomatic management

* <u>Topical analgesics</u> (e.g., oral viscous <u>lidocaine</u>)

* <u>Antacids</u> and acid suppressor medications (e.g., <u>PPI</u>, <u>H2 blockers</u>)

* Complications: <u>esophageal stricture</u>, <u>esophageal perforation</u>, <u>tracheoesophageal fistula</u>





### Radiation enteritis [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)




RT for abdominopelvic <u>malignancy</u> frequently causes proinflammatory injuries to both the small and large bowel.




#### Acute radiation enteritis [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)




Acute radiation enterocolitis is common but often <u>self-limited</u>. Diagnostics and management vary with the severity of the symptoms.




* Onset: hours to weeks after RT

* <u>Incidence</u>: 80% of patients receiving abdominopelvic RT [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)

* Clinical features: <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>, weight loss

* Diagnostics

* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u>

* Consider additional studies based on symptoms:

* <u>Diarrhea</u>: <u>stool diagnostic studies</u>

* Severe abdominal <u>pain</u> or weight loss: CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u>

* Management: <u>supportive care</u>

* <u>Antidiarrheal therapy</u>, e.g., bulking agents, antimotility agents

* <u>Antiemetic therapy</u>

* <u>Treatment of dehydration</u>

* Discontinuation of RT if symptoms are severe





#### Chronic radiation enteritis [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)




* Onset: months to years after RT

* <u>Incidence</u>: 20% of patients receiving abdominopelvic RT [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)

* Clinical features: <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>malabsorption</u>, <u>ulcerations</u>, strictures, <u>fistulas</u>, <u>bowel obstruction</u>, perforation

* Diagnostics

* Imaging, e.g., <u>CT enterography</u>, <u>MRI</u> enterography

* Endoscopy, e.g., <u>EGD</u>, <u>colonoscopy</u>

* Management [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KJWUEm0)

* Medical management

* <u>Malnutrition</u>: <u>specialized nutrition support</u>

* <u>Diarrhea</u>: <u>antidiarrheal therapy</u>

* <u>Small bowel</u> strictures: low residual diet

* <u>Bile acid</u> <u>malabsorption</u>: <u>bile acid sequestrants</u>

* <u>Small bowel bacterial overgrowth</u>: <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* Surgical management  [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)

* Resection of strictures to relieve <u>bowel obstruction</u>

* Repair <u>fistulas</u> and/or perforations.





### Radiation proctitis [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)[[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JHWsr50)




<u>Radiation proctitis</u> is a common complication of <u>pelvic</u> RT for anal, <u>prostate</u>, rectal, cervical, and/or <u>bladder cancer</u>.




#### <u>Risk factors</u> [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)




* Therapy-associated <u>risk factors</u>: total area <u>irradiated</u>, dose of radiation, type of radiation  [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)

* <u>Patient-associated risk factors</u>

* <u>Diabetes</u>, <u>ASCVD</u>, smoking

* <u>Inflammatory bowel disease</u>  [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)

* <u>HIV</u>, <u>immunocompromised individuals</u> (limited evidence) [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)





#### Acute radiation proctitis [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)




* Onset: 0–3 months after RT [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)

* <u>Incidence</u>: up to 75% of patients undergoing <u>pelvic</u> radiation [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)

* Pathophysiology: radiation therapy → acute <u>mucosal</u> inflammation [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHWzs50)

* Clinical features

* Rectal <u>pain</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>tenesmus</u>

* Mucus discharge, minor bleeding

* Diagnostics: endoscopy, possibly with <u>biopsy</u>  [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)

* Endoscopic findings: <u>ulcerations</u>, deep-red <u>edematous</u> <u>mucosa</u>

* <u>Histopathology</u> findings: <u>edema</u>, <u>ulceration</u>, <u>hyperemia</u>, loss of <u>microvillus</u> architecture

* Management: <u>supportive care</u>

* <u>Antidiarrheal therapy</u>

* <u>Treatment of dehydration</u>

* <u>5-ASA</u> and/or <u>steroid</u> enemas

* Discontinuation of RT if symptoms are severe





#### Chronic radiation proctitis [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cYdaLo0)




* Onset: 3 months to years after RT  [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)

* <u>Incidence</u>: 2–20% of patients undergoing <u>pelvic</u> radiation  [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)

* Pathophysiology: radiation causes damage to <u>blood vessels</u> → compromised blood flow to the rectal wall → <u>fibrotic</u> changes and <u>ischemia</u> [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LJWwum0)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)

* Clinical features [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nJW7um0)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)

* Clinical features of <u>acute radiation proctitis</u>

* Bowel strictures, <u>fistulas</u>, obstruction, perforation

* Bleeding (potentially life-threatening)

* Diagnostics: endoscopy with <u>biopsy</u>

* Endoscopic findings [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XSc9yb0)

* Pale <u>mucosa</u>, <u>telangiectasias</u>

* Strictures, <u>ulcerations</u>, <u>fistulas</u>

* Heavy bleeding

* <u>Histopathology</u> findings: damaged small <u>arteries</u> and <u>arterioles</u> showing signs of <u>intimal</u> <u>fibrosis</u>

* Management [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cYdaLo0)

* Medical management

* Topical formaldehyde

* <u>Sucralfate</u> enemas

* Interventional management

* Endoscopic therapy, e.g., argon plasma coagulation

* <u>Hyperbaric oxygen therapy</u>

* <u>Surgery</u>, e.g., resection, <u>colonic</u> diversion
