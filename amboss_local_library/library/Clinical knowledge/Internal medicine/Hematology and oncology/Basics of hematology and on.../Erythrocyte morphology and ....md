## Summary

<u>Erythrocytes</u>, or <u>red blood cells</u> (<u>RBCs</u>), are the most common blood cells. Normal <u>RBCs</u> have a biconcave shape and contain <u>hemoglobin</u> but no <u>nucleus</u> or organelles. <u>Dysmorphic RBCs</u> (e.g., <u>sickle cells</u>, <u>target cells</u>) have an altered form and are often a sign of an underlying condition. <u>Hemoglobin</u> (<u>Hb</u>) is composed of <u>heme</u> and <u>globin</u> subunits and is responsible for transporting oxygen and carbon dioxide throughout the body. <u>Hb</u> can undergo conformational changes (e.g, depending on its oxygenated state), which influence how it binds and releases O<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub>. Deficient <u>Hb</u> (<u>anemia</u>), genetic <u>Hb variants</u> (e.g., <u>HbS</u>, <u>HbC</u>), and certain substances (<u>carbon monoxide</u>, <u>nitrates</u> that form <u>methemoglobin</u>, <u>cyanide</u>) affect the affinity for and ability to transport O<sub>2</sub>, resulting in decreased tissue oxygenation.

## Erythrocyte morphology

### Normal morphology




* No <u>nucleus</u>, <u>cell organelles</u>, or granules

* Biconcave shape

* Maintained by <u>spectrin</u>, a <u>cytoskeletal</u> protein

* Leads to a large surface area to volume <u>ratio</u>, enabling rapid <u>gas exchange</u>

* Provides great flexibility, allowing for easy movement through narrow vessels and <u>capillaries</u>

* Contain <u>hemoglobin</u>, which leads to acidophilic <u>cytoplasm</u>

## Metabolism of erythrocytes

The lifespan of <u>RBCs</u> is about 120 days. After this time, <u>macrophages</u> in the <u>reticuloendothelial system</u> of the bone and the <u>spleen</u> <u>phagocytose</u> <u>RBCs</u>. They are then broken down and their parts recycled.




### <u>Globin</u> metabolism




<u>Globin</u> chains are released and converted into <u>amino acids</u>.




### Hemoglobin metabolism




Process: <u>heme</u> (red) → biliverdin (green pigment) → <u>bilirubin</u> (yellow pigment)



1. * <u>Heme</u> is converted to <u>biliverdin</u> by heme oxygenase.

* Requires <u>NADPH</u> + H<sup>+</sup>

* <u>Carbon monoxide</u> (CO) is released.

* <u>Iron</u> is released, oxidized, and taken up by <u>transferrin</u> to be recycled.
2. * <u>Biliverdin</u> is converted to <u>bilirubin</u> by biliverdin reductase (requires <u>NADPH</u> + H<sup>+</sup>)

### Bilirubin metabolism



1. * Unconjugated bilirubin (insoluble in water) is released into the blood by <u>macrophages</u> → binds to <u>albumin</u> and reaches the <u>liver</u>
2. * <u>Unconjugated bilirubin</u> is converted into <u>bilirubin</u> via the enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase in the <u>liver</u>.

* <u>Bilirubin</u> is conjugated with glucuronic acid → <u>bilirubin</u> diglucuronide = conjugated bilirubin (<u>water soluble</u>)

* Most <u>conjugated bilirubin</u> is excreted into the <u>GI tract</u> via <u>bile</u>, while some is released into the blood.
3. * <u>Conjugated bilirubin</u> excreted in <u>bile</u> is broken down by GI bacteria into urobilinogen

* Most <u>urobilinogen</u> is converted to stercobilin → excreted in feces (brown color).

* Part of <u>urobilinogen</u> oxidates to urobilin → excreted in the urine (yellow color).

> Indirect (unconjugated) <u>bilirubin</u> is insoluble in water.




### 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shunt




* 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate mutase is vital for the formation of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (intermediate in <u>glycolysis</u>) → 2,3-BPG
* <u>2,3-BPG mutase</u> is unique to <u>erythrocytes</u> and <u>placental</u> cells.

* <u>2,3-BPG</u> binds to <u>hemoglobin</u> → conformational change → release of oxygen into tissue

* <u>2,3-BPG</u> binds with greater affinity to <u>deoxygenated hemoglobin</u> than oxygenated <u>hemoglobin</u>.





### Energy production




* Mature <u>RBCs</u> are unable to generate energy (<u>ATP</u>) via the <u>Krebs cycle</u> because they lack <u>mitochondria</u>.

* <u>Glucose</u> is the main source of energy for <u>RBCs</u>.

* Biochemical energy-producing pathways available in <u>RBCs</u>

* <u>Glycolysis</u> (catabolizes 90% of the available <u>glucose</u>) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/riYfGK)

* <u>Pentose-phosphate pathway</u>

* Glutathione reduction (a reaction that converts <u>oxidized glutathione</u> to its reduced form)

## Hemoglobin synthesis

### Overview




<u>Hb</u> is a circulating globular protein composed of a heme moiety with a central <u>iron</u> ion and four subunits of <u>globin</u>.




* The main function of <u>Hb</u> is to take up O<sub>2</sub> from the <u>lungs</u> and deliver it to tissues.

* It can undergo conformational changes (e.g., depending on its state of oxygenation), which influence how it binds and releases O<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub>.

* Deficient or defective <u>Hb</u> can ultimately affect the transport of O<sub>2</sub> (see “<u>Hemoglobin variants</u>” below for details).

* For more information about disorders of <u>Hb</u>, see “<u>Anemia</u>.”





### Heme synthesis




* Heme is synthesized from protoporphyrin, a <u>porphyrin</u>.

* Porphyrins: a group of intermediates in the <u>heme synthesis</u> pathway that is composed of four subunit rings 

* Includes <u>porphobilinogen</u> and <u>uroporphyrinogen</u>

* Binding of a central ferrous <u>iron</u> (Fe<sup>2+</sup>) forms a <u>porphyrin</u> complex (i.e., <u>heme</u>).

* The steps of <u>heme synthesis</u> occur both in the <u>cytoplasm</u> and the <u>mitochondria</u> (the first and final steps occur in <u>mitochondria</u>).

### Globin




* <u>Globin</u> is an integral part of the <u>Hb</u> molecule.

* Tetramer consisting of four individual <u>polypeptide</u> subunits that bind <u>heme</u>; composed of <u>amino acids</u> that fold to form 8 alpha helices

* There are 6 types of <u>globin</u> chains.

* β, δ, γ, ε are encoded on <u>chromosome</u> 11.

* α and ζ are encoded on <u>chromosome</u> 16.

* The combination of subunits in the <u>Hb</u> molecule determines the type of <u>Hb</u> (e.g., embryonic, fetal, <u>newborn</u>, or adult <u>Hb</u>); each subunit is able to bind one O<sub>2</sub> molecule

* Mutations in <u>genes</u> encoding <u>globin</u> results in <u>Hb variants</u>.

## Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport

### Overview




* O<sub>2</sub>, CO<sub>2</sub>, and protons all bind <u>Hb</u> and influence one another's affinity to <u>Hb</u>, which is important for <u>gas exchange</u>.

* <u>Hb</u> releases CO<sub>2</sub> more easily with increasing pO<sub>2</sub> (<u>Haldane effect</u>).

* <u>Hb</u> binds CO<sub>2</sub> more easily if the pO<sub>2</sub> is low.

* <u>Hb</u> releases O<sub>2</sub> easier with increasing H<sup>+</sup>(<u>Bohr effect</u>).

* CO<sub>2</sub> is mainly carried in three forms in the body:

* Plasma <u>bicarbonate</u>, <u>HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup></u> (70%)

* Bound to <u>Hb</u> (carbamino-<u>Hb</u>, <u>HbCO<sub>2</sub></u>) at the N-terminus of <u>globin</u> (does not compete with O<sub>2</sub> at <u>heme</u>)

* Dissolved in blood (5%)





### Oxygenation and deoxygenation of <u>Hb</u>




* Oxyhemoglobin

* <u>Hemoglobin</u> with oxygen bound to its <u>heme</u> component (oxygenated) → bright red blood

* Oxygenated <u>hemoglobin</u> has approximately 300 times higher affinity for oxygen compared to <u>deoxygenated hemoglobin</u>

* Exhibits <u>positive cooperativity</u> and positive <u>allostery</u>

* Deoxyhemoglobin

* <u>Hemoglobin</u> with no oxygen bound to its <u>heme</u> component (deoxygenated) → dark red blood

* Blue to purple appearance of tissue during <u>hypoxia</u> → <u>cyanosis</u>

* <u>Deoxygenated hemoglobin</u> has a low affinity for oxygen → release of oxygen is promoted





### Bicarbonate buffer system




* <u>RBCs</u> carry carbonic anhydrase, which converts <u>HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup></u> and H<sup>+</sup> to H<sub>2</sub>O and CO<sub>2</sub> in the following steps: HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> + H<sup>+</sup>⇄ H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> ⇄ H<sub>2</sub>O + CO<sub>2</sub>

* Ultimately, excess H<sup>+</sup> during acidic states is eliminated through conversion to CO<sub>2</sub>, which can be exhaled.

* During basic states, the <u>bicarbonate buffer system</u> can reverse so that CO<sub>2</sub> is converted to HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> + H<sup>+</sup>.
* Chloride shift: Excess intracellular HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> produced this way is released into the plasma in exchange for <u>Cl<sup>-</sup></u>.

* This phenomenon makes <u>HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup></u> the most important buffer in the body.

* For more details on the buffering mechanisms of the body, see “Compensation.”





### Bohr effect




* The O<sub>2</sub> affinity of <u>Hb</u> is inversely proportional to the CO<sub>2</sub> content and H<sup>+</sup> concentration of blood.

* High CO<sub>2</sub> and H<sup>+</sup> concentrations (from tissue metabolism) cause decreased affinity for O<sub>2</sub> → O<sub>2</sub> that is bound to <u>Hb</u> is released to tissue (the <u>O<sub>2</sub>-Hb dissociation curve</u> is shifted to the right).

* <u>HbO<sub>2</sub></u> + H<sup>+</sup> ⇄ H<sup>+</sup><u>Hb</u> + O<sub>2</sub>

* <u>HbO<sub>2</sub></u> + CO<sub>2</sub> ⇄ <u>Hb</u>-COO<sup>-</sup> + H<sup>+</sup> + O<sub>2</sub>





### Haldane effect




* The CO<sub>2</sub> affinity of <u>Hb</u> is inversely proportional to the oxygenation of <u>Hb</u>.

* When <u>Hb</u> is deoxygenated (typically in peripheral tissue), uptake of CO<sub>2</sub> is facilitated.

* When <u>Hb</u> is oxygenated (in high pO<sub>2</sub>, for example, in the <u>lungs</u>):

* Oxygenated <u>Hb</u> has a decreased affinity for CO<sub>2</sub>→ CO<sub>2</sub> that is bound to <u>Hb</u> is released in the <u>pulmonary arteries</u> to diffuse into the <u>alveoli</u> (the <u>O2-Hb dissociation curve</u> is shifted to the left).

* <u>Hb</u> releases bound H<sup>+</sup>;  → ↑ H<sup>+</sup> shifts equilibrium to CO<sub>2</sub> production (see equation above) → CO<sub>2</sub> is exhaled in <u>lungs</u>

## Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve

### Overview




* The <u>O<sub>2</sub>-Hb dissociation curve</u> shows the <u>arterial partial pressure of O<sub>2</sub></u> (<u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>) in relation to the percentage saturation of <u>Hb</u>, i.e., the binding affinity of <u>Hb</u> for O<sub>2</sub>.

* Relationship between saturation and <u>partial pressure</u> of O<sub>2</sub> is not linear

* Reflected in the sigmoidal shape (S-shape) of the curve, which begins flat, then rises steeply before plateauing.

* Due to <u>positive cooperativity</u> (the phenomenon by which, e.g., binding of one O<sub>2</sub> molecule to a single <u>heme</u> increases the O<sub>2</sub> affinity of <u>hemoglobin</u>, facilitating the binding of subsequent O<sub>2</sub> molecules).

* <u>Myoglobin</u> is composed of a single <u>polypeptide</u> chain that is associated with a monomeric <u>heme</u> molecule.

* Therefore, it cannot show <u>positive cooperativity</u> and its curve is not sigmoidal.

* The binding affinity of <u>Hb</u> is influenced by external factors that may lead to a left or right shift of the <u>O<sub>2</sub> dissociation curve</u> (ODC).

### Shift to the right of the oxygen dissociation curve




* ↓ <u>Hb</u> affinity for O<sub>2</sub> → ↑ O<sub>2</sub> dissociation from <u>Hb</u> → ↑ tissue oxygenation → ↑ <u>P<sub>50</sub></u> (the <u>partial pressure</u> of oxygen needed to maintain 50% of <u>Hb</u> saturated)

* Causes of shift to the right include:

* ↑ <u>Partial pressure</u> of carbon dioxide (↑ <u>PCO<sub>2</sub></u>)

* ↑ Body temperature (e.g., <u>fever</u>)

* ↑ H<sup>+</sup> (↓ <u>pH</u>)

* ↑ <u>2,3-BPG</u> (generated by <u>2,3-BPG mutase</u> during <u>erythrocyte</u> <u>glycolysis</u>)

* ↑ Exercise

* ↑ Altitude





### Shift to the left of the oxygen dissociation curve




* ↑ <u>Hb</u> affinity for O<sub>2</sub> → ↓ O<sub>2</sub> dissociation from <u>Hb</u> → ↓ tissue oxygenation → ↑ <u>erythropoietin</u> synthesis (due to renal <u>hypoxia</u>) → compensatory <u>erythrocytosis</u>

* Causes of <u>shift to the left</u> include:

* ↓ <u>Partial pressure</u> of carbon dioxide (<u>PCO<sub>2</sub></u>)

* ↓ Body temperature

* ↓ H<sup>+</sup> (↑ <u>pH</u>)

* ↓ <u>2,3-BPG</u> (e.g., due to mutations in the <u>BPGM</u> <u>gene</u> on <u>chromosome</u> 7) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hl1c9S0)

* ↑ CO

* ↑ <u>Methemoglobin</u>

* ↑ <u>Fetal hemoglobin</u> (<u>HbF</u>)

#### Arterial oxygen content (<u>CaO<sub>2</sub></u>)




* Definition: the sum of oxygen bound to <u>hemoglobin</u> and dissolved in plasma within arterial blood

* Formula: <u>arterial oxygen content</u> (<u>CaO<sub>2</sub></u>, mL of oxygen per 100 mL of blood) = (1.34 x <u>Hb</u> x <u>SaO<sub>2</sub></u>) + (0.003 x <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>)

* <u>Hb</u> (g/dL) = <u>hemoglobin concentration</u>

* <u>SaO<sub>2</sub></u> (%) = <u>arterial oxygen saturation</u> in <u>hemoglobin</u>

* <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u> (mm Hg) = <u>arterial oxygen saturation</u> in plasma (<u>partial pressure</u> of oxygen)

* 1.34 (mL) = maximum oxygen binding capacity of 1 g of <u>hemoglobin</u>. Normal blood <u>Hb</u> concentration is approximately 15 g/dL. The oxygen-binding capacity of blood is, therefore: 15 g/dL x 1.34 mL ≈ 20 mL of oxygen per dL of blood.

* 0.003 = solubility coefficient of oxygen in plasma

* <u>Arterial oxygen content</u> is directly proportional to the <u>Hb</u>, <u>SaO<sub>2</sub></u>, and <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>.





#### Oxygen delivery (<u>DO<sub>2</sub></u>)




* Definition: the rate at which oxygen is transferred from the <u>lungs</u> to the peripheral circulation

* Formula: <u>oxygen delivery</u> (<u>DO<sub>2</sub></u>, mL O<sub>2</sub>/min) = <u>cardiac output</u> (CO) x arterial oxygen content (<u>CaO<sub>2</sub></u>)

* <u>Oxygen delivery</u> to an organ = organ blood flow rate x arterial oxygen content (<u>CaO<sub>2</sub></u>). For example, renal <u>oxygen delivery</u> (renal <u>DO<sub>2</sub></u>) = <u>renal blood flow</u> rate (<u>RBF</u>) x arterial oxygen content (<u>CaO<sub>2</sub></u>).

* In an adult with a <u>cardiac output</u> of 5 L/min, the <u>oxygen delivery</u> is ∼ 1000 mL/min.

* <u>Oxygen delivery</u> is directly proportional to <u>cardiac output</u> and arterial oxygen concentration.





### <u>Carbon monoxide poisoning</u>




* Formation of carboxyhemoglobin: <u>hemoglobin</u> that is removed from oxygen transportation because its oxygen binding site is occupied by <u>carbon monoxide</u>

* See “<u>Carbon monoxide intoxication</u>.”





### <u>Methemoglobinemia</u>




* <u>Methemoglobin</u> has an <u>iron</u> molecule in the oxidized state (Fe3+) rather than the reduced state (Fe2+), which renders it unable to transport oxygen to the tissue.

* See “<u>Methemoglobinemia</u>.”

> To remember that ferrous <u>iron</u> (Fe2+) is the reduced form of <u>iron</u>, think: “Joe reduced 2 ferocious animals from his <u>iron</u> cage.”
