## Summary

Oncology is the science of tumors. This article explains basic concepts relevant to the development, progression, spread, and diagnosis of cancer. Histological analysis helps to determine <u>tumor</u> type and grade, whereas the <u>TNM classification</u> system is used to assess staging. Because it is standardized and used internationally, the <u>TNM classification</u> helps unify oncological research and therapy protocols. <u>Metastasis</u> occurs via different pathways mainly by hematogenous or lymphatic spreading.




<u>Principles of cancer care</u>, <u>paraneoplastic syndromes</u>, and <u>tumor markers</u> are covered in detail in their respective articles.

| --- | --- |
| Term | Definition |

| Tumor | <br><br>* Abnormal growth of cells or <u>edema</u> that leads to swelling of tissue<br><br>* <u>Tumor</u> does not necessarily mean <u>neoplasia</u>.<br><br><br> |
| Adenoma | <br>* A <u>benign tumor</u> that arises from glandular <u>epithelial</u> cells.<br><br> |
| Carcinoma | <br>* Malignant cells that arise from <u>epithelium</u> (e.g., <u>squamous cell carcinoma</u>, <u>basal cell carcinoma</u>)<br><br> |
| Sarcoma | <br>* Malignant cells that arise from <u>mesenchyme</u> (e.g., <u>sarcoma botryoides</u>, <u>liposarcoma</u>, <u>leiomyosarcoma</u>)<br><br> |
| Hamartoma | <br><br>* A benign mass composed of mature cells that are native to the tissue of origin but have abnormal tissue organization (e.g., <u>Peutz-Jeghers</u> syndrome, <u>pulmonary hamartoma</u>, <u>iris hamartoma</u>)<br><br>* Low potential to undergo malignant transformation<br><br><br> |
| Choristoma | <br>* Presence of normal tissue at a site where this type of tissue is not normally found (e.g., the presence of gastric tissue in the <u>ileum</u>, as is sometimes seen in <u>Meckel diverticulum</u>)<br><br> |
| <br>Dysplasia  <br> | <br><br>* Presence of <u>pleomorphism</u> (cells of different shape and size)<br><br>* Loss of cell orientation<br><br>* Increased <u>nuclear:cytoplasmic ratio</u><br><br>* No invasion of the <u>basement membrane</u><br><br><br> |
| <br>Carcinoma in situ (<u>CIS</u>)  <br> | <br><br>* Irreversible severe <u>dysplasia</u><br><br>* Involves the entire thickness of the <u>epithelium</u><br><br>* No invasion of the <u>basement membrane</u><br><br><br> |
| <br>Invasive carcinoma  <br> | <br><br>* Invasion of the <u>basement membrane</u><br><br>* Factors that promote invasion<br><br>* <u>Metalloproteases</u> (e.g., <u>hydrolases</u>, <u>collagenases</u>)<br><br>* Inactivation of <u>E-cadherins</u> → disruption of intercellular adhesions between neighboring cells<br><br><br> |
| <br><u>Metastasis</u>  <br> | <br>* Spread of malignant cells to distant organs, tissues (e.g., <u>colorectal cancer</u> spreading to the <u>liver</u>)<br><br>* Lymphatic spread<br><br>* Hematogenous spread<br><br> |

### Benign and <u>malignant tumors</u>




| Overview of benign and <u>malignant tumors</u> |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | Benign tumor | Malignant tumor |
| Differentiation(grading) | <br>* Well-differentiated (low grade)<br><br> | <br>* Poorly differentiated (high grade)<br><br> |
| Growth | <br><br>* Slow growth with a low rate of cell division (low <u>mitotic</u> activity)<br><br>* Mass effect with compression of non-<u>tumor</u> tissue<br><br><br> | <br><br>* Unpredictable growth, high rate of cell division (high <u>mitotic</u> activity)<br><br>* Suppressed cellular <u>apoptosis</u><br><br>* Locally destructive and invasive<br><br><br> |
| Macroscopy | <br><br>* <u>Necrosis</u> is rare.<br><br>* Well-demarcated from surrounding tissue<br><br><br> | <br><br>* Possible <u>necrosis</u> (e.g., <u>squamous cell carcinoma of the lung</u>)<br><br>* Usually no <u>tumor</u> capsule<br><br>* Infiltration (invasion) of surrounding tissue<br><br>* Bleeding<br><br><br> |
| Microscopy | <br>* Almost no irregularities of cellular structures<br><br> | <br>* Detection and confirmation of cellular atypia<br><br>* <u>Nuclei</u>: Enlargement , <u>polymorphism</u> , <u>polychromasia</u> , increased cell count and/or enlarged nucleoli are all signs of increased activity of <u>nuclei</u>.<br><br>* Numerous mitotic figures (increased <u>proliferation</u> of malignant cells with typical and atypical <u>mitoses</u>)<br><br> |
| <u>Metastasis</u> and relapse | <br><br>* No <u>metastases</u><br><br>* Relapses are rare.<br><br><br> | <br>* <u>Metastases</u> and relapses are common.<br><br> |

## Carcinogenesis

### Overview




* Definition: a multistep process by which normal cells develop and accumulate genetic mutations (inherited or acquired), resulting in a monoclonal expansion of mutated cells that can progress to the development of <u>neoplasia</u>

* Process
1. * Initiation: <u>DNA damage</u>
2. * Promotion: <u>DNA damage</u> passed on
3. * Latency: the time between promotion and progression
4. * Progression: <u>proliferation</u> of the <u>neoplastic</u> cell line with the acquired <u>DNA damage</u>, leading to malignant transformation

### Properties of malignant cells




* Sustained proliferative signaling: due to mutation of <u>genes</u> regulating cell division and growth

* (Proto‑)<u>oncogene</u>: See “<u>Proto-oncogene</u>” below.

* Growth factor <u>receptors</u> (e.g., <u>ERBB2</u>, <u>EGFR</u>)

* Signaling molecules (e.g., <u>Ras</u>, <u>B-Raf</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mYbVpH)

* <u>Transcription factors</u> (e.g., <u>MYC</u>, <u>TP53</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5YbipH)

* <u>Cell cycle</u> regulators (e.g., <u>cyclins</u>, <u>CDKs</u>)

* Evade growth suppressors [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MYbMpH)

* <u>Tumor suppressor genes</u>: See “<u>Tumor suppressor genes</u>” below.

* Loss of contact inhibition

* Mutated or absent <u>E-cadherin</u> → loss of growth inhibitory signals and contact inhibition of <u>proliferation</u> → excessive cell <u>proliferation</u>

* Due to mutations in <u>genes</u> that regulate contact inhibition (e.g., <u>NF2</u> <u>gene</u>)

* <u>Genome instability</u> and mutations

* Mutations: See “<u>Gene mutations</u>” and “<u>Chromosomal translocation</u>” in the “<u>Basics of human genetics</u>” article.

* <u>Gene amplification</u>: increased <u>gene expression</u> (e.g., overexpression of <u>ERBB2</u> <u>gene</u> in several forms of <u>breast cancer</u>)

* Resist <u>cell death</u> 

* Occurs because of mutations in <u>apoptosis</u> regulatory <u>genes</u>

* Loss of regulatory <u>genes</u> (e.g., <u>Bax</u>, <u>TP53</u>, <u>BCL-2</u>) leads to cell immortality and increased <u>proliferation</u>.

* Enable replicative immortality: <u>telomerase</u> reactivation → ↑ length of <u>telomeres</u> → ↑ number of possible cell replication cycles

* Deregulate cellular energetics: a shift in cellular metabolism from <u>glycolysis</u> and <u>mitochondrial</u> <u>oxidative phosphorylation</u> to <u>glycolysis</u> and preferential <u>lactate</u> generation, regardless of the oxygen supply (Warburg effect)

* In differentiated, non-malignant cells, <u>glycolysis</u> converts <u>glucose</u> to <u>pyruvate</u>, which then undergoes <u>mitochondrial</u> <u>oxidative phosphorylation</u> under aerobic conditions, or <u>anaerobic glycolysis</u> to generate <u>lactate</u> under anaerobic conditions.

* In malignant cells, the so-called <u>Warburg effect</u> occurs, in which <u>pyruvate</u> is preferentially converted to <u>lactate</u>, regardless of the cellular oxygen supply. This mechanism supplies rapidly dividing malignant cells with the necessary carbon to synthesize cellular structures. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nYb7pH)

* Induce <u>angiogenesis</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MYbMpH)

* <u>Angiogenesis</u> helps maintain an adequate supply of oxygen and <u>nutrients</u> to the <u>neoplastic</u> cells.

* Regulated by angiogenic (e.g., <u>VEGF</u>, FGF) and inhibitory factors that are produced by <u>neoplastic</u> or supporting cells

* Occurs as:

* Vasculogenesis: de novo formation of <u>blood vessels</u> from <u>bone marrow</u>-derived <u>endothelial</u> cells

* Neoangiogenesis: formation of <u>blood vessels</u> from existing <u>blood vessels</u> and <u>capillaries</u>

* Newly formed <u>blood vessels</u> may be dilated or leaky.

* Activate invasion and <u>metastasis</u>: See “<u>Metastasis</u>” below.

* Avoid immune detection: See “<u>Defense mechanisms of malignant cells</u>” below.

* <u>Tumor</u>-promoted inflammation

### Defense mechanisms of malignant cells [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LYbwpH)




* <u>Immune response</u> modification

* ↓ <u>MHC class I</u> expression on malignant cells → inability of <u>cytotoxic T-cells</u> to recognize and mount an <u>immune response</u> against these cells

* Secretion of <u>immunosuppressive</u> molecules (e.g., <u>TGF-β</u>) → enhanced <u>immune tolerance</u> of malignant cells

* Enhanced regulatory <u>T-cell</u> activity → limited <u>immune response</u>

* <u>Immune checkpoints</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EmX8hA)

* Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (<u>CTLA-4</u>)

* Upregulation of <u>CTLA-4</u> → promotion of binding to <u>B7 protein</u> (surface <u>receptor</u> on <u>antigen-presenting cells</u>, also called <u>CD80</u> or <u>CD86</u>) → <u>T-cell</u> inactivation and simultaneous reduction of costimulation by binding of <u>CD28</u> to <u>B7</u>

* <u>CTLA-4</u> is inhibited by anti-<u>CTLA-4</u> <u>antibody</u> <u>ipilimumab</u>.

* Programmed <u>death</u>-<u>ligand</u> system

* Expression of <u>programmed death-ligand 1</u> (<u>PD-L1</u>) and PD-L2 on <u>tumor</u> cells surface or environment → binding to programmed cell death protein 1 (<u>PD-1</u>) on <u>T-cells</u> → <u>T-cell</u> dysfunction

* <u>PD-L1</u> and <u>PD-L2</u> are inhibited by <u>cancer immunotherapy</u> agents <u>atezolizumab</u>, <u>durvalumab</u>, and <u>avelumab</u>.

* <u>PD-1</u> is inhibited by <u>cancer immunotherapy</u> agents <u>nivolumab</u> and <u>pembrolizumab</u>.

* Development of resistance to <u>chemotherapeutic agents</u>

* Expression of multidrug resistance protein 1 (<u>MDR1</u>; also called <u>P-glycoprotein</u>). [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pYbLJH)

* P-glycoproteins are transmembrane <u>ATP</u>-dependent efflux pump <u>proteins</u> that decrease intracellular concentrations of <u>chemotherapeutics</u>.

* Expressed in <u>adrenocortical carcinoma</u> as well as cancers of the <u>colon</u>, <u>liver</u>, <u>pancreas</u>, <u>kidney</u>, <u>ovary</u>, and <u>breast</u>

### Etiologic field cancerization [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vBWAcL0)




* Definition: a collection of factors (e.g., lifestyle, environmental, microbial, genetic) and their interactions that affect tissues and potentially predispose individuals to the development of disease

* Pathophysiology: <u>carcinogenic</u> exposure → transformation of normal cells → wide area of premalignant cells at the primary site and adjacent areas

* Examples

* Interactions between the <u>immune system</u>, microbiome, and diet influence the development of <u>ulcerative colitis</u> and <u>colorectal cancer</u>.

* Prolonged exposure to <u>tobacco smoke</u>, <u>alcohol</u> consumption, and persistent <u>HPV infection</u> influence the development of <u>head and neck cancer</u>.

* Chronic sun exposure, UV radiation, and genetic predisposition influence the development of <u>skin</u> cancer.

* Exposure to industrial <u>carcinogens</u>, <u>chronic inflammation</u>, and genetic susceptibility influence the development of <u>bladder cancer</u>.

## Oncogene

* Oncogene: the product of a <u>gain-of-function mutation</u> in a <u>proto-oncogene</u> which leads to overexpression of signaling <u>proteins</u> and growth factors, and thus, uncontrolled cellular <u>proliferation</u> (e.g., <u>dysplasia</u>, <u>neoplasia</u>)

* Only one <u>allele</u> of the <u>proto-oncogene</u> requires damage to form an <u>oncogene</u>.

* Often present in cancer cells

* Proto-oncogene: <u>Genes</u> that encode <u>proteins</u> that are important in normal cell division and <u>cell differentiation</u>. Examples include:

* Protein <u>kinases</u>, e.g., protein kinase B (<u>PKB</u>)

* <u>PKB</u> activates and inhibits various substrates, regulates <u>apoptosis</u>, activates <u>translation</u> and, indirectly, cell division.

* <u>PKB</u> is activated by <u>phosphatidylinositide 3-kinase</u> (PI3K).

* <u>Ligand</u>-directed <u>transcription factors</u> (intracellular <u>hormone</u> <u>receptors</u>)

* <u>GTP-binding proteins</u>

* <u>G protein-coupled receptors</u>

* Small <u>G-proteins</u> such as <u>Ras</u>

* <u>Tyrosine kinase receptors</u>

* Growth factors and <u>cytokines</u>

## Grading and staging

### Tumor grading




* Definition: the process of classifying tumors based on their histological appearance (degree of cell differentiation)

* Indicators of poor differentiation

* High-<u>proliferation</u> index

* Presence of nucleoli

* <u>Giant cells</u> with multiple <u>nuclei</u>

* Hyperchromasia and heterogeneous <u>chromatin</u> distribution

* Abnormal shape of cell or <u>nucleus</u> (nuclear atypia, enlarged nucleoli)

* Different cell polarity

* Different orientation of <u>nuclei</u> belonging to the same group of cells

* Increase in <u>mitotic figures</u>

* <u>Anaplasia</u>: loss of morphological features of malignant cells so that resemblance to normal cells of a particular tissue where <u>tumor</u> cell originated from is lost





#### Grading systems



* AJCC grading system

* Most commonly used grading system for nonhematological malignancies

* Can be applied to a wide range of tumors

* Usually, higher-grade tumors are more aggressive than lower-grade tumors.




| Grading | Differentiation of malignant tissue |
| --- | --- |
| G1 | <br>Well differentiated (low grade) <br> |
| G2 | Moderately differentiated (intermediate grade) |
| G3 | Poorly differentiated (high grade) |
| G4 | <br>Undifferentiated/<u>anaplastic</u> (high grade) <br> |
| G<sub>X</sub> | Differentiation cannot be assessed. |




* Cancer-specific grading systems

* <u>Gleason score</u> for <u>prostate cancer</u>

* Nottingham grading system for <u>breast cancer</u>

### Tumor staging [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qYbCJH)




* Definition: a method of determining and classifying a <u>tumor</u> according to its spread throughout the body

* Prognosis: The stage of the <u>tumor</u> is typically more important than the grade in determining the prognosis.





> [!NOTE]
> Spread determines Stage, and Stage determines Survival more than grade.




#### <u>TNM classification</u>




* T stage: size or direct extent of the primary <u>tumor</u>  

* Tcis: <u>carcinoma in situ</u> (no <u>basement membrane</u> penetration and no infiltration of <u>submucosa</u>)

* T1–4 based on the size and/or extent of the primary <u>tumor</u> (T4 refers to the infiltration of neighboring organs)

* N stage: involvement of regional <u>lymph</u> nodes

* N0: no <u>lymph node</u> involvement

* N1–3 based on type of <u>tumor</u> and <u>lymph node</u> involvement

* M stage: presence of distant <u>metastasis</u>

* M0: no distant <u>metastasis</u>

* M1: present distant <u>metastasis</u>

* Mx: unknown if distant <u>metastasis</u> present or not

* By adding a "C" to any category, it is possible to express the certainty of the diagnosis:

* C1: routine procedure (<u>clinical examination</u>, <u>x-ray</u>)

* C2: special procedure (e.g. <u>ERCP</u>, CT)

* C3: based on <u>biopsy</u>, <u>cytology</u> or surgical exploration

* C4: based on <u>surgery</u> and additional histopathological workup

* C5: based on <u>autopsy</u> and histopathological workup

* By adding a prefix to <u>TNM</u> it is possible to indicate additional diagnostic or clinical information:

* cTNM: staging based on clinical criteria

* pTNM: histopathological staging





> [!TIP]
> T, N, and M have independent prognostic values. N and M are typically the most important determinants of prognosis.

## Metastasis

### Definition



* Metastasis: the spread of malignant cells to distant organs, tissues (e.g., <u>colorectal cancer</u> spreads to the <u>liver</u>)




### Types of <u>metastasis</u> [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p30Ljf)




* Lymphatic metastasis

* Dissemination of malignant cells via lymphatic vessels and <u>lymph nodes</u>

* Most common route of <u>metastasis</u> for most <u>carcinomas</u>

* Hematogenous metastasis

* Dissemination of malignant cells via <u>blood vessels</u>

* Venous dissemination is more common than arterial dissemination because thin <u>vein</u> walls facilitate invasion.

* Most common route of <u>metastasis</u> for most <u>sarcomas</u>

* Seeding (oncology): spillage of malignant cells to neighboring structures → implants at a site adjacent to the primary <u>tumor</u> and subsequent growth (e.g., after a <u>biopsy</u> of cancer the cancer cells spill and implant along the <u>biopsy</u> canal)

* Transcoelomic metastasis: spread of malignant cells into body cavities by penetration of surfaces such as the <u>pleura</u>, <u>pericardium</u>, and <u>peritoneum</u> (e.g., <u>ovarian cancer</u> spread to the <u>liver</u> via the <u>peritoneal cavity</u>)

* Canalicular metastasis: spread of malignant cells via canalicular system (e.g. <u>bile</u> ducts, <u>lactiferous ducts</u>, <u>urinary tract</u>, and the <u>subarachnoid space</u>)

* Cavitating pulmonary metastasis [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/m5XVkA)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/55XikA)

* Spread of malignant cells that form cavitary lesions in the <u>lungs</u> (e.g., gastrointestinal <u>adenocarcinomas</u>, <u>renal cell carcinoma</u>)

* Cavitary lesions are most commonly caused by <u>squamous cell carcinoma</u> (e.g., <u>lung</u>, head, and neck cancer)

* Cystic pulmonary metastasis: spread of malignant cells that form cystic lesions (contain a thin wall) in the <u>lungs</u> (e.g., <u>colorectal cancer</u>, <u>soft tissue sarcomas</u>, <u>transitional cell carcinoma</u>) [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/M5XMkA)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/n5X7kA)





> [!NOTE]
> “Four Carcinomas Route Hematogenously”: Follicular <u>thyroid carcinoma</u>, Choriocarcinoma, Renal cell <u>carcinoma</u>, and Hepatocellular <u>carcinoma</u> spread via the blood, compared to most <u>carcinomas</u> which spread lymphatically.

### Mechanisms of <u>metastasis</u>




* Complex genetic changes are responsible for the selection of <u>tumor</u> subclones that are capable of <u>metastasis</u>.

* All <u>metastases</u> can be understood as arising from a two-part process: invasion of local extracellular tissue and dissemination and <u>colonization</u>.

* Invasion of extracellular tissue: loss of adhesion to the <u>basement membrane</u> → invasion through <u>basement membrane</u> → passage through extracellular tissue

* Loss of <u>E-cadherin</u> expression is associated with <u>tumor</u> <u>metastatic</u> potential.

* Overproduction of <u>proteases</u> such as <u>collagenase</u> and <u>matrix metalloproteinases</u> degrade the <u>basement membrane</u> and <u>interstitial</u> matrix. <u>Neoplastic</u> cells encounter various <u>chemotactic</u> and angiogenic factors in the newly exposed <u>extracellular matrix</u>.

* <u>Autocrine signaling</u> via <u>tumor</u>-produced <u>cytokines</u> and <u>paracrine signaling</u> by cleaved matrix components and extracellular growth factors stimulate <u>tumor</u> cell locomotion towards vasculature or <u>lymphatics</u>.

* Dissemination and <u>colonization</u>: encountering vascular or lymphatic routes → evasion of host defenses → implantation with distant tissue

* Host defenses destroy the majority of circulating cancer cells. Mechanisms to avoid this include <u>tumor</u> cell aggregation, formation of <u>platelet</u>-<u>tumor</u> complexes, and binding of active <u>coagulation factors</u> to form malignant <u>emboli</u>.

* Disruption of cellular adhesion molecules (<u>laminins</u>, <u>cadherins</u>) enables extravasation at distant tissues.
