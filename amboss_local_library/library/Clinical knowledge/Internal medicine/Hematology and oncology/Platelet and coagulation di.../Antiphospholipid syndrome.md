# Antiphospholipid syndrome

## Summary

Antiphospholipid syndrome (<u>APS</u>) is an autoimmune disease associated with increased risk of <u>thrombosis</u> due to the presence of procoagulatory <u>antibodies</u>. <u>APS</u> can manifest in isolation or alongside other autoimmune diseases such as <u>systemic lupus erythematosus</u> (<u>SLE</u>). Typical clinical manifestations include recurring venous, arterial, and/or <u>microcirculation</u> <u>thrombotic events</u> (e.g., <u>DVTs</u>, <u>stroke</u>, <u>TIAs</u>) and obstetrical complications (e.g., recurrent <u>miscarriages</u>, premature births). <u>APS</u> should be suspected in patients with a history of <u>thrombosis</u> or <u>miscarriage</u>; diagnosis is confirmed by the detection of serum <u>antiphospholipid antibodies</u> (e.g., <u>lupus anticoagulant</u>, <u>anticardiolipin antibodies</u>). Management of acute <u>thrombotic events</u> varies widely depending on the clinical manifestation (e.g., <u>stroke</u>, <u>DVT</u>). Long-term <u>management of APS</u> (e.g., systemic anticoagulation with <u>warfarin</u> or <u>heparin</u>) is determined by the patient's risk of future <u>thrombotic</u> complications. Additional pharmacotherapy may be indicated for certain manifestations. <u>Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome</u> (<u>CAPS</u>) is a rare but severe form of <u>APS</u> that requires immediate treatment.

## Etiology

* Primary

* <u>Idiopathic</u>

* Associated with <u>genetic marker</u> <u>HLA</u>-DR7

* Secondary  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4aKkk)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)

* <u>Systemic lupus erythematosus</u> (most common cause of secondary <u>APS</u>)

* <u>Rheumatoid arthritis</u>

* <u>Neoplasms</u>

* <u>HIV</u>, <u>hepatitis A</u>, <u>hepatitis B</u>, <u>hepatitis C</u>

* Bacterial infections (e.g., <u>syphilis</u>, <u>Lyme disease</u>, <u>tuberculosis</u>)

---

## Pathophysiology

* Formation of procoagulatory <u>antiphospholipid antibodies</u> 

* <u>Antibodies</u> form complexes with <u>anticoagulant</u> <u>proteins</u>, thereby inactivating them (e.g., <u>protein C</u>, <u>protein S</u>, <u>antithrombin III</u>).

* <u>Antibodies</u> activate <u>platelets</u> and vascular <u>endothelium</u>.

* Induction of a <u>hypercoagulable state</u> → ↑ risk of <u>thrombosis</u> and embolism [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/I4aYkk)

---

## Clinical features

<u>APS</u> usually manifests with recurring <u>thrombotic events</u> that may affect any organ. See also “<u>Clinical features suggestive of underlying thrombophilia</u>”.




* Venous

* <u>Deep vein thrombosis</u>

* <u>Pulmonary embolism</u>

* <u>Livedo reticularis</u>

* <u>Ulceration</u> (see “<u>Chronic venous insufficiency</u>”)

* Arterial

* <u>Stroke</u>, <u>transient ischemic attacks</u>

* Occlusion of organ <u>arteries</u> (e.g., <u>myocardial infarction</u>)

* Occlusion of <u>distal</u> extremity <u>arteries</u> (<u>ischemia</u> and <u>gangrene</u>)

* <u>Capillaries</u>: <u>splinter hemorrhages</u>

* <u>Pregnancy</u>-related: : recurrent <u>miscarriages</u> and premature births

* Nonthrombotic manifestations: may include <u>valvular heart disease</u>, neurocognitive disorders

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)




* Consider diagnostic testing based on the presence of typical symptoms, e.g.: 

* <u>Thrombotic</u> complications

* Recurrent <u>miscarriages</u>

* Obtain <u>antiphospholipid antibody</u> testing to confirm diagnosis.

* Consider testing for associated autoimmune diseases (e.g., <u>SLE</u>), <u>neoplasms</u>, and infectious diseases.

* Involve rheumatology early.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Thrombosis</u> in <u>APS</u> is typically unprovoked (e.g., <u>unprovoked DVT</u>), recurrent, and/or manifests in unusual sites (e.g., <u>kidneys</u>, <u>liver</u>, <u>retina</u>). It is most commonly seen in younger individuals (< 50 years of age) and in individuals with comorbid autoimmune diseases (e.g., <u>SLE</u>).

### Routine <u>laboratory studies</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)




Obtain for all patients; results are nonspecific but may support the diagnosis.




* <u>CBC</u>

* <u>Thrombocytopenia</u>

* <u>Leukocytopenia</u>

* <u>Hemolytic anemia</u> (may be <u>AIHA</u> or <u>MAHA</u>)

* <u>CMP</u>: may reveal <u>laboratory evidence of hemolysis</u> and/or nephropathy

* <u>Coagulation panel</u>: prolonged <u>aPTT</u> (caused by <u>lupus anticoagulant</u>)

* <u>Urinalysis</u>: may show <u>proteinuria</u>, e.g., in patients with <u>thrombotic microangiopathy</u>





### Antiphospholipid antibodies (<u>aPL antibodies</u>) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4aKkk)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)




Reserve testing for patients with characteristic clinical features to avoid <u>false positives</u>.




* Lupus anticoagulant (LA): <u>antibodies</u> against certain phospholipids in <u>cellular membranes</u>; detection involves a three-step procedure 
1. * Screening for phospholipid-dependent coagulation with either:

* Prolonged <u>aPTT</u>

* Prolonged <u>dilute Russell viper venom time</u> (<u>dRVVT</u>)
2. * Mixing study: The patient's plasma is mixed with normal plasma (which contains <u>clotting factors</u>).

* <u>aPTT</u> or <u>dRVVT</u> normalize: Presence of <u>lupus anticoagulant</u> ruled out; prolonged <u>aPTT</u> may be due to a lack of <u>clotting factors</u>.

* <u>aPTT</u> or <u>dRVVT</u> remain prolonged: <u>Lupus anticoagulant</u> may be present.
3. * Confirmation of phospholipid dependence: Phospholipid is added.

* <u>aPTT</u> or <u>dRVVT</u> normalize: Presence of <u>lupus anticoagulants</u> is confirmed.

* <u>aPTT</u> or <u>dRVVT</u> remain prolonged: Consider a factor deficiency.

* Anticardiolipin antibodies (<u>IgG</u> and <u>IgM</u>): <u>antibodies</u> against <u>cardiolipin</u>, a phospholipid in <u>cellular membranes</u>

* Anti-β2-glycoprotein antibodies (<u>IgG</u> and <u>IgM</u>): <u>antibodies</u> directed against the <u>cardiolipin</u> ­binding factor β2­ glycoprotein I that have prothrombotic effects  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)





> [!TIP]
> Patients with <u>APS</u> can test <u>false positive</u> for <u>syphilis</u> (positive <u>VDRL</u> or <u>RPR</u>) because the <u>antigen</u> used in <u>syphilis</u> tests is <u>cardiolipin</u>.




#### Interpretation [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)




* Symptomatic patients: The presence of ≥ 1 type of <u>aPL antibody</u> supports the diagnosis.

* Asymptomatic patients 

* The presence of associated <u>antibodies</u> is not sufficient to establish a diagnosis.

* Persistent positivity is associated with a higher risk of <u>thrombotic</u> and obstetric complications.





> [!TIP]
> Positive <u>lupus anticoagulant</u> is associated with the highest risk of <u>thrombosis</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> A finding of positive <u>aPL antibodies</u> without clinical features is not sufficient to confirm a diagnosis of <u>APS</u>.




### Additional studies [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)




* Nonclassic <u>aPL antibodies</u>: may further support the diagnosis

* Positive <u>antibodies</u> against annexin, <u>protein C</u>, and/or <u>protein S</u>

* Positive <u>ANAs</u> and/or anti-Ds-<u>DNA</u> <u>antibodies</u>

* Imaging studies

* May be indicated to evaluate for associated conditions and <u>thrombotic events</u>

* Examples:

* <u>Stroke imaging</u>

* <u>Lower extremity venous ultrasound</u> for <u>DVT</u>

* <u>TTE</u> to evaluate for <u>valvular heart disease</u>

* <u>Histopathology</u>: may be needed to confirm <u>thrombosis</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4aKkk)

## Management

Management should be specialist-guided.




### Approach [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)




* Treat acute <u>thrombotic events</u> (e.g., <u>DVT</u>, <u>MI</u>).  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)

* Provide urgent <u>management for CAPS</u> in acutely ill patients with <u>multiorgan failure</u> due to <u>thrombosis</u>.

* Initiate long-term <u>thromboprophylaxis</u>.

* Primary <u>thromboprophylaxis</u>: <u>low-dose aspirin therapy</u>

* Secondary <u>thromboprophylaxis</u>

* First-line: <u>warfarin</u>

* Second-line: <u>LMWH</u> or <u>UFH</u>

* <u>Thromboprophylaxis</u> in pregnant individuals: <u>low-dose aspirin therapy</u> PLUS <u>heparin</u> (<u>LMWH</u> or <u>UFH</u>)

* Provide <u>supportive care</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Systemic anticoagulation is the cornerstone of treatment for <u>thrombotic</u> <u>APS</u>.




### Thromboprophylaxis for APS [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)




#### Primary <u>thromboprophylaxis</u>




* Consider for patients with no history of <u>thrombosis</u> who have:

* A history of obstetric <u>APS</u>

* Positive <u>aPL antibodies</u> without an <u>APS</u> diagnosis, especially those with:

* High-risk <u>aPL antibody</u> profile

* <u>ASCVD risk factors</u>

* Comorbid autoimmune diseases (e.g., <u>SLE</u>)

* Recommended regimen: <u>low-dose aspirin therapy</u>





> [!TIP]
> Decisions on primary <u>thromboprophylaxis</u> should be made by a specialist based on individual <u>thrombosis</u> risk and bleeding risk.




#### Secondary <u>thromboprophylaxis</u>




* Indication: <u>APS</u> with a history of <u>thrombosis</u>

* Recommended regimens

* First-line: <u>warfarin</u> DOSAGE

* Target <u>INR</u>: usually 2–3

* Provide <u>bridging anticoagulation</u> (e.g., <u>enoxaparin</u> DOSAGE) until target <u>INR</u> is reached.

* For patients with recurrent <u>thrombosis</u> despite target <u>INR</u>: Adjust therapy under specialist guidance.

* Second-line 

* Heparins: including <u>LMWH</u> (e.g., <u>enoxaparin</u> DOSAGE) and <u>UFH</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)

* <u>DOACs</u> (not recommended for arterial <u>thrombosis</u>): e.g., <u>dabigatran</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)

* Treatment duration

* Unprovoked <u>thrombosis</u>: Continue long-term.

* Provoked <u>thrombosis</u> (e.g., after <u>surgery</u>):

* Stop according to general population guidelines.

* See also “<u>Anticoagulation for PE</u>” and “<u>Therapeutic approach to DVT</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> <u>Antiphospholipid antibodies</u> can prolong the <u>aPTT</u> and consequently provide falsely elevated <u>INR</u> results, which may lead to inadequate anticoagulation. Consider measurement of <u>factor X</u> activity to confirm adequate anticoagulation. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)




#### Thromboprophylaxis for APS during pregnancy [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iX1JB20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)




* Patients without an <u>APS</u> diagnosis with positive <u>aPL antibodies</u> (with or without <u>SLE</u>): Consider <u>low-dose aspirin therapy</u>.

* Patients with an <u>APS</u> diagnosis

* <u>Low-dose aspirin therapy</u> PLUS <u>heparin</u> (<u>LMWH</u> or <u>UFH</u>)

* History of <u>thrombotic</u> <u>APS</u>: Use therapeutic <u>heparin</u> dosages.

* History of obstetric <u>APS</u> (without <u>thrombosis</u>): Use prophylactic <u>heparin</u> dosages.

* Patients require close monitoring and management by a team of specialists to reduce the risk of <u>miscarriage</u> and <u>thrombosis</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Warfarin</u> is <u>teratogenic</u> and therefore contraindicated during <u>pregnancy</u>.




### <u>Supportive care</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)




* Management of additional <u>risk factors</u> for <u>thrombosis</u>

* <u>Atherosclerotic risk factors</u>: <u>ASCVD risk assessment</u> and management

* <u>Risk factors for VTE</u>

* Avoidance of medications that contain <u>estrogen</u>

* Pharmacological <u>thromboprophylaxis</u> during the perioperative period

* <u>Patient counseling</u> (e.g., about <u>VKA</u> treatment, <u>contraceptives</u>, <u>hormone</u> therapy)

* <u>Counseling on lifestyle modifications</u> (e.g., healthy diet, physical activity)

* Management of complications (e.g., <u>thrombocytopenia</u>, nephropathy)





### Management of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VX1GC20)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Te16yf0)




<u>CAPS</u> is a rare but severe form of <u>APS</u> caused by acute and systemic small vessel <u>thrombosis</u>.




* Suspect in acutely ill patients with <u>multiorgan failure</u> due to <u>thrombosis</u>.

* Consider other <u>differential diagnoses of platelet disorders</u>.

* Diagnosis of <u>CAPS</u> is based on:

* Simultaneous involvement of ≥ 3 organs, systems, or tissues

* Confirmation of positive <u>aPL antibodies</u>

* Start treatment as soon as <u>CAPS</u> is suspected; do not wait for laboratory confirmation.

* Treatment is not standardized but may include the following: [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lc1vcf0)

* Combination of high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u>, <u>heparin</u>, plasma exchange, and <u>IV immunoglobulin</u>

* Management of triggers: e.g., infections (most common), surgical interventions, <u>neoplasms</u>





> [!TIP]
> Diagnosis of <u>CAPS</u> is difficult because clinical presentation varies widely and approximately 50% of affected individuals have no history of <u>aPL antibody</u> positivity. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VX1GC20)




> [!WARNING]
> The <u>mortality rate</u> of <u>CAPS</u> is approximately 50%, even with treatment. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VX1GC20)
