# Anticoagulant reversal

## Summary

<u>Anticoagulant</u> reversal is indicated for <u>major bleeding</u> (e.g., critical site bleeding and/or bleeding with <u>hemodynamic instability</u>) and before urgent procedures with significant bleeding risk. The approach to <u>anticoagulant</u> reversal is based on the clinical context and the <u>anticoagulant</u>. For all clinically significant bleeding (with or without <u>major bleeding</u>), stop the <u>anticoagulant</u> and provide general <u>hemostatic measures</u> (e.g., <u>antifibrinolytics</u> and procedural intervention). The agents for <u>anticoagulant</u> reversal are <u>vitamin K</u> and <u>prothrombin complex concentrate</u> (<u>PCC</u>) for <u>vitamin K antagonists</u> (<u>VKAs</u>), idaricizumab for <u>dabigatran</u>, <u>4-factor PCC</u> for <u>factor Xa inhibitors</u>, and <u>protamine</u> for <u>heparin</u>. Reversal agents should be used with caution, as they increase the risk of <u>thrombotic events</u>. All patients who undergo <u>anticoagulant</u> reversal should be monitored closely.




See “<u>Periprocedural management of oral anticoagulant therapy</u>" for management of anticoagulation before nonemergency procedures (e.g., <u>DOAC</u> interruption and <u>bridging anticoagulation</u>).

> [!WARNING]
> Nonspecific reversal agents such as <u>4-factor PCC</u>, <u>aPCC</u>, <u>recombinant activated factor VII</u>, <u>thrombocyte</u> concentrates, and <u>FFP</u> have procoagulatory effects. Before these drugs are administered, the increased risk of <u>thrombosis</u> should be carefully weighed against the risk of ongoing bleeding.
