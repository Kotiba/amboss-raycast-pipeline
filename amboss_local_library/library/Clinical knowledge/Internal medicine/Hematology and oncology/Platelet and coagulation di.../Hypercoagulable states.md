[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bo0H0S)

## Summary

A hypercoagulable state, i.e., thrombophilia, is a predisposition to forming blood clots. Depending on the etiology, one or more factors of the <u>Virchow triad</u> (stasis, hypercoagulability, <u>endothelial</u> damage) may be involved. Hypercoagulability may be acquired or inherited and can affect <u>veins</u> and/or <u>arteries</u>. It leads to an increased risk of developing <u>venous thromboembolism</u> (<u>VTE</u>), which most commonly manifests as <u>deep venous thrombosis</u> of the lower extremities or <u>pulmonary embolism</u>. Arterial involvement increases the risk of <u>myocardial infarction</u>, <u>stroke</u>, and spontaneous abortion. Evaluation for hypercoagulability includes assessment of potential <u>risk factors</u> (e.g., <u>immobilization</u>, smoking, <u>oral contraceptive</u> use, and <u>malignancy</u>) and laboratory tests to assess anomalies of the <u>clotting cascade</u> (e.g., <u>factor V Leiden</u>, <u>antiphospholipid antibody syndrome</u>). Treatment is based on the underlying condition and typically includes a reduction of <u>risk factors</u> and/or the administration of <u>anticoagulants</u>.

## Definitions

* Thrombophilia: A predisposition to increased coagulation that typically manifests as recurrent <u>thromboembolism</u>.

* Thromboembolism: The formation and/or migration of blood clots in different locations of the venous or arterial vasculature that can occlude or impair the pulmonary or <u>systemic circulation</u>.

* Venous thromboembolism (<u>VTE</u>)

* Blood clots that form within the venous vascular system, dislodge, and travel to a distant location, e.g., the <u>pulmonary arteries</u> via the right <u>heart</u>

* Examples include <u>deep venous thrombosis</u>, <u>pulmonary embolism</u>, <u>portal vein thrombosis</u>, <u>cerebral venous thrombosis</u>

* Provoked VTE: <u>VTE</u> occurring in the presence of <u>traditional risk factors for thromboembolic disease</u>, e.g., ≥ 1 strong trigger OR multiple weak triggers  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cmcq310)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9XXNZC)

* Unprovoked VTE: <u>VTE</u> occurring in the absence of identifiable triggers.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PBcWaU0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4Bc3aU0)

* Arterial thromboembolism

* Blood clots that form within the arterial vascular system, dislodge, and travel to a distant location, e.g., <u>distal</u> systemic <u>arteries</u> and <u>arterioles</u>

* Usually, an acute event that results in <u>ischemic</u> tissue damage (e.g., <u>stroke</u>, <u>acute mesenteric ischemia</u>, <u>acute limb ischemia</u>, <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>, <u>pulmonary infarction</u>)

## Etiology

The development of thromboembolic disease is multifactorial and more commonly triggered by traditional <u>risk factors</u> than by inherited or <u>acquired thrombophilia</u>. A single patient may have multiple underlying conditions or <u>risk factors</u> (both hereditary and acquired) which can lead to a higher cumulative risk of <u>thromboembolism</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmcvf10)




### Traditional risk factors for thromboembolic disease [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cmcq310)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9XXNZC)




* Venous <u>thrombosis</u>: See also “<u>Risk factors for VTE</u>.”  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kBcmaU0)

* Strong: trauma, <u>fractures</u>, major orthopedic <u>surgery</u>, oncological <u>surgery</u>, <u>immobilization</u> combined with other <u>risk factors</u>

* Moderate: nononcological <u>surgery</u>, <u>exogenous</u> <u>estrogen</u> (e.g., <u>OCPs</u>, <u>HRT</u>); , <u>pregnancy</u> and <u>puerperium</u>, previous <u>VTE</u>

* Weak: advanced age, prolonged travel, bed rest (e.g., > 3 days) as a sole <u>risk factor</u>, <u>metabolic syndrome</u>

* Arterial <u>thrombosis</u>: See also “<u>Atherosclerotic risk factors</u>.”

* Smoking

* <u>Obesity</u>

* <u>Hyperlipidemia</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u>

## Clinical features

### Clinical features of <u>thromboembolism</u>




These typically depend on the location of <u>thromboembolism</u>, the vasculature involved, and the underlying condition.




* <u>VTE</u> (most common): See “<u>Clinical features of DVT</u>”, “<u>Clinical features of PE</u>”, “<u>Clinical features of cerebral venous thrombosis</u>”, and “<u>Portal vein thrombosis</u>.”

* <u>Arterial thromboembolism</u>

* <u>Ischemic stroke</u>: See “<u>Stroke symptoms by affected vessel</u>” and “<u>Stroke symptoms by affected region</u>.”

* <u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>: See “<u>Clinical features of ACS</u>.”

* Others: e.g., the <u>6 P's</u> of <u>acute limb ischemia</u>, clinical features of <u>intestinal ischemia</u>, <u>splenic infarction</u>





### Clinical features suggestive of underlying thrombophilia [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmcvf10)




* <u>VTE</u> characteristics

* Onset at age < 50 years of either of the following:

* <u>Unprovoked VTE</u>

* <u>VTE</u> associated with only weak <u>risk factors</u> (See “<u>Traditional risk factors for thromboembolism</u>.”)

* Unusual <u>thrombus</u> localization: e.g., <u>portal vein thrombosis</u>, <u>mesenteric vein thrombosis</u>, <u>cerebral venous thrombosis</u>, <u>central retinal vein occlusion</u>.

* Strong <u>family history</u> of <u>VTE</u>

* <u>Recurrent VTE</u> or multiple <u>VTE</u>

* History of <u>warfarin-induced skin necrosis</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1yb2Vw)

* Frequent obstetrical complications: e.g., <u>recurrent pregnancy loss</u>, <u>IUFD</u>, <u>IUGR</u>, <u>preeclampsia</u> [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5BciYU0)

* <u>Arterial thromboembolism</u> (e.g., <u>stroke</u>) in a young patient with no <u>cardiovascular risk factors</u>





> [!TIP]
> Consider <u>antiphospholipid syndrome</u> in young patients with <u>stroke</u> with no <u>cardiovascular risk factors</u>.

## Pathophysiology

Underlying mechanisms vary depending on whether thrombophilia is hereditary or acquired.




* <u>Hereditary thrombophilias</u>: typically caused by mutations of <u>proteins</u> and enzymes involved in the <u>coagulation cascade</u>

* <u>Acquired thrombophilias</u>: varying underlying mechanisms that include stasis, <u>endothelial</u> injury, changes in elements of the <u>coagulation cascade</u>, as well as the formation, release, or exposure to additional <u>procoagulant</u> substances.

## Diagnosis

### General principles




* Most thrombophilias are identified following a <u>thromboembolic event</u>, the majority of which are multifactorial, involving a combination of transient and chronic <u>risk factors</u>.

* <u>Thrombophilia testing</u> is unnecessary in most cases.

* Perform a detailed clinical evaluation to identify patients who may benefit from additional testing.

* See “Prevention” for <u>screening recommendations</u> in asymptomatic patients.





### Routine investigations




Often performed as part of the initial workup of a <u>thromboembolic event</u> and can help identify some predisposing conditions.




* <u>CBC</u>: E.g., cell counts may be abnormal in <u>MPNs</u>.

* <u>BMP</u>: e.g., can suggest <u>nephrotic syndrome</u>

* <u>LFTs</u>: abnormalities can suggest <u>liver</u> disease, e.g, <u>cirrhosis</u>

* <u>Coagulation studies</u>: e.g., ↑ <u>aPTT</u> in <u>antiphospholipid antibody syndrome</u>

* <u>ESR</u>: e.g., can be elevated in <u>malignancy</u> or <u>SLE</u>

* <u>β-hCG</u>: sensitive and specific for undiagnosed <u>pregnancy</u>





### Thrombophilia testing [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/id1Jpf0)




<u>Thrombophilia testing</u> is only warranted in select cases, however, there are no universally agreed-upon indications. The final decision on specific investigations is usually done by a specialist based on a detailed individual assessment, clinical judgment, and whether results will alter management  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9rcNjd0)




#### Patient selection




* Evaluate the presence, type, extent, and underlying triggers of <u>thromboembolic events</u>.

* Further testing is typically unnecessary for patients with:

* Clear and strong <u>traditional risk factors for thromboembolic disease</u>

* Previously diagnosed acquired <u>causes of thrombophilia</u>

* Consider referral to a specialist for further testing in patients with <u>clinical features suggestive of underlying thrombophilia</u>, for example:

* <u>Hereditary thrombophilias</u>: <u>unprovoked VTE</u>, <u>recurrent VTE</u>, young patients with <u>provoked VTE</u> and only weak <u>risk factors</u>, or <u>VTE</u> occurring at an unusual location, <u>recurrent pregnancy loss</u>

* <u>Antiphospholipid syndrome</u>: patients with arterial <u>thrombosis</u> in the absence of traditional <u>cardiovascular risk factors</u> ., or patients with extensive <u>DVT</u> or <u>PE</u>

* <u>Myeloproliferative neoplasms</u> and <u>PNH</u>: <u>VTE</u> involving the <u>splanchnic</u> <u>veins</u> (e.g., <u>portal vein thrombosis</u>, <u>mesenteric vein thrombosis</u>)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Thrombophilia workup</u> can safely be deferred in most patients with a <u>provoked VTE</u>, e.g., due to a strong trigger. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)




#### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmcvf10)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TQc6vX0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5BciYU0)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pQcLCX0)




Consider the following based on clinical suspicion as guided by a specialist:




* <u>Hereditary thrombophilias</u>  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5BciYU0)

* <u>Activated protein C resistance</u> assay; followed by <u>genetic testing</u> for <u>Factor V Leiden</u> if positive

* <u>Prothrombin G20210A mutation</u> testing

* Activity assays for <u>protein C</u>, <u>protein S</u>, <u>antithrombin</u>

* <u>Acquired thrombophilias</u>

* <u>APLAS</u>: <u>antiphospholipid antibody</u> panel (<u>lupus anticoagulants</u>, anticardiolipin <u>antibodies</u>, and <u>anti-β2-glycoprotein antibodies</u>)

* <u>MPNs</u>: <u>peripheral blood smear</u> and <u>bone marrow biopsy</u>

* <u>PNH</u>: <u>flow cytometry</u>

* <u>HIT</u> (with intermediate or high <u>4T score</u>): <u>HIT-specific diagnostic tests</u>, e.g., <u>PF4 heparin immunoassay</u>, <u>serotonin release assay</u>

* <u>ASCVD</u>: <u>lipoprotein(a)</u> levels





#### Timing of investigations




* Not indicated during an acute event  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)

* Only conduct once initial anticoagulation therapy has been completed.

* Ensure there has been a sufficient duration since the completion of therapy. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)

* <u>Vitamin K antagonists</u>: ≥ 2 weeks

* <u>Direct oral anticoagulants</u>: usually 2–3 days





### Screening for occult <u>malignancy</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1yb2Vw)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bybHdw)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xyb9dw)




* Indications: : <u>unprovoked VTE</u> (especially in patients aged > 50 years), <u>recurrent VTE</u>, unusual <u>thrombus</u> location

* Investigations

* Routine age-appropriate <u>cancer screening</u> is recommended and may include: routine <u>laboratory studies</u>, <u>urinalysis</u>, <u>FOBT</u>, serum <u>calcium</u> levels.

* Consider <u>CXR</u>, <u>colonoscopy</u>, <u>mammogram</u>, <u>digital rectal examination</u>, <u>Pap smear</u>.  [[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bybHdw)

## Treatment

### General principles




* A diagnosis of thrombophilia seldom alters the management of acute <u>thromboembolic events</u>, except in select cases (see “Acute management of specific thrombophilias”). [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)

* Most patients are diagnosed following a <u>thromboembolic event</u> and long-term management consists mostly of <u>secondary prevention</u>.

* Options include anticoagulation and reduction of <u>modifiable risk factors</u>.

* Recommendations vary depending on the type and extent of <u>thromboembolism</u> and patient characteristics (see conditions in “Standard management of thromboembolic disease”).

* Management of asymptomatic patients consists primarily of nonpharmacologic prophylaxis against <u>thromboembolic events</u>; see “Prevention” for details.





> [!WARNING]
> Weigh risks and benefits of anticoagulation individually for patients with increased bleeding risk, e.g., risk of falls, severe <u>hypertension</u>.




### Standard management of thromboembolic diseases




#### <u>Arterial thromboembolism</u>




* Acute management can include <u>revascularization</u>, <u>fibrinolytics</u>, and anticoagulation depending on the location and extent of <u>thromboembolism</u> and patient factors.

* See “<u>Management of STEMI</u>”, “<u>Management of NSTEMI/UA</u>”, “<u>Management of ischemic stroke</u>”, and “<u>Secondary prevention strategies for ASCVD</u>.”

* See also “Treatment” in “<u>Acute limb ischemia</u>”, “<u>Intestinal ischemia</u>”, and “<u>Splenic infarct</u>.”





#### <u>Venous thromboembolism</u>




* Acute management typically involves <u>anticoagulant</u> administration.

* <u>Therapeutic approach to DVT</u> varies for <u>provoked DVT</u> and <u>unprovoked DVT</u> and depends on location and risk of recurrence.

* Most patients with <u>PE</u> require anticoagulation; in some causes <u>empiric anticoagulation for PE</u> or <u>thrombolysis for PE</u> may be necessary.

* For high-risk patients (based on individual risk assessment), consider <u>bridging anticoagulation</u>: i.e., parallel administration of <u>warfarin</u> and <u>heparin</u> until target <u>INR</u> is reached, typically for 4–6 days.   [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HB0KXi)

* High-risk conditions include:

* Mechanical <u>mitral valve</u>

* <u>Stroke</u> or <u>TIA</u> in the last three months

* <u>A-fib</u> with <u>CHADS2 score</u> ≥ 5

* High-risk thrombophilias include:

* <u>Antithrombin</u>, <u>Protein C</u>, or <u>protein S</u> deficiency

* <u>Antiphospholipid syndrome</u>

* <u>Homozygous</u> <u>factor V Leiden mutation</u>

* Consider an <u>inferior vena cava filter</u> if <u>anticoagulant</u> therapy is contraindicated.

* See also “<u>Treatment of DVT</u>”, “<u>Treatment of PE</u>”, and “Treatment” in “<u>Portal vein thrombosis</u>”, and “<u>Cerebral venous thrombosis</u>.” [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UyYbe7)





### Acute management of specific thrombophilias




Consult a hematologist to tailor treatment according to individual patient risks.




* <u>Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia type II</u>: <u>Heparin</u> is contraindicated and <u>argatroban</u> or lepirudin should be prescribed instead; see “<u>Empirical management of HIT</u>” and “<u>Nonheparin anticoagulation</u>.”

* <u>Antithrombin III deficiency</u>: can lead to <u>heparin resistance</u> and may require <u>antithrombin</u> concentrate in addition to <u>heparin</u> in order to be effective [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jQc_DX0)

* <u>Protein C deficiency</u> and <u>protein S deficiency</u>: To avoid <u>warfarin-induced skin necrosis</u>, bridge oral anticoagulation with <u>heparin</u>.  [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jQc_DX0)

* <u>Antiphospholipid syndrome</u> [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iBcJ0U0)

* Anticoagulation regimens: lifelong anticoagulation usually required

* <u>VTE</u>: <u>VKAs</u> (e.g., <u>warfarin</u>) with <u>heparin bridging</u>

* <u>Arterial thromboembolism</u>: High-dose <u>VKA</u> OR standard-dose <u>VKA</u> combined with <u>ASA</u>

* Consider adjunctive therapy for recurrent or very severe disease.

## Prevention

This section describes screening for asymptomatic patients at risk of thrombophilia and <u>primary prevention</u> measures for <u>thromboembolism</u> in patients with positive screening or <u>risk factors</u> (see “Treatment” for <u>secondary prevention</u> measures). Standard <u>VTE prophylaxis</u> is indicated in select circumstances regardless of thrombophilia status (e.g., postoperative status, prolonged <u>immobilization</u> or hospitalization, active <u>malignancy</u>).




### Indications for <u>thrombophilia screening</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmcvf10)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pQcLCX0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eLcx910)




Screening for thrombophilia in asymptomatic patients may be indicated in patients at high risk and, depending on the results, a specialist may choose to start prophylaxis. Possible indications for screening include:




* Patients with a personal history of a <u>thromboembolic event</u> who wish to conceive or start <u>contraception</u>

* Selection of <u>contraception</u> in patients with a strong <u>family history</u>

* High-risk patients who are pregnant or having <u>fertility counseling</u>





> [!TIP]
> There is no clear guidance for <u>thrombophilia screening</u> and <u>VTE prophylaxis</u> for asymptomatic patients; decisions should be guided by a specialist. [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eLcx910)




### Management of asymptomatic thrombophilia




<u>Primary prevention</u> is generally not recommended. However, some high-risk patients may benefit from managing <u>modifiable risk factors</u> and primary prophylaxis measures. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TQc6vX0)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pQcLCX0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eLcx910)




* <u>Obesity</u>: Recommend weight loss.

* Tobacco use: Encourage <u>smoking cessation</u>.

* Medications: Avoid <u>estrogens</u> (e.g., <u>OCPs</u>), <u>erythropoietin</u>, and <u>testosterone</u>.

* Extended <u>varicosis</u>: Consider <u>compression stockings</u>, an <u>LMWH</u>, and referral for surgical repair.

* <u>Myeloproliferative neoplasms</u>: Start treatment in consultation with a specialist (e.g., anticoagulation, immunotherapy, or <u>allogeneic stem cell transplant</u>).

* High-risk situations: Primary prophylaxis (<u>compression stockings</u>, chemical prophylaxis with an <u>LMWH</u> or <u>direct oral anticoagulant</u>) may be appropriate.

* <u>Immobilization</u> and travel lasting > 4 hours

* Cancer

* <u>Pregnancy</u>: Consider prophylaxis with <u>LMWH</u> plus <u>ASA</u> for patients with <u>APLAS</u> who desire children. [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fQckvX0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iBcJ0U0)

* <u>Surgery</u>: Consider pneumatic stockings, early <u>physiotherapy</u>, and hydration in addition to the above measures.

* See also “<u>Approach to VTE prophylaxis</u>” and “<u>ASCVD prevention</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> Primary prophylactic measures are also recommended in the absence of laboratory findings of thrombophilia if patients have a strong <u>family history</u> of thrombophilia. [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pQcLCX0)




> [!TIP]
> Avoid <u>OCPs</u> in patients who are carriers of <u>Factor V Leiden</u>. [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MBcMYU0)
