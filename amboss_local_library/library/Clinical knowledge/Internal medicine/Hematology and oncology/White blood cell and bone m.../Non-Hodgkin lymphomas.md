# Non-Hodgkin lymphomas

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Hematology and oncology > White blood cell and bone marrow disorders > Non-Hodgkin lymphomas*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NT0-I2)

---

## Summary

<u>Lymphomas</u> are malignancies that arise from <u>lymphocytes</u> and are classified as either <u>Hodgkin lymphomas</u> (characterized by <u>Reed-Sternberg</u> cells) or non-Hodgkin <u>lymphomas</u> (NHLs), which comprise all other types of <u>lymphoma</u>. NHLs are further classified according to cell type, i.e., <u>B cells</u>, <u>T cells</u>, and <u>natural killer (NK) cells</u>; location (nodal or extranodal); and <u>tumor grade</u>. Low-grade tumors originate from mature cells that have a slow growth rate and an indolent clinical course. The most common low-grade <u>B-cell lymphoma</u> is <u>follicular lymphoma</u>, while the most common low-grade <u>T-cell lymphomas</u> are cutaneous <u>T-cell lymphomas</u>, such as <u>mycosis fungoides</u>. High-grade tumors have a rapid growth rate and an aggressive clinical course. Certain subtypes of NHL, such as <u>Burkitt lymphoma</u>, are more common in children and young adults than in older adults. NHL is diagnosed by obtaining a <u>biopsy</u> of the affected tissue and carrying out a detailed assessment, including <u>immunophenotyping</u>, genetics, and molecular testing. These studies allow for the identification of specific NHL subtypes, which guides treatment. Generally, treatment involves a combination of <u>chemotherapy</u> and <u>radiation therapy</u>. Patients with high-grade NHLs and those with low-grade tumors and limited disease are treated with curative intent. Patients with advanced, low-grade tumors who experience symptoms usually receive <u>palliative treatment</u>.

## Epidemiology

* <u>Incidence</u>: NHL is the most common <u>hematopoietic</u> <u>neoplasm</u>.  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HbXKE9)

* Age

* The <u>incidence</u> of all NHLs increases with age; the peak <u>incidence</u> is in those aged > 50 years.

* High-grade <u>lymphomas</u> are most common in children and young adults (20–40 years).




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

---

## Etiology

* <u>Chromosomal translocations</u>: most commonly t(14;18)

* Infections [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ebXxs9)

* <u>EBV</u>

* <u>HIV</u>

* <u>HTLV</u>-1

* <u>HCV</u>

* <u>Helicobacter pylori</u>: associated with <u>gastric lymphoma</u>, e.g., <u>MALT lymphoma</u>, <u>diffuse large B-cell lymphoma</u> (<u>DLBCL</u>)

* Autoimmune diseases: <u>Hashimoto thyroiditis</u>, <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>

* <u>Immunodeficiency</u>: <u>congenital immunodeficiencies</u>, <u>AIDS</u>, history of <u>chemotherapy</u> and/or <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u>

* Environmental factors: <u>aromatic hydrocarbons</u> (e.g., <u>benzene</u>), radiation

## Clinical features

* Nodal disease: typically painless <u>lymphadenopathy</u> associated with fatigue and <u>weakness</u> (multiple noncontiguous <u>lymph nodes</u> may be involved) ;  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nVX7EC)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_uY5FI)

* High grade

* Rapidly growing mass/nodes

* <u>Constitutional symptoms</u> or <u>B symptoms</u> (i.e., weight loss, <u>fever</u>, night sweats)

* Low grade

* Slow-growing or undulant <u>lymphadenopathy</u> (over months or years)

* <u>Hepatosplenomegaly</u>

* <u>Cytopenias</u>: Patients may present with <u>anemia</u> or bleeding, or have an increased susceptibility to infections.




![Lymphadenopathy](../../../../../media/rae0rqlu6st5a4r1f4qkgey7.jpg)

Lymphadenopathy




* Extranodal disease (primary or secondary): The symptoms are highly dependent on the affected tissue; <u>B symptoms</u> are common. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nVX7EC)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_uY5FI)

* <u>GI tract</u>: e.g., early satiety, <u>GI bleeding</u>

* Neurological involvement: e.g., <u>headache</u>, <u>focal neurologic symptoms</u>

* Primary cutaneous NHL or secondary <u>skin</u> infiltration: e.g., <u>rash</u>, <u>plaques</u>, tumors, <u>ulcers</u>

* <u>Thyroid</u> involvement (rare): e.g., <u>nodules</u>, <u>goiter</u>

* <u>Oncologic emergencies</u>/<u>paraneoplastic syndromes</u>  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nVX7EC)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_uY5FI)

* Examples include <u>tumor lysis syndrome</u>, <u>hypercalcemia</u>, <u>spinal cord compression</u>, <u>superior vena cava syndrome</u>, <u>cardiac tamponade</u>, <u>lymphomatous meningitis</u>, and a <u>CNS</u> mass.

* See “<u>Oncologic emergencies</u>” for detailed information on the management of these conditions.

See ”Differential diagnosis of <u>B symptoms</u>”, “<u>Enlarged lymph nodes</u>”, and “<u>Differential diagnosis of granulomatous disease</u>” for more information.




### Extranodal masses




* <u>Thymoma</u>

* <u>Teratoma</u> (and other <u>germ cell tumors</u>)

* <u>Thyroid</u> <u>neoplasm</u>





> [!NOTE]
> The 4 T's of <u>anterior</u> <u>mediastinal masses</u>: Thymoma, Teratoma (and other <u>germ cell tumors</u>), Thyroid <u>neoplasm</u>, and Terrible <u>lymphoma</u>.




### Necrotizing lymphadenitis (<u>Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease</u>) [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TbX6G9)




* Etiology: unknown

* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* Rare; most commonly reported in Asian population

* Sex: <u>♀</u> > <u>♂</u>

* Mean age: 30 years

* Clinical features: painful <u>cervical lymphadenopathy</u> and <u>fever</u>

* Diagnostics: <u>Lymph node</u> <u>biopsy</u> shows single or multiple <u>necrotic</u> foci, histiocytic cellular infiltration, without granulocytic involvement.

* Treatment: typically resolves spontaneously within 1–4 months of onset without treatment





The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Lwawj5)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NKX-g_)




* Suspect NHL in patients with suggestive clinical or laboratory features.

* Confirm the diagnosis and determine the subtype via <u>lymph node</u> and/or tissue <u>biopsies</u>.

* Stage and classify the disease (see “<u>Staging of NHL</u>”), e.g.:

* Imaging studies: to determine the extent of the disease and detect possible <u>CNS</u> involvement

* <u>Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy</u>: to detect <u>bone marrow</u> involvement





> [!TIP]
> The clinical presentation of patients with NHL varies (e.g., nodal symptoms, extranodal symptoms, <u>oncologic emergencies</u>, or <u>paraneoplastic syndromes</u>); therefore, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion to facilitate early diagnosis.




### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)




* Routine <u>laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: may show <u>anemia</u>, <u>thrombocytopenia</u>; <u>WBC count</u> may be high or low (commonly <u>leukopenia</u>, <u>lymphocytosis</u>)

* <u>BMP</u>: may show abnormal renal function  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y6Xdn_)

* Serum <u>calcium</u>: may show <u>hypercalcemia</u>

* <u>Liver chemistries</u>: may be abnormal in patients with <u>liver</u> infiltration or <u>primary hepatic lymphoma</u>  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F6Xg5_)

* Markers of disease activity [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DUc12b0)

* <u>Uric acid</u>: usually elevated

* <u>LDH</u>: usually elevated

* Serum <u>β2-microglobulin</u>: may be elevated

* Others: <u>CRP</u>, <u>ESR</u>

* Viral <u>serologies</u>

* <u>HIV screening</u>

* Additional studies can be suggestive of the underlying etiology (e.g., <u>hepatitis B</u> and C, <u>EBV</u>, <u>HTLV</u>-1).





### Confirmatory diagnostics tests [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_uY5FI)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Lwawj5)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NKX-g_)




<u>Histopathological studies</u> are required to diagnose <u>lymphoma</u>. Large samples are preferred as intact tissue architecture is required to classify the subtype. If initial <u>biopsy</u> results are negative in patients with symptoms that are highly suggestive of <u>lymphoma</u>, consider obtaining a larger <u>biopsy</u> sample and repeating the studies.




#### Selection of <u>biopsy</u> sample




* Nodal disease

* Select the most appropriate node for <u>biopsy</u> (e.g., a node with significant, progressive, and persistent enlargement).

* Imaging methods such as CT and <u>PET-CT</u> may be used to select a site (see “<u>Staging of NHL</u>”).

* Techniques [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NKX-g_)

* Preferred: excisional <u>lymph node</u> <u>biopsy</u> or <u>core needle biopsy</u>

* Alternative: incisional <u>lymph node</u> <u>biopsy</u>

* Avoid <u>fine-needle aspiration biopsy</u>.

* Extranodal disease

* Excisional tissue <u>biopsies</u> are recommended.

* <u>Biopsies</u> frequently require guidance, e.g., with endoscopy   or using <u>ultrasound</u>.

#### <u>Histopathology</u> and specialized studies




These studies help determine the subtype of NHL.




* <u>Histopathology</u>: provides a detailed morphology of individual proliferating cells and a description of the pattern of <u>lymph node</u> (or tissue) infiltration (e.g., nodular, diffuse)

* <u>Immunophenotype</u> (e.g., <u>flow cytometry</u>, <u>immunohistochemistry</u>)

* Detects surface <u>antigens</u>, determines the specific cell type (<u>B cell</u>/<u>T cell</u>), and identifies specific markers

* Possible findings include:

* <u>B-cell lymphomas</u>: <u>CD20</u> positive

* <u>T-cell lymphomas</u>: <u>CD3</u> positive

* Genetic studies

* Cytogenetics (<u>karyotype</u>, <u>FISH</u>): can identify <u>chromosomal abnormalities</u>, e.g., t(14;18) in <u>follicular lymphoma</u>

* Molecular testing (e.g., <u>PCR</u>): can detect clonality, e.g., in <u>B-cell receptors</u> or <u>T-cell receptors</u>

## Staging and classification

### Staging [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Lwawj5)




* Imaging

* Indicated in all patients for staging and to assess response to therapy

* Choice of imaging modality depends on the suspected subtype of NHL (uptake of <u>FDG</u> varies between subtypes)

* <u>FDG</u>-avid NHLs (most subtypes): <u>PET-CT</u>

* Non-<u>FDG</u>-avid NHLs : <u>CT whole body</u> with contrast

* <u>Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy</u>: indicated in most newly diagnosed patients with NHL

* Assessment of <u>CNS</u> involvement

* Indications

* Patients considered high-risk for <u>CNS</u> involvement  [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/u6Xp5_)

* <u>Primary CNS lymphoma</u>

* Patients with neurological signs and symptoms

* Patients with <u>HIV</u>

* Recommended modalities include:

* Imaging (<u>MRI</u> or CT <u>brain</u>)

* <u>Lumbar puncture</u> with <u>CSF</u> assessment (<u>cytology</u>; detection of <u>EBV</u> <u>DNA</u>)

### Classification [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Lwawj5)




* Lugano classification is the preferred classification method for primary nodal NHL. 

* Imaging is used to assess the number and location of affected <u>lymph nodes</u>, <u>tumor</u> bulk, and <u>liver</u> and <u>spleen</u> involvement.

* <u>Bone marrow biopsy</u> to assess <u>bone marrow</u> involvement

* The disease is then classified as either:

* Limited disease (stage I + II): one node or conglomerate (stage I), or ≥ 2 nodes or conglomerates on one side of the <u>diaphragm</u> (stage II)

* Advanced disease (stage III + IV): nodes on both sides of the <u>diaphragm</u> or supradiaphragmatic nodes with splenic involvement (stage III), or diffuse or disseminated disease (stage IV)

* See “<u>Lugano classification of lymphomas</u>” for more information.

* Previously, a version of the <u>Cotswolds-modified Ann Arbor system</u> was used, excluding the presence of <u>B symptoms</u>.

## Treatment

Most patients with newly diagnosed NHL require <u>chemotherapy</u>, <u>radiotherapy</u>, or both. In some patients with indolent NHLs, such as <u>follicular lymphoma</u>, occasionally a <u>watch and wait</u> strategy can be used. Consultation with a hematologist-oncologist is essential for planning and initiating treatment.




### Approach [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FVXgwC)




* Perform <u>prechemotherapy screening</u>.

* Select treatment based on the subtype of NHL, staging, and prognosis 

* Most patients will receive treatment with systemic <u>chemotherapy</u> and/or <u>radiotherapy</u>.

* Low-grade NHL (initial stages): Consider <u>radiotherapy</u> with curative intent.

* Low-grade NHL (advanced stages): usually <u>palliative chemotherapy</u>

* High-grade NHL: usually <u>chemotherapy</u> with curative intent

* Select patients may benefit from additional interventions, including <u>splenectomy</u> and <u>hematopoietic stem cell transplantation</u> (<u>HSCT</u>).

* Provide supportive treatment: see also “<u>Principles of cancer care</u>.”

* Adequate hydration and nutrition

* Management of <u>chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting</u> and other side effects of <u>chemotherapy</u>

* <u>DVT prophylaxis</u>

* Consider prophylaxis for <u>tumor lysis syndrome</u>.  [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wUch2b0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Prechemotherapy screening</u> is essential in all patients receiving <u>chemotherapy</u> for NHL to adequately adjust chemotherapeutic doses according to patients' hepatic, renal, and cardiac function.




### Medical therapy




#### Treatment options [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FVXgwC)




* <u>Radiotherapy</u>

* May be curative or palliative

* Can be conventional or in form of radioimmunotherapy, in which engineered <u>monoclonal antibodies</u> that are specific for <u>tumor</u> <u>antigens</u> are bound to radioactive <u>nucleotides</u>, which results in higher doses of radiation reaching the target cancer cells and less off-target effects.

* Systemic <u>chemotherapy</u>: Regimens usually include combinations of <u>chemotherapeutic agents</u>, <u>steroids</u>, and immunotherapy.

* <u>Antifolates</u>: high-dose <u>methotrexate</u> ; (in combination with <u>leucovorin</u>) for <u>primary CNS lymphoma</u>

* <u>Alkylating agents</u>: e.g., <u>cyclophosphamide</u> (C)

* <u>Topoisomerase II inhibitors</u>: e.g., <u>etoposide</u> (E), <u>doxorubicin</u>/hydroxydaunorubicin (H)

* Alkaloids: e.g., <u>vincristine</u>/oncovin (V/O)

* <u>Steroids</u>: e.g., <u>prednisolone</u> (P), <u>dexamethasone</u>

* Immunotherapy: e.g., <u>rituximab</u> (R)

* <u>Intrathecal chemotherapy</u>: Intrathecal <u>methotrexate</u> can be considered for <u>leptomeningeal</u> involvement.

## Prognosis

* Typically, the prognosis of NHL is worse than that of <u>Hodgkin lymphoma</u>.  [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JbXsu9)

* Low-grade <u>lymphomas</u>: median survival of 6–10 years

* High-grade <u>lymphomas</u>: survival typically several months (years in less aggressive variants)

* Indicators of poor prognosis: old age, number of involved nodal and extranodal sites, ↑ <u>LDH</u>, ↑ <u>beta2 microglobulin</u> [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/M0XMg9)
