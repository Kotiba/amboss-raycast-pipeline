## Summary

<u>Respiratory failure</u> is the acute or chronic inability of the respiratory system to maintain <u>gas exchange</u> (<u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u> < 60 mm Hg, <u>PaCO<sub>2</sub></u> > 50 mm Hg). Causes can be extrapulmonary (e.g., <u>CNS depression</u> due to narcotic overdose) as well as pulmonary (e.g., <u>acute exacerbation of COPD</u>). <u>Respiratory failure</u> can be classified as <u>hypoxemic</u> (type 1) or <u>hypercapnic</u> (type 2). <u>Clinical features of hypoxemia</u> include <u>respiratory distress</u>, <u>cyanosis</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, and <u>altered mental status</u>. <u>Clinical features of hypercapnia</u> include <u>hypoventilation</u>, <u>headache</u>, warm extremities, and <u>asterixis</u>. Diagnostics include <u>arterial blood gas analysis</u> and possibly chest imaging to detect the underlying disease. Treatment includes supportive measures (<u>oxygen delivery</u> and/or ventilator support) and treatment of the underlying condition. Complications may arise due to prolonged <u>hypoxemia</u> and can affect various organs (e.g., renal/<u>heart failure</u>, <u>brain damage</u>).




For children, see "<u>Respiratory distress and failure in children</u>."

## Definitions

* Respiratory failure: the acute or chronic inability of the respiratory system to maintain adequate <u>gas exchange</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mhcVeX0)

* <u>Hypoxemic respiratory failure</u>: defined as a < 60 mm Hg (< 8 kPa)

* <u>Hypercapnic respiratory failure</u>: defined as a > 50 mm Hg (> 6.7 kPa)

* <u>Hypoxemic</u> and <u>hypercapnic respiratory failure</u> can occur together.

* Respiratory arrest: the complete cessation of breathing in patients with a <u>pulse</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kqXmA_)

* Respiratory distress: A clinical syndrome associated with breathing disorders (See “<u>Clinical features of respiratory distress</u>.”)

> [!TIP]

### By duration [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)




* Acute <u>respiratory failure</u>: develops over minutes to hours as a result of an acute illness or insult

* Chronic respiratory failure: longstanding <u>respiratory failure</u> resulting from chronic illness (e.g., <u>COPD</u>, <u>ILD</u>, <u>obesity hypoventilation syndrome</u>)





> [!TIP]
> In patients with <u>hypercapnia</u>, a normal <u>pH</u> suggests chronic <u>CO<sub>2</sub> retention</u> while a low <u>pH</u> raises concern for acute or acute-on-chronic <u>CO<sub>2</sub> retention</u>, which requires immediate intervention (see also “<u>Respiratory acidosis</u>.”)

## Etiology

This section lists <u>causes of respiratory failure</u> and <u>respiratory arrest</u> by type and mechanism. See “<u>Etiology of dyspnea</u>” for a system-based approach.




> [!TIP]
> <u>Respiratory failure</u> and <u>respiratory arrest</u> share the same causes and patients with <u>respiratory failure</u> can quickly decompensate into <u>respiratory arrest</u>.




### Causes of hypoxemia [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tk16MS0)




* Impaired alveolar <u>diffusion</u> (e.g., due to <u>pulmonary edema</u>, severe <u>pneumonia</u>, pulmonary hemorrhage , <u>idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis</u>)

* <u>Right-to-left shunt</u>

* Pulmonary <u>right-to-left shunt</u> (e.g., due to <u>ARDS</u>, <u>pulmonary contusions</u>/hemorrhage, <u>lung</u> collapse, <u>tension pneumothorax</u>)

* Intracardiac <u>right-to-left shunt</u> (e.g., due to <u>Eisenmenger syndrome</u>)

* <u>V/Q mismatch</u> (e.g., due to severe <u>pneumonia</u>, <u>pulmonary edema</u>, <u>pulmonary embolism</u>, <u>atelectasis</u>)

* Decreased <u>FiO<sub>2</sub></u> (e.g., due to <u>asphyxiant gas</u> exposure, <u>high altitude illness</u>)

* <u>Hypoventilation</u> (see “<u>Causes of hypercapnia</u>”)  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tk16MS0)





### Causes of hypercapnia [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tk16MS0)




<u>Hypercapnia</u> may be caused by decreased ventilation (<u>hypoventilation</u>) resulting from any of the following:




* <u>Airway obstruction</u> and/or increased <u>physiologic dead space</u> due to:

* <u>Lung</u> diseases: e.g., <u>AECOPD</u>, <u>acute asthma exacerbation</u>, <u>bronchiolitis</u>, <u>aspiration</u>, <u>bronchiectasis</u>, <u>cystic fibrosis</u>

* <u>Upper airway</u> obstruction: e.g., <u>laryngospasm</u>, <u>angioedema</u>, <u>anaphylaxis</u>, tumors, <u>croup</u>, <u>epiglottitis</u>, <u>diphtheria</u>, <u>drowning</u>, <u>OSA</u>

* <u>CNS depression</u> (e.g., due to <u>opioid intoxication</u>, <u>benzodiazepine</u> intoxication, <u>barbiturate intoxication</u>, <u>TBI</u>, <u>cerebral herniation</u>, <u>stroke</u>, <u>inhalant-related disorders</u>)

* <u>Respiratory muscle weakness</u> (e.g., due to <u>myasthenia gravis</u>, <u>Guillain-Barré syndrome</u>, <u>myopathies</u>, <u>ALS</u>, high cervical <u>spinal cord injury</u>, <u>poliomyelitis</u>)

* Decreased <u>chest wall compliance</u> (e.g., due to <u>rib fractures</u>, <u>tetanus</u>, <u>seizures</u>, <u>fibrothorax</u>; , <u>obesity hypoventilation syndrome</u>, <u>circumferential burns</u>)

* <u>Electrolyte</u> disturbances (e.g., <u>anorexia nervosa</u>)





> [!TIP]
> Increased O<sub>2</sub> consumption and/or CO<sub>2</sub> production (e.g., due to severe <u>sepsis</u>, <u>toxic shock syndrome</u>, <u>cardiogenic shock</u>, <u>multiorgan dysfunction</u>) may contribute to <u>respiratory failure</u>.

## Clinical features

### By affected system




* Respiratory: clinical features of respiratory distress

* <u>Dyspnea</u>

* Increased <u>respiratory drive</u>: <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>hyperventilation</u>

* Increased work of breathing (increased respiratory effort)

* <u>Nasal flaring</u>

* <u>Grunting</u>

* Pursed <u>lips</u>

* <u>Respiratory accessory muscle</u> use

* Intercostal, subcostal, and/or suprasternal <u>retractions</u>

* <u>Tripod positioning</u>

* <u>Paradoxical breathing</u>

* Other <u>clinical features of hypoxemia</u>: <u>agitation</u>, <u>cyanosis</u>

* Inability to lie flat

* <u>Airway</u>: <u>clinical features of airway compromise</u>

* <u>CNS</u>: altered <u>respiratory drive</u>





### By underlying process




* Clinical features of hypoxemia

* <u>Tachypnea</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>

* <u>Cyanosis</u>

* <u>Pleuritic chest pain</u>

* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>arrhythmia</u>

* <u>Altered mental status</u>: e.g., confusion, <u>somnolence</u>, <u>agitation</u>

* Clinical features of hypercapnia

* <u>Hypopnea</u>

* <u>Headache</u>, daytime sleepiness

* <u>Anxiety</u>

* Warm extremities

* <u>Papilledema</u>

* <u>Asterixis</u>

* <u>Coma</u>

* <u>Paralytic ileus</u>

* Clinical features of the underlying condition

* <u>Fever</u>, e.g., due to <u>sepsis</u>, <u>pneumonia</u>

* <u>Cough</u>, e.g., due to <u>pneumonia</u>, <u>COPD</u>

* <u>Chest pain</u>, e.g., due to <u>pneumonia</u>, <u>pulmonary embolism</u>

* Signs of general <u>muscle weakness</u>, e.g., due to <u>myasthenia gravis</u>, <u>Guillain-Barré syndrome</u>, <u>myopathies</u>, <u>ALS</u>

* <u>Pain</u> on inspiration, e.g., due to <u>rib fracture</u>

* <u>CNS depression</u>, e.g., due to <u>opioid</u> use





> [!WARNING]
> Recognize signs of imminent or ongoing <u>respiratory arrest</u> (e.g., <u>gasping</u>, <u>inspiratory stridor</u>, decreased <u>respiratory rate</u>, <u>cyanosis</u>, absent chest rise, or acute oxygen desaturation) and treat it immediately if present.

## Initial management

This section outlines the management of acute <u>respiratory failure</u>. For the management of <u>chronic respiratory failure</u>, see articles on specific causes, e.g., “<u>COPD</u>” and “<u>Interstitial lung disease</u>.”




### Approach [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* Use <u>ABCDE approach</u> including <u>IV access</u>, <u>pulse oximetry</u>, and <u>cardiac monitoring</u>.

* Provide immediate <u>respiratory support</u> tailored to the underlying cause and severity of <u>respiratory failure</u>.

* Ascertain <u>code status</u> early if feasible.

* Identify and treat <u>rapidly reversible causes of respiratory failure</u>.

* Perform focused clinical evaluation.

* Obtain initial diagnostics: e.g., <u>ABG</u>, routine <u>laboratory studies</u>, <u>ECG</u>, <u>CXR</u>, <u>POCUS</u>

* Consider advanced diagnostic testing based on initial findings.

* <u>Treat the underlying cause of respiratory failure</u>.





### <u>Respiratory support</u>




* <u>Airway management</u>: <u>Secure the airway</u> if <u>clinical features of airway compromise</u> are present.

* <u>Rescue breaths</u>: indicated immediately for patients in <u>respiratory arrest</u>

* <u>Oxygen therapy</u>

* Indicated for <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> < 95%

* Consider <u>HFNC</u> for <u>hypoxemic respiratory failure</u>.

* <u>Mechanical ventilation</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)

* Consider <u>indications for NIPPV</u> and <u>indications for invasive mechanical ventilation</u>.

* Choose an appropriate <u>ventilation strategy</u>.

* Adjust <u>mechanical ventilator parameters</u> as needed to correct <u>hypercapnia</u> and/or <u>hypoxemia</u>.

* ↑ Respiratory rate and/or ↑ <u>tidal volume</u> → ↑ ventilation

* ↑ <u>FiO<sub>2</sub></u> and/or ↑ <u>PEEP</u> → ↑ oxygenation

* See “<u>Improving ventilation in mechanically ventilated patients</u>” and “<u>Improving oxygenation in mechanically ventilated patients</u>” for further details.





### Treatment of rapidly reversible causes of respiratory failure




* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u>: <u>emergency chest decompression</u> and <u>chest tube placement</u>

* <u>Opioid-induced respiratory failure</u>: <u>naloxone</u>

* <u>Flash pulmonary edema</u>: <u>NIPPV</u>, <u>vasodilators for AHF</u>

* <u>Foreign body aspiration</u>: <u>maneuvers to dislodge an aspirated FB</u>, <u>emergency airway procedures for FBA</u>

* <u>Anaphylaxis</u>: <u>IM epinephrine</u>

* <u>Angioedema</u>: <u>C1 esterase inhibitor</u>

* <u>Status asthmaticus</u>: <u>bronchodilators</u>, <u>corticosteroids</u>, <u>magnesium</u>

## Diagnosis

<u>Respiratory failure</u> and <u>respiratory arrest</u> are <u>clinical diagnoses</u>. <u>ABG analysis</u>, rapid <u>laboratory studies</u>, and bedside imaging can help identify the type of <u>respiratory failure</u> and guide treatment of the underlying cause.




> [!WARNING]
> <u>Respiratory failure</u> and arrest require immediate management, i.e., prior to diagnostic confirmation.




### Initial investigations [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* <u>ABG</u>: for diagnostic confirmation 

* <u>Alveolar-arterial gradient</u> (<u>A-a gradient</u>): ([P<sub>atm</sub> - 47] × <u>FiO<sub>2</sub></u> - [<u>PaCO<sub>2</sub></u>/0.8]) - <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>

* Normal <u>A-a gradient</u>: <u>hypoventilation</u> or ↓ oxygen uptake

* ↑ <u>A-a gradient</u>: <u>V/Q mismatch</u> or shunting

* ↑ CO<sub>2</sub> and ↓ <u>pH</u>: acute <u>hypercapnia</u> (see “<u>Initial blood gas analysis</u>”)

* <u>CBC</u>: to assess for <u>anemia</u>

* <u>BMP</u>: to assess for metabolic and <u>electrolyte</u> derangements

* <u>ECG</u>

* Consider the following based on clinical suspicion:

* <u>BNP</u>: to assess for <u>acute heart failure</u>

* <u>Troponin</u>: to assess for <u>myocardial infarction</u>

* <u>D-dimer</u>: to assess for <u>pulmonary embolism</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>ABG analysis</u> is key to diagnosing and classifying <u>respiratory failure</u>.




### Bedside imaging [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* <u>CXR</u>: to assess for <u>chest wall</u>, pleural, and/or <u>lung</u> lesions (e.g., trauma, <u>ARDS</u>, <u>pneumonia</u>, <u>pneumothorax</u>, <u>atelectasis</u>, <u>pleural effusion</u>)

* <u>POCUS</u>: to assess <u>lung</u>, <u>pleura</u>, <u>heart</u>, <u>pericardium</u>, and <u>deep veins</u> (See “<u>Lung POCUS</u>” and “<u>Focused cardiac ultrasound</u>.”)





> [!TIP]
> <u>POCUS</u> may help to quickly identify <u>pneumothorax</u>, <u>pulmonary edema</u>, <u>pleural effusions</u>, <u>heart failure</u>, <u>cardiac tamponade</u>, or the presence of <u>DVT</u> and/or signs of acute right <u>heart</u> strain suggesting <u>pulmonary embolism</u>.




### Advanced diagnostic testing [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* CT chest: for detailed pulmonary evaluation (e.g., trauma, <u>ARDS</u>, <u>tumor</u>, <u>pleural effusion</u>, <u>pneumonia</u>)

* <u>CTA</u> chest: for suspected <u>pulmonary embolism</u>

* <u>Echocardiography</u>: for suspected <u>heart failure</u>, <u>cardiac tamponade</u>, or signs of right <u>heart</u> strain suggesting <u>pulmonary embolism</u> or <u>pulmonary hypertension</u>

* CT head: for suspected <u>head trauma</u> or <u>stroke</u>

* <u>CO-oximetry</u>: to assess for <u>carbon monoxide toxicity</u> or <u>methemoglobinemia</u>

## Complications

* Pulmonary: e.g., irreversible <u>lung</u> scarring after <u>pulmonary embolism</u> or <u>pneumonia</u>, ventilator dependence

* Cardiac: : e.g., <u>arrhythmias</u>, <u>heart failure</u>, <u>cardiac arrest</u>

* Neurological: : e.g., <u>hypoxic</u> <u>brain injury</u>, irreversible <u>brain damage</u>, <u>brain death</u>

* Renal: e.g., <u>acute renal failure</u>

* Gastrointestinal: e.g., <u>stress ulcer</u>, <u>ileus</u>

* Nutritional: e.g., <u>hypoglycemia</u>, <u>electrolyte</u> disturbances

* Other: <u>hypoxic</u> damage to other organs




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
