# Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Pulmonology > Obstructive lung diseases > Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3F0S33)

---

## Summary

Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) is the acute constriction of the lower <u>airways</u> that manifests during physical exertion. Although EIB most commonly affects individuals with <u>asthma</u>, it can also manifest in those without a history of <u>asthma</u>. The typical presentation includes <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>wheezing</u>, <u>cough</u>, and chest tightness that begins within 15 minutes of initiating intense exercise and resolves within an hour. The initial diagnostic workup comprises of <u>spirometry</u> with bronchial <u>reversibility testing</u>, and depending on results, may be followed by additional studies (e.g., <u>bronchial provocation studies</u>, evaluation of differential diagnoses) or the initiation of treatment. The first step in management is nonpharmacological therapy (e.g., pre-exercise warm-up, reduction of environmental triggers) for all patients and therapeutic optimization in patients with <u>asthma</u>. First-line pharmacological therapy consists of inhaled <u>beta-agonists</u> 15–30 minutes before exercise; usage should be limited to no more than 3 times a week to prevent <u>tachyphylaxis</u>. Patients with refractory symptoms or those who require inhaled <u>beta-agonists</u> ≥ 4 times a week should be started on daily pharmacological therapy (e.g., with <u>inhaled corticosteroids</u> or <u>montelukast</u>); referral to a pulmonologist may be considered.

## Clinical features

* Symptoms typically start within 15 minutes of initiation of intense exercise, and resolve within 60 minutes. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* Common symptoms include: [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* <u>Dyspnea</u>

* <u>Wheezing</u>

* <u>Cough</u>

* Chest tightness (or <u>chest pain</u>, in children)

* Increased mucus production

* Reduced exercise tolerance





> [!TIP]
> EIB typically develops within 15 minutes of exercise and may continue to worsen despite cessation of exercise; if <u>SOB</u> rapidly resolves with cessation of physical activity, consider <u>differential diagnoses of EIB</u>, e.g., <u>deconditioning</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D611M30)

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)




* Perform baseline <u>spirometry</u> with bronchial <u>reversibility testing</u>.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3B1S0j0)

* If <u>spirometry</u> is normal but EIB is still suspected, perform a <u>bronchial provocation test</u>.

* Positive provocation test: EIB confirmed; start treatment.

* Negative <u>bronchial provocation test</u>: Workup for <u>differential diagnoses of EIB</u>.





### <u>Spirometry</u> with <u>reversibility testing</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

### <u>Bronchial provocation tests</u>




* Exercise challenge test [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* Measure <u>spirometry</u> at baseline.

* Patients exercise to achieve 85–95% of their estimated <u>maximum heart rate</u> for approximately 6 minutes.

* During the challenge, patients breathe dry air (< 10 mg H2O/L) through the mouth.

* <u>Spirometry</u> is performed at 5, 10, 15, and 30 minutes after exercise.

* <u>Airway obstruction</u> is present if an <u>FEV<sub>1</sub></u> drop of ≥ 10% is recorded in 2 consecutive measurements.

* <u>Surrogate</u> <u>bronchial provocation tests</u>: used as an alternative to exercise to assess bronchial hyperresponsiveness [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* <u>Eucapnic voluntary hyperpnea</u> (<u>EVH</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* Positive if there is ≥ 10% drop in <u>FEV<sub>1</sub></u>

* Preferred in professional athletes but not widely available

* <u>Histamine</u> or <u>methacholine challenge test</u>: positive if ≥ 20% drop in <u>FEV<sub>1</sub></u>

* Osmotic agents (<u>mannitol</u>, <u>hypertonic saline</u>): positive if there is ≥ 15% drop in <u>FEV<sub>1</sub></u>





> [!TIP]
> Exercise challenge tests can also be performed in the field using portable <u>spirometry</u>; this may be helpful for individuals whose EIB is triggered by particular sports or weather conditions. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

## Treatment

### Approach [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)




* Advise all patients on nonpharmacological therapy.

* Patients with known <u>asthma</u>: optimize <u>asthma treatment</u>, including inhaler technique and <u>medication adherence</u>.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D611M30)

* Patients with no history of <u>asthma</u>: Start an inhaled <u>beta-agonist</u> to be used before exercise.

* Reevaluate patients regularly.

* Patients with refractory symptoms may benefit from daily therapy.

* Consider referral to a pulmonologist for patients with atypical or refractory symptoms.





### Nonpharmacological therapy [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)




* Pre-exercise warm-up and breathing exercises. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* Warm-up should be for 10–15 minutes at 50–80% of the <u>maximum heart rate</u>.

* May create a <u>refractory period</u> for up to 3 hours in which symptoms are not experienced  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* Reduction of environmental triggers, e.g.: [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* Cold weather

* Advise patients to exercise indoors during the winter months.

* For outdoor exercise, patients should cover the mouth with a mask or scarf and/or breathe through the nose.

* <u>Allergens</u>

* Avoid exercising near high-traffic roads or during peak travel hours.

* Train indoors if the pollen count is high; avoid exercising outdoors early in the morning and late in the day, and shower immediately afterward.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* Patients who react to <u>chlorine</u> should avoid indoor pools and consider swimming in water disinfected using other methods.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)

* Recommend <u>smoking cessation</u>.  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NB1-aj0)

* There is some weak evidence to support a <u>low sodium diet</u>, and fish oil and <u>ascorbic acid</u> supplementation. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)





### Pharmacological therapy [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D611M30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)




#### First-line: <u>beta-agonist</u> therapy




* Options (see also “<u>Antiasthmatic medications</u>”)  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D611M30)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)

* <u>SABA</u>, e.g., <u>albuterol</u> DOSAGE

* <u>LABA</u>, e.g., <u>salmeterol</u> DOSAGE

* Patients with known <u>asthma</u>: <u>inhaled corticosteroid</u>/<u>LABA</u> combination, e.g., <u>budesonide</u>/<u>formoterol</u> (<u>off label</u>) DOSAGE

* Use: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* 10–15 minutes prior to exercise

* Maximum ≤ 3 times/week  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l61vO30)

* If response is insufficient and/or <u>beta-agonist</u> is needed ≥ 4 times/week, manage as refractory EIB.





#### Refractory symptoms




* Consider the addition of any of the following: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* Daily <u>inhaled corticosteroid</u>, especially in patients with <u>asthma</u> (for recommendations and dosages see “<u>Antiasthmatic medications</u>”) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D611M30)

* <u>LTRA</u>, e.g., <u>montelukast</u> DOSAGE daily or 2 hours pre-exercise  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)

* Specialists may suggest <u>anticholinergic agents</u>, <u>mast cell stabilizers</u>, or <u>methylxanthines</u>, but their <u>efficacy</u> in EIB is unclear.





> [!TIP]
> Elite athletes require both specific <u>confirmatory testing</u> and therapeutic use exemptions for some medications in order to compete; check with the relevant sporting bodies (e.g., World Anti-<u>Doping</u> Agency) before prescribing medications. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J61sN30)




> [!WARNING]
> Patients should not use an inhaled <u>SABA</u> or <u>LABA</u> ≥ 4 times/week, as chronic use can result in tolerance. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/O61IO30)
