# Hypermagnesemia

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Nephrology > Fluids, electrolytes, and acid-base disorders > Hypermagnesemia*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FG0ga3)

## Summary

Hypermagnesemia is an <u>electrolyte</u> disorder in which serum <u>magnesium</u> levels are above the <u>reference range</u>. The most common cause is <u>renal insufficiency</u> in combination with increased <u>magnesium</u> intake (e.g., from <u>laxatives</u>). Other causes include <u>magnesium</u> therapy, <u>hypothyroidism</u>, and <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>. Mild hypermagnesemia is often asymptomatic but higher <u>magnesium</u> levels (e.g., above 4–5 mg/dL) can cause neuromuscular, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular symptoms, including <u>ECG</u> abnormalities. In very severe cases, hypermagnesemia can lead to <u>cardiac arrest</u> and <u>death</u>. Concomitant <u>electrolyte</u> imbalances (e.g., <u>hypocalcemia</u>) should be assessed, as they may worsen symptoms. The most important treatment step is the discontinuation of <u>magnesium</u> intake, which is sufficient for most asymptomatic or stable patients. Symptomatic patients may be treated with IV isotonic fluids, IV <u>calcium</u>, and <u>loop diuretics</u>. <u>Acute dialysis</u> should be considered in severe cases.

## Etiology

* Decreased <u>magnesium</u> elimination [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)

* <u>Renal insufficiency</u>;  (e.g., resulting from severe <u>hypovolemia</u>): most common cause

* <u>Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia</u>

* <u>Adrenal insufficiency</u>, e.g., <u>Addison disease</u>

* <u>Hypothyroidism</u>

* Increased <u>magnesium</u> intake ;  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* <u>Magnesium</u> therapy, e.g., for <u>eclampsia</u>, <u>premature labor</u>, <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>

* Overdose of drugs containing <u>magnesium</u>, e.g., <u>laxatives</u> including <u>magnesium hydroxide</u>, <u>antacids</u>

* Oversupplementation

* Increased tissue breakdown [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u>

* Trauma, e.g., <u>burns</u>, <u>surgery</u>

* <u>Sepsis</u>, <u>shock</u>, <u>cardiac arrest</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Renal insufficiency</u> in combination with increased <u>magnesium</u> intake (e.g., from <u>laxatives</u>) is the most common <u>cause of hypermagnesemia</u>.

## Clinical features

Mild hypermagnesemia is often asymptomatic; symptoms typically occur if <u>magnesium</u> levels are above 4–5 mg/dL. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F-cg_U0)




* Neuromuscular

* Reduced <u>deep tendon reflexes</u>

* <u>Lethargy</u>

* <u>Somnolence</u>

* Blurry <u>vision</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1r12TR0)

* Muscle <u>paralysis</u> (flaccid <u>quadriplegia</u>)

* <u>Respiratory failure</u>

* Gastrointestinal

* <u>Nausea</u>

* <u>Vomiting</u>

* Cardiovascular 

* <u>Hypotension</u>

* <u>Bradycardia</u>

* <u>Cardiac arrest</u>

* <u>ECG</u> changes: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sCdttH0)

* Peaking <u>T waves</u> (moderate hypermagnesemia) or <u>T wave flattening</u> (severe hypermagnesemia)

* Prolonged <u>PR interval</u>

* Prolonged QRS interval

* <u>Prolonged QT interval</u>





> [!TIP]
> Because most of the total <u>magnesium</u> in the body is located intracellularly, serum <u>magnesium</u> levels may not accurately reflect total body <u>magnesium</u> levels, and symptoms may not correlate with specific serum levels. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xr19fR0)




> [!WARNING]
> Very high <u>magnesium</u> levels (e.g., ≥ 10 mg/dL) may lead to <u>respiratory failure</u>, <u>heart block</u>, and/or <u>cardiac arrest</u>! [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

## Diagnosis

### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xr19fR0)




* Serum <u>electrolytes</u>

* ↑ <u>Magnesium</u> (may not reflect total body levels)  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/br1HfR0)

* Associated abnormalities

* ↓ <u>Calcium</u> (usually mild and asymptomatic)

* ↑ <u>Potassium</u> (less common)

* <u>Renal function tests</u>: often show findings of renal impairment, e.g., ↑ <u>creatinine</u>, ↓ <u>estimated GFR</u>

* <u>Coagulation studies</u>: may show decreased clotting time

* Further studies: Consider depending on the suspected <u>cause of hypermagnesemia</u> (e.g., <u>endocrine testing for adrenal insufficiency</u>).





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Hypocalcemia</u> may worsen <u>symptoms of hypermagnesemia</u> because <u>calcium</u> usually antagonizes the effects of <u>magnesium</u>. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/br1HfR0)




### <u>ECG</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




<u>ECG</u> changes are usually seen if <u>magnesium</u> levels are > 7 mg/dL. Findings are nonspecific and may include: [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xr19fR0)




* <u>Bradycardia</u>

* ↑ <u>PR interval</u> and <u>AV block</u>

* ↑ QRS duration

* ↑ <u>QT interval</u>

## Treatment

Stable and asymptomatic patients with normal renal function usually do not require medical therapy.




### All patients [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xr19fR0)




* Discontinue <u>magnesium</u>-containing medication and any other <u>magnesium</u> intake.

* Identify and treat the underlying <u>cause of hypermagnesemia</u>, e.g.:

* <u>Management of AKI</u>

* <u>Management of CKD</u>

* <u>Management of hypothyroidism</u>





### Significant and/or symptomatic hypermagnesemia  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xr19fR0)




* Supportive treatment

* Initiate <u>continuous cardiac monitoring</u>.

* Administer IV <u>isotonic crystalloids</u> (e.g., <u>normal saline</u>);  to dilute extracellular <u>magnesium</u>.

* Consult nephrology to evaluate the need for <u>acute dialysis</u> (especially in patients with <u>renal insufficiency</u>).

* Life-threatening hypermagnesemia (e.g., patients with <u>hypotension</u> or <u>respiratory failure</u>)

* Consider <u>calcium</u> administration as a temporizing measure until <u>acute dialysis</u> can be arranged.

* Agents: Follow local protocols for dosing where possible.

* 10% <u>calcium gluconate</u> DOSAGE (<u>off-label</u>)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HCYKtr)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CSWqbO0)

* OR 10% <u>calcium</u> <u>chloride</u> DOSAGE (<u>off-label</u>)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HCYKtr)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CSWqbO0)

* For patients in <u>cardiac arrest</u>, follow <u>ACLS algorithm</u> and management in “<u>Reversible causes of cardiac arrest</u>.”

* Mildly symptomatic patients:  (with normal renal function): Consider <u>loop diuretics</u>, e.g., <u>furosemide</u>. DOSAGE (<u>off-label</u>) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* If <u>magnesium</u> intake was oral: Consider <u>magnesium</u>-free <u>laxatives</u> or enemas.





> [!WARNING]
> Prevention is key in hypermagnesemia. Caution should be used in all patients treated with <u>magnesium</u>-containing medication, especially those with <u>renal insufficiency</u>.
