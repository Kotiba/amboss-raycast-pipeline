# Refeeding syndrome

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Endocrinology > Disorders of glucose metabolism > Refeeding syndrome*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/n807M3)

---

## Summary

Refeeding syndrome is a life-threatening condition that can occur when severely <u>malnourished</u> individuals resume eating. This includes individuals with <u>protein-energy malnutrition</u>, <u>eating disorders</u>, chronic <u>alcohol</u> use, prolonged <u>fasting</u>, major surgeries, or critical illnesses. Refeeding in individuals who have physiologically adapted to <u>malnutrition</u> can cause severe <u>hypophosphatemia</u>, <u>hypokalemia</u>, and <u>hypomagnesemia</u>, and trigger <u>thiamine deficiency</u> syndrome. Clinical features include <u>weakness</u>, <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>, <u>respiratory distress</u>, confusion, <u>seizures</u>, and <u>edema</u>. Diagnosis is based on <u>electrolyte</u> levels and signs of organ dysfunction caused by metabolic derangements. Management is aimed at both treatment and prevention. Measures include gradual reintroduction of nutrition, <u>electrolyte</u> monitoring and repletion, and treatment of complications.

---

## Pathophysiology

Abrupt and/or excessive resumption of nutrition in severely <u>malnourished</u> patients can cause massive <u>insulin</u> release → increased displacement of <u>magnesium</u>, <u>potassium</u>, and <u>phosphate</u> (shift from extracellular to intracellular) → ↓ <u>phosphate</u>, ↓ <u>potassium</u>, ↓ <u>magnesium</u> (serum levels) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T616430)

## Clinical features

* Neurological: <u>seizures</u>; , <u>ataxia</u>, <u>encephalopathy</u>

* Cardiovascular: <u>arrhythmias</u> (e.g., <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>torsades de pointes</u>, <u>cardiac arrest</u>; ), <u>edema</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)

* Gastrointestinal: abdominal discomfort, <u>bloating</u> after meals  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)

* Musculoskeletal: <u>weakness</u>, <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>

## Management

Tailor prevention and treatment measures to patients in collaboration with a dietician. Follow local protocols if available.  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bm1HVh0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)




### Approach [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bm1HVh0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)




* Before <u>nutritional support</u> (e.g., feeding, dextrose-containing IV solutions) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)

* Consider hospital admission for stabilization, e.g., volume and <u>electrolyte repletion</u>.

* Consider starting IV <u>thiamine</u> DOSAGE to prevent <u>Wernicke encephalopathy</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)

* During <u>nutritional support</u>

* Use the oral or enteral route for feeding and <u>electrolyte repletion</u> as much as possible.

* <u>Calculate caloric intake for refeeding</u> based on patient age, weight, and risk profile.

* Treat any features of refeeding syndrome that may occur, e.g., <u>phosphate repletion</u>, <u>potassium repletion</u>, <u>magnesium repletion</u>, <u>thiamine deficiency</u>.

* Identify and treat complications, e.g., <u>volume overload</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>, <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> Avoid aggressive <u>IV fluid resuscitation</u> in patients at risk of refeeding syndrome to prevent <u>volume overload</u> and <u>third spacing</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bm1HVh0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)




### Monitoring [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)




* <u>Vital signs</u>

* <u>Input/output monitoring</u>

* <u>Volume status assessment</u>

* Daily weights

* <u>Changes in mental status</u>

* For patients at high risk (e.g., with severe <u>electrolyte</u> abnormalities, severe <u>malnutrition</u>):

* Obtain <u>ECG</u>.

* Consider <u>hemodynamic monitoring</u>.

* Measure serum <u>glucose</u> and <u>electrolytes</u> every 12 hours for the first 72 hours.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bm1HVh0)





### Caloric intake calculation for refeeding [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)




Adjust existing daily caloric goals (e.g., <u>nutritional goals for anorexia nervosa</u>) according to individual patient needs and risk.




* Adults

* Start with 100–150 g of dextrose OR 10–20 kcal/kg for the first 24 hours.

* Increase by 33% of the daily goal every 1–2 days.

* Children and <u>infants</u> (ages 28 days to 18 years)

* Aim to start caloric intake at 40–50% of the daily calorie goal.

* Start dextrose at a rate of 4–6 mg/kg/minute.

* Advance by 1–2 mg/kg/minute daily as guided by blood <u>glucose</u> levels to a maximum of 14–18 mg/kg/minute.





> [!TIP]
> Remember to include calories from maintenance IV dextrose and medications infused in <u>dextrose solutions</u> in daily caloric intake calculations.




### Management of <u>electrolyte</u> disturbances [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)




* <u>Replete serum electrolytes</u> as needed.

* Delay increasing calories until <u>electrolytes</u> are corrected.

* If <u>electrolytes</u> are not easily corrected or levels fall significantly:

* Reduce calories by 50%.

* Consider stopping <u>nutritional support</u> if an <u>electrolyte</u> disturbance becomes life-threatening.

* If <u>electrolytes</u> remain stable with dextrose infusion over several days, consider gradually increasing calories again.  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Hypophosphatemia</u> is the primary biochemical indicator of refeeding syndrome. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ym1nVh0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i_cJLU0)




### Other <u>nutritional support</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmWvfN0)




* Continue IV <u>thiamine</u> for at least 5–7 days in patients with clinical features of or <u>risk factors</u> for <u>thiamine deficiency</u> in consultation with a nutritionist.

* Provide daily multivitamins for 10 days.
