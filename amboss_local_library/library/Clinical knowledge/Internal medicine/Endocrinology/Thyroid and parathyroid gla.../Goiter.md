## Summary

Goiter is any abnormal enlargement of the <u>thyroid gland</u>. The condition has various causes, with the most common worldwide being <u>iodine deficiency</u>. In the US, however, Hashimoto's and <u>Graves' disease</u> are more common etiologies. Goiters can be classified based on their morphology, function, or dignity (benign or malignant). Symptoms depend on etiology and are often absent. However, patients may present with <u>hyperthyroidism</u> or <u>hypothyroidism</u>. Large goiters may also cause obstructive symptoms due to compression of the <u>trachea</u> and/or the <u>esophagus</u>. Diagnosis is established based on <u>clinical examination</u>, laboratory tests, and imaging techniques. Management depends on the underlying condition and may include administering <u>iodine</u> (for treating nontoxic <u>euthyroid goiter</u>) or performing <u>surgery</u> (e.g., for treating local compression or <u>thyroid cancers</u>).

## Etiology

* <u>Iodine deficiency</u> (leading cause of goiter worldwide)

* Inflammation (e.g., <u>Hashimoto thyroiditis</u>, <u>subacute granulomatous thyroiditis</u>)

* <u>Graves disease</u>

* <u>Thyroid cysts</u> (e.g., <u>thyroglossal cyst</u>)

* <u>Thyroid adenomas</u>

* <u>Thyroid carcinomas</u>

* Ingestion of goitrogens (e.g., <u>lithium</u> carbonate)

* Elevated <u>TSH</u> production (e.g., <u>pituitary adenoma</u>, <u>paraneoplastic syndrome</u>)

* Congenital goiter

## Classification

### <u>Thyroid gland</u> versus goiter




* Normal adult <u>thyroid gland</u> 

* Weight: ∼ 20–30 g

* Volume: ∼ 7–10 mL

* Location: <u>caudal</u> to <u>larynx</u> surrounding the anterolateral part of the <u>trachea</u>

* Goiter

* Enlarged volume of <u>thyroid gland</u>

* Goiters can be differentiated based on

* Morphology (growth pattern, size)

* <u>Thyroid</u> function

* Benignity/<u>malignancy</u>

### Morphology




* Growth pattern of goiter

* Diffuse goiter: diffusely enlarged <u>thyroid</u>

* <u>Graves disease</u>

* Inflammation (e.g., <u>Hashimoto thyroiditis</u>)

* <u>TSH</u>-secreting <u>pituitary adenoma</u>

* <u>Iodine deficiency</u>

* Nodular goiter: irregular enlarged <u>thyroid</u> due to <u>nodule</u> formation 

* Uninodular goiter (e.g., cysts, <u>adenoma</u>, cancer)

* Toxic and nontoxic multinodular goiter

* Goiter size: see ”<u>Classification of goiter by palpation</u>” below.





### <u>Thyroid</u> function of goiter




* Nontoxic goiter: normal <u>TSH</u>, fT<sub>3</sub>, and fT<sub>4</sub>levels
* E.g., <u>Iodine deficiency</u>

* Toxic goiter: increased <u>thyroid hormone production</u>
* E.g., <u>Graves disease</u>, <u>toxic multinodular goiter</u>

* Hypothyroid goiter: decreased <u>thyroid hormone production</u>
* E.g., <u>Hashimoto's disease</u>, congenital hypothyroid goiter





### Dignity of goiter




* Malignant goiter: e.g., <u>thyroid carcinoma</u>

* Benign (bland) goiter: benign <u>thyroid</u> enlargement

## Clinical features

* Patients are commonly asymptomatic.

* Altered <u>hormone</u> metabolism may occur

* Symptoms of <u>hyperthyroidism</u>

* Symptoms of <u>hypothyroidism</u>

* Obstructive symptoms

* Compression of the <u>trachea</u> → exertional <u>dyspnea</u> and, in severe cases, <u>stridor</u> or <u>wheezing</u>

* Compression of the <u>esophagus</u> → <u>dysphagia</u>

* Potentially:

* <u>Lymph node</u> enlargement (e.g., cervical <u>lymph nodes</u>) in malignant infiltration

* Pemberton sign: a <u>physical examination</u> finding in patients with goiter (bilateral arm elevation results in facial <u>plethora</u> due to an obstructed thoracic inlet that increases venous pressure)

## Diagnosis

* Best initial step: palpation of <u>thyroid gland</u> (see “<u>Classification of goiter by palpation</u>” below)

* Laboratory tests

* Initial workup: <u>TSH</u> levels

* Possible screen for <u>thyroid antibodies</u> (see <u>thyroid antibodies</u>)

* If medullary <u>carcinoma</u> is suspected, check <u>calcitonin</u> levels (see <u>thyroid cancer</u>).

* Imaging: determination of goiter size, nodularity, and consistency

* <u>Ultrasound</u>

* Further evaluation may include: CT, <u>MRI</u>

* Interventions

* Depend on the suspected pathology

* Fine-needle <u>biopsy</u> for <u>cytology</u>





### Classification of goiter by palpation




According to the <u>World Health Organization</u> (WHO) classification:




* Grade 0: no goiter is palpable or visible.

* Grade 1: palpable goiter, not visible when neck is held in normal position

* Grade 2: a clearly swollen neck (also visible in normal position of the neck) that is consistent with a goiter on palpation
