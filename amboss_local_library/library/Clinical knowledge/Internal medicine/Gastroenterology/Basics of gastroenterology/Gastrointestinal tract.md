## Summary

The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) is the system of organs that allows for the consumption and digestion of food, absorption of <u>nutrients</u>, and excretion of waste in the form of fecal matter. It includes the <u>oral cavity</u>, <u>esophagus</u>, <u>stomach</u>, <u>small intestine</u>, and <u>large intestine</u>. It is derived from the <u>primitive gut tube</u> and can be divided into the <u>foregut</u>, <u>midgut</u>, and <u>hindgut</u>, each of which is distinct in its embryological development and neurovascular supply. The <u>veins</u> of all three embryological segments drain directly or indirectly into the <u>portal vein</u>, which is connected to the caval venous system via a system of <u>portocaval shunts</u>. The GIT is innervated by the <u>sympathetic nervous system</u>, <u>parasympathetic nervous system</u>, and <u>enteric nervous system</u>, the last of which is unique to the GIT. All organs of the GIT are composed of four histological layers: <u>mucosa</u>, <u>submucosa</u>, muscularis propria, and serosa (<u>intraperitoneal organs</u>) or adventitia (<u>extraperitoneal organs</u>). The <u>secretory and regulatory products of the gastrointestinal tract</u> vary from segment to segment depending on the regional histological characteristics, presence of specialized cells, and function.




This article provides an overview of the gastrointestinal tract as a whole. The <u>oral cavity</u>, <u>stomach</u>, <u>small intestine</u>, and <u>large intestine</u> are covered in detail in their own articles, as are the accessory organs such as the <u>liver</u>, <u>gall bladder</u>, and <u>pancreas</u>. The <u>peritoneum</u> and major <u>blood vessels</u> of the <u>abdominal cavity</u> are covered in the “<u>Abdominal cavity</u>” article.

### Function




* Ingestion of food and water

* <u>Mastication</u>

* Transport of the food bolus or <u>chyme</u> through the gastrointestinal tract

* Digestion (i.e., breaking food down into <u>nutrients</u>)

* Absorption of <u>nutrients</u> and water

* Excretion of waste as feces

* <u>Immunity</u>

### Arterial supply




* Most of the GIT is supplied by the <u>anterior</u> (unpaired) <u>branches of the abdominal aorta</u>. In contrast, non-GI organs in the <u>abdominal cavity</u> are supplied by the <u>posterior</u> and/or <u>lateral</u> (paired) <u>branches of the abdominal aorta</u>.

* Exceptions:

* <u>Esophagus</u>: supplied by the esophageal branches of the <u>inferior thyroid artery</u>, the <u>thoracic aorta</u>, and the <u>left gastric artery</u>

* <u>Distal</u> <u>anal canal</u>: supplied by the <u>middle rectal artery</u>  and the <u>inferior rectal artery</u>

* <u>Watershed zones</u>

* <u>Splenic flexure</u>: supplied by the branches of both superior and <u>inferior mesenteric arteries</u>

* Rectosigmoid junction: supplied by the <u>superior rectal artery</u> and last sigmoid branch of the <u>inferior mesenteric artery</u>

### Venous drainage




* The <u>veins</u> of most of the gastrointestinal tract drain directly or indirectly into the <u>portal vein</u>.

* Exceptions:

* <u>Esophagus</u>: drains into the inferior <u>thyroid</u> <u>vein</u>, the <u>azygos vein</u>, <u>hemiazygos vein</u>, and the <u>left gastric vein</u>

* For more information about the tributaries of each individual <u>vein</u> and description of <u>portocaval anastomoses</u> and <u>cavocaval anastomoses</u>, see the “<u>Abdominal cavity</u>” article.

## Innervation and gut motility

### Innervation of the gastrointestinal tract [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4mY3fp)




* The gastrointestinal tract is innervated by the <u>autonomic nervous system</u>.

* Extrinsic innervation: <u>parasympathetic</u> and <u>sympathetic nervous system</u>

* Intrinsic innervation: <u>enteric nervous system</u>

* Embryologically derived from the <u>neural crest</u>

* Regulated by the <u>autonomic nervous system</u> but can function independently

* Crucial for gastrointestinal motility and secretion

* For more information about the extrinsic and intrinsic innervation, see “Nerves of the <u>abdominal cavity</u>” and “<u>Enteric nervous system</u>” correspondingly.

## Embryology

* Primitive gut tube : Most of the gastrointestinal tract is derived from the <u>primitive gut tube</u>, which is a derivative of the <u>endodermal</u> lining of the <u>yolk sac</u>; ; :  following craniocaudal and <u>lateral</u> embryonic folds. (See “Morphogenesis” in the article on <u>embryogenesis</u>.) 

* 4<sup>th</sup> week of development: The three regions of the <u>primitive gut tube</u> become distinguishable (i.e., <u>foregut</u>, <u>midgut</u>, and <u>hindgut</u>), and each region has its own neurovascular supply.

* Most organs of the gastrointestinal tract are derived from these three regions. (See “<u>Foregut, midgut, and hindgut derivatives and corresponding neurovascular structures</u>” above.)

* Non-gut tube derivatives

* <u>Buccopharyngeal membrane</u>: <u>oral cavity</u>

* <u>Cloacal membrane</u>: the <u>anal canal</u> <u>distal</u> to the <u>pectinate line</u>

* <u>Dorsal</u> <u>mesentery</u>: <u>spleen</u>, <u>dorsal</u> <u>pancreatic</u> bud, the <u>gastrosplenic ligament</u>, <u>splenorenal ligament</u>, and the <u>greater omentum</u>

* <u>Ventral</u> <u>mesentery</u>: <u>liver</u>, <u>ventral</u> <u>pancreatic</u> bud, the <u>falciform ligament</u>, and the <u>lesser omentum</u>

* Somatic <u>mesoderm</u>: <u>parietal peritoneum</u>

* <u>Splanchnic</u> <u>mesoderm</u>: <u>visceral peritoneum</u>

* Germ <u>layers of the gastrointestinal tract</u>

* <u>Endoderm</u>  : <u>epithelium</u> and <u>mucosal</u> glands of the gastrointestinal tract and the <u>parenchyma</u> of the <u>liver</u> and <u>pancreas</u> (the splenic <u>parenchyma</u> is derived from <u>mesoderm</u>.)

* <u>Mesoderm</u>: <u>lamina propria</u>, <u>muscularis mucosa</u>, <u>submucosa</u>, and muscularis propria

* <u>Ectoderm</u> (<u>neural crest cells</u>): <u>enteric nervous system</u>

* Rotation of the foregut: 90° clockwise around its <u>longitudinal axis</u> (between the 4<sup>th</sup> and 6<sup>th</sup> week of development)

* The <u>anterior</u> border forms the <u>lesser curvature</u> and the <u>posterior</u> border forms the <u>greater curvature of the stomach</u>.

* The <u>liver</u> and <u>duodenum</u> move to the right, while the <u>spleen</u> and <u>pancreas</u> move to the left of the <u>abdominal cavity</u>.

* The left <u>vagus nerve</u> is then <u>anterior</u> to the <u>stomach</u>, while the right vagus is <u>posterior</u> to it.

* Rotation of the midgut: 270° counterclockwise around the <u>superior mesenteric artery</u> (SMA)  
1. * 4<sup>th</sup> week: <u>midgut</u> elongation in utero
2. * 6<sup>th</sup> week: physiological <u>herniation</u> of the <u>midgut</u> out of the umbilical ring;   → 90° counter-clockwise rotation
3. * 10<sup>th</sup> week: reentry of the <u>midgut</u> into the abdominal cavity → 180° rotation around the <u>superior mesenteric artery</u> inside the <u>abdominal cavity</u> (for a total of 270°)  → fixation of the <u>duodenojejunal flexure</u> and <u>cecum</u> to the <u>posterior</u> abdominal wall





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Omphalocele</u> is caused by a failure of the <u>midgut</u> to return to the <u>abdominal cavity</u> after its physiological <u>herniation</u> through the umbilical ring. Accordingly, the <u>amniotic membrane</u> and <u>peritoneum</u> form the hernial sac of the <u>omphalocele</u>.




> [!NOTE]
> <u>Gastroschisis</u> is caused by a developmental defect of the abdominal wall through which intestinal loops herniate out directly (i.e., the hernial sac is absent).




> [!NOTE]
> A <u>congenital umbilical hernia</u> is caused by a protrusion of intra-abdominal contents through a patent umbilical orifice. The <u>peritoneum</u> is the hernial sac of an <u>umbilical hernia</u>.
