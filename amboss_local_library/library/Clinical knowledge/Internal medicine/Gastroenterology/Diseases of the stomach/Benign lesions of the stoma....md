# Benign lesions of the stomach and small intestine

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Gastroenterology > Diseases of the stomach > Benign lesions of the stomach and small intestine*

## Summary

Benign lesions of the <u>stomach</u> and <u>small intestine</u> are diverse and include <u>epithelial</u> polyps and subepithelial lesions. These lesions are often discovered incidentally during endoscopy. Causes vary, with some polyps arising from chronic gastric <u>mucosal</u> injury due to conditions such as <u>Helicobacter pylori infection</u> or <u>autoimmune gastritis</u>, while others are associated with chronic <u>proton pump inhibitor</u> (<u>PPI</u>) therapy or <u>hereditary polyposis syndromes</u>. Most lesions are asymptomatic, but large growths can cause symptoms such as <u>gastrointestinal bleeding</u>, <u>anemia</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>, and <u>intestinal obstruction</u>. Diagnosis is primarily made via <u>esophagogastroduodenoscopy</u> (<u>EGD</u>), which allows for direct visualization; <u>endoscopic ultrasound</u> (<u>EUS</u>) is particularly useful for evaluating subepithelial lesions. <u>Biopsy</u> with <u>histopathology</u> is often required to confirm the diagnosis and differentiate between lesion types. Management is tailored to the specific lesion and may include conservative monitoring, eradication of <u>H. pylori</u>, or endoscopic or surgical resection of symptomatic, large, or premalignant lesions. While many lesions are benign, some, particularly gastric and <u>duodenal</u> <u>adenomas</u>, carry a significant risk of malignant transformation into <u>adenocarcinoma</u>.
