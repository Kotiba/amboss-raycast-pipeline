# Femoral hernia

## Summary

A femoral <u>hernia</u> is an uncommon type of <u>inguinal hernia</u>, in which intra-abdominal contents (e.g., <u>intraperitoneal</u> fat, <u>mesentery</u>, bowels) herniate into the <u>femoral canal</u> through the <u>femoral ring</u>. <u>Risk factors</u> for femoral <u>hernias</u> include old age, female sex, <u>obesity</u>, and previous <u>hernia</u> repair. Patients present with a globular swelling inferior to the <u>inguinal ligament</u> and <u>medial</u> to the <u>femoral vein</u> that worsens with <u>coughing</u> or straining. The diagnosis is primarily clinical with <u>ultrasonography</u> being indicated if the diagnosis is inconclusive. Although femoral <u>hernias</u> are uncommon, they are associated with a high risk of incarceration and strangulation and should, therefore, be surgically treated as early as possible. The preferred treatment for non-complicated femoral <u>hernias</u> is mesh <u>hernioplasty</u>, while repair without a mesh is used for complicated femoral <u>hernias</u>.

---

## Epidemiology

* Uncommon <u>hernia</u> (∼ 5% of all <u>hernias</u>)

* Sex: <u>♀</u> > <u>♂</u> (3:1)

* Peak <u>incidence</u>: 40–70 years  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F1agij)




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

---

## Etiology

* Advancing age and female sex (see “<u>Epidemiology</u>” above)

* Increased intra-abdominal pressure

* <u>Obesity</u>

* Chronic <u>constipation</u>

* <u>Chronic cough</u> (e.g., due to <u>COPD</u>)

* Straining during <u>micturition</u> (e.g., due to <u>prostatic</u> <u>hypertrophy</u>)

* <u>Multiparity</u>

* Previous abdominal surgeries (especially those involving the inguinal region)





> [!NOTE]
> In contrast to <u>indirect inguinal hernias</u>, which may occur congenitally, femoral <u>hernias</u> are almost always acquired.
> References:[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/t1aXij)

## Pathophysiology

* Boundaries of the <u>femoral canal</u>  

* <u>Anterior</u>: <u>inguinal ligament</u> (<u>Poupart's ligament</u>)

* <u>Posterior</u>: pubic ramus and <u>pectineal ligament</u>

* <u>Medial</u>: <u>lacunar ligament</u> (<u>Gimbernat ligament</u>)

* <u>Lateral</u>: <u>femoral vein</u>

* Typical femoral <u>hernia</u> (common): protrusion of <u>intraperitoneal</u> contents along with the transverse abdominal <u>fascia</u> through the <u>femoral ring</u> into the <u>femoral canal</u>

> To remember the <u>femoral triangle</u> contents, think LaMe NAVEL: (from <u>lateral</u> to <u>medial</u>) femoral Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space (femoral canal → femoral <u>hernia</u>), Lymphatics

## Clinical features

* Non-complicated femoral <u>hernia</u>

* A globular, subcutaneous swelling in the groin 

* Localization: inferior to the <u>inguinal ligament</u>, <u>lateral</u> to the <u>pubic tubercle</u>, and <u>medial</u> to the <u>femoral vein</u>

* Swelling enlarges with <u>coughing</u> (palpable <u>cough impulse</u>) or a <u>Valsalva maneuver</u>

* Possibly, non-specific, dragging <u>pain</u>

* Complicated femoral <u>hernia</u>: see “Complications” below





References:[[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F1agij)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/81aOij)

## Diagnosis

* A femoral <u>hernia</u> is primarily a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>.

* Groin <u>ultrasound</u> is used when the diagnosis is inconclusive (see “Differential diagnosis” below).





> [!WARNING]
> Femoral <u>hernias</u> can be difficult to palpate in <u>obese</u> patients. <u>Ultrasound</u> is indicated if a femoral <u>hernia</u> is suspected in this patient group!




References:[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F30g4f)

## Differential diagnoses

* Direct or indirect <u>inguinal hernias</u> (see “<u>Overview of hernias</u>”)

* Femoral pseudohernia

* <u>Lipoma</u>

* <u>Lymph node</u> enlargement

* <u>Femoral artery aneurysm</u>

* Saphenous varix: a dilated, saccular swelling of the <u>great saphenous vein</u> that lies just <u>distal</u> to the junction of the <u>femoral vein</u> and the <u>great saphenous vein</u>

* <u>Psoas abscess</u>





References:[[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F1agij)




The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

---

## Treatment

* Non-complicated femoral <u>hernia</u>: early elective surgical repair with mesh <u>hernioplasty</u> (tension-free repair)

* Complicated femoral <u>hernia</u>:  (see “Complications” below): <u>herniorrhaphy</u> (non-mesh repair)





> [!NOTE]
> All femoral <u>hernias</u> should be surgically repaired because of the high risk of complications.

## Complications

* Incarceration: irreducible femoral <u>hernia</u> due to trapped <u>hernia</u> contents in <u>hernia</u> sac
* Richter hernia: <u>herniation</u> of only a part of the circumference of the bowel wall.

* Strangulation: <u>ischemic necrosis</u> of contents within the <u>hernia</u> sac as blood flow is compromised due to incarceration

* Warm, tender, and <u>erythematous</u>/discolored swelling

* Features of <u>mechanical bowel obstruction</u>

* Possibly <u>fever</u>, <u>leukocytosis</u>, and <u>hemodynamic instability</u>

* Features of <u>paralytic ileus</u> supervene once <u>bowel perforation</u> and/or <u>peritonitis</u> develop





> [!WARNING]
> Reduction of a <u>strangulated hernia</u> should not be attempted because generalized <u>peritonitis</u> would occur following reduction of strangulated bowel loops!




> [!NOTE]
> Although femoral <u>hernias</u> account for only about 5% of all <u>hernias</u>, they account for about 40% of all complicated <u>hernias</u>!




> [!NOTE]
> A femoral <u>hernia</u> should be considered among 40–70-year-old women presenting with signs of <u>mechanical bowel obstruction</u>!
