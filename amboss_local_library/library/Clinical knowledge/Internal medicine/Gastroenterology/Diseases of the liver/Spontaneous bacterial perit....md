# Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Gastroenterology > Diseases of the liver > Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GM0BJg)

---

## Summary

Spontaneous bacterial <u>peritonitis</u> (<u>SBP</u>) is a bacterial infection of ascitic fluid that occurs in the absence of an identifiable intraabdominal source of infection. It is the most common bacterial infection and a leading cause of hospital admission and mortality among patients with <u>cirrhosis</u>. <u>Enteric</u> <u>gram-negative bacteria</u> (e.g., <u>E. coli</u>, <u>Klebsiella</u> spp.) have historically been the most common isolates; however, gram-positive, <u>fluoroquinolone</u>-resistant, and multidrug-resistant bacteria are increasingly common. <u>SBP</u> may manifest with <u>fever</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>, and/or <u>altered mental status</u>, but some patients are asymptomatic at presentation. Diagnosis is based on the finding of elevated ascitic fluid <u>neutrophil</u> count (≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup>) without an intraabdominal surgically-treatable source of infection. Timely <u>antibiotic</u> administration is the mainstay of therapy. <u>Empiric antibiotic</u> choice depends on the setting of infection (i.e., <u>community-acquired infection</u> vs. <u>healthcare-associated infection</u>), previous <u>antibiotic</u> exposure, and local bacterial susceptibility patterns. IV <u>albumin</u> supplementation is used as adjunctive therapy. Long-term <u>prophylactic antibiotic therapy</u> is recommended to prevent recurrent infection.

---

## Definitions

* Spontaneous bacterial <u>peritonitis</u>: : infection of the ascitic fluid in the absence of any focal intraabdominal, surgically treatable source of infection [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/X5b9i8)

* <u>Secondary bacterial peritonitis</u>: inflammation of the <u>peritoneum</u> caused by bacterial infection from a surgically treatable intraabdominal source [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)

---

## Epidemiology

* Most common bacterial infection in patients with <u>cirrhosis</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)

* Represents over 30% of bacterial infections among hospitalized patients with <u>cirrhosis</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wNbhW8)

* <u>Prevalence</u> among asymptomatic outpatients with <u>decompensated cirrhosis</u> is estimated to be up to 3.5% [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9NbNW8)




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

## Etiology

### <u>Risk factors</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)




<u>SBP</u> in adults occurs almost exclusively in patients with <u>cirrhosis</u> and <u>ascites</u>.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vNbA18)



* Additional <u>risk factors</u> in patients with <u>cirrhosis</u> include: [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)

* Low ascitic fluid protein concentration (< 1.5 g/dL)

* <u>Upper gastrointestinal bleeding</u>

* Prior episodes of <u>SBP</u>




### Pathophysiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KlbUBF)




* Bacterial translocation from the intestinal lumen to <u>mesenteric</u> <u>lymph nodes</u> →;  spread to systemic and portal circulation → <u>colonization</u> and subsequent infection of ascitic fluid

* Contributing factors related to underlying <u>portal hypertension</u> and <u>cirrhosis</u>:

* Intestinal dysmotility

* Bacterial overgrowth

* Altered intestinal permeability

* Systemic <u>immune dysfunction</u>





### Microbiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KlbUBF)




* Usually a monomicrobial infection; suspect <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> if multiple organisms are demonstrated on ascitic fluid <u>gram stain</u> or culture (see “<u>Secondary bacterial peritonitis</u>” in “Differential diagnoses” section).

* Gram-negative <u>enteric</u> bacteria (e.g., <u>Escherichia coli</u>, <u>Klebsiella</u> spp.) are most common.

* Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., <u>Streptococcus</u> spp., <u>Staphylococcus</u> spp., <u>Enterococcus</u> spp.) are increasing in <u>prevalence</u>.

* Increasing <u>prevalence</u> of <u>fluoroquinolone</u>-resistant and multidrug-resistant bacteria   [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6lbjBF)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/plbLBF)





> [!TIP]
> <u>SBP</u> is typically a monomicrobial bacterial infection. The presence of multiple organisms on ascitic fluid <u>gram-stain</u> or culture should raise suspicion for <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u>.

## Clinical features

Symptoms and signs of <u>SBP</u> may be subtle or absent.




* <u>Diffuse abdominal pain</u>/tenderness

* <u>Fever</u> and chills

* Worsening <u>ascites</u>

* New-onset or worsening <u>encephalopathy</u>

* <u>Jaundice</u>

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Constipation</u> or <u>diarrhea</u> (<u>peristaltic</u> signs sparse or absent in cases of <u>ileus</u>)

---

## Diagnosis

<u>SBP</u> is diagnosed when the ascitic fluid <u>neutrophil</u> count is ≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup>, with or without positive ascitic fluid bacterial cultures, and in the absence of another intraabdominal source of infection. <u>SBP</u> is often asymptomatic and a high index of suspicion is essential in any patient with <u>cirrhosis</u> and <u>ascites</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)




### Approach [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* Assess for and, if present, initiate <u>management of septic shock</u>.

* Obtain 2 sets of <u>blood cultures</u> before the administration of <u>antibiotics</u>.

* Perform <u>paracentesis</u> without delay  and before the administration of <u>antibiotics</u>. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PkbWLF)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jkb_nF)

* Rule out <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> (see the section “Differential diagnosis”) and other causes of abdominal <u>pain</u> (see “<u>Acute abdominal pain</u>”). [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OlbICF)





### <u>Laboratory studies</u>




* <u>Blood cultures</u> (2 sets)

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (e.g., <u>CRP</u>, <u>ESR</u>) may be elevated  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tlbXAF)

* <u>CBC</u>: potentially <u>leukocytosis</u>

* <u>Liver chemistries</u>: findings consistent with <u>cirrhosis</u> (see “Diagnostics” in “<u>Cirrhosis</u>”); evaluate for worsening <u>hepatic function</u>

* <u>Basic metabolic panel</u>: to evaluate for <u>acute kidney injury</u>, <u>hepatorenal syndrome</u>, <u>hyponatremia</u>

* <u>Coagulation panel</u>





### Peritoneal fluid analysis [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* Obtained via <u>diagnostic paracentesis</u>

* When indicated, perform this procedure as soon as possible (ideally before the administration of <u>antibiotics</u>).

* See “<u>Paracentesis</u>” for further details on indications, contraindications, steps, troubleshooting, and complications of this procedure.





#### Indications for diagnostic paracentesis in SBP




Patients with <u>cirrhosis</u> and <u>ascites</u> with any of the following: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* Clinical deterioration or hospital admission  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mkbVoF)

* Signs of infection (e.g., <u>fever</u>; , <u>hypothermia</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>shock</u>, <u>leukocytosis</u>, <u>acidosis</u>)

* Gastrointestinal signs or symptoms (e.g., abdominal <u>pain</u>/tenderness, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>ileus</u>, <u>GI bleed</u>)

* <u>Encephalopathy</u>

* Worsening <u>ascites</u>

* Worsening <u>liver</u> and/or renal function





#### Peritoneal fluid analysis in SBP




* Cell count and differential: <u>neutrophil</u> count of ≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup> indicates <u>SBP</u>
* If the ascitic fluid is bloody (i.e., <u>RBC</u> > 10<sup>3</sup>/mm<sup>3</sup>), calculate the corrected <u>neutrophil</u> count by subtracting one <u>neutrophil</u> for every 250 <u>RBCs</u>. [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nkb7oF)

* <u>Gram stain</u>: limited value; very low <u>sensitivity</u> and high <u>false-positive rates</u>. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LkbwoF)

* Bacterial cultures: often negative

* Positive cultures not required for diagnosis but should be ordered in every patient suspected of having <u>SBP</u>  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/okb0KF)

* Bacterascites

* Definition: a positive ascitic fluid <u>bacterial culture</u> in the presence of ascitic fluid <u>neutrophil</u> count < 250/mm<sup>3</sup> that is not associated with a surgically treatable intraabdominal source of infection

* Interpretation: <u>Bacterascites</u> may represent transient bacterial <u>colonization</u> or early <u>SBP</u>. [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RkblnF)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mObVGF)

* Other

* <u>Albumin</u>: <u>serum-ascites albumin gradient</u> (<u>SAAG</u>) > 1.1 indicates <u>portal hypertension</u>-related <u>ascites</u>.

* Total protein, <u>glucose</u>, <u>LDH</u>: helpful in distinguishing <u>SBP</u> from <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> (see “Differential diagnosis” section)





> [!TIP]
> <u>SBP</u> is diagnosed when the ascitic fluid <u>neutrophil</u> count is ≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup> in the absence of another intraabdominal source of infection. The diagnosis of <u>SBP</u> does not require positive ascitic fluid cultures.




### Imaging




Imaging tests are not required for diagnosis but may be indicated in patients with new-onset or worsening <u>ascites</u>  (see ”Diagnostics” in “<u>Cirrhosis</u>”) or if <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> is suspected (see “Differential diagnosis” section).

## Treatment

### <u>Antimicrobial therapy</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* Indications (presence of any of the following in a patient with <u>cirrhosis</u> and <u>ascites</u>): [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)

* Strong suspicion for infection

* Positive <u>SIRS</u> criteria, e.g., <u>fever</u>

* Specific <u>clinical features of SBP</u>, e.g., abdominal <u>pain</u> and tenderness, <u>AMS</u>

* Ascitic fluid <u>neutrophil</u> count ≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup>

* Most common isolates: <u>Escherichia coli</u>, <u>Streptococcus</u> spp., <u>Staphylococcus</u> spp., and <u>Klebsiella</u> (see “Etiology” section) [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/plbLBF)

* <u>Risk factors</u> for resistant pathogens include: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)

* <u>Nosocomial infection</u>

* Recent infection with multiresistant bacteria

* Recent exposure to <u>broad-spectrum antibiotics</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)

* Long-term <u>fluoroquinolone</u> prophylaxis

* Duration

* Narrow <u>antibiotic</u> coverage according to bacterial susceptibility.

* Continue <u>antibiotic therapy</u> for at least 5–7 days. [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qkbCKF)

#### Management of <u>bacterascites</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* Start <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> only if signs/symptoms of infection are present (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for SBP</u>”). [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)

* If asymptomatic, repeat <u>paracentesis</u> after 48 hours and treat with <u>antibiotics</u> if repeat culture is positive.  [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RkblnF)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Empiric antibiotics</u> are not indicated for asymptomatic patients with <u>bacterascites</u>.




### Adjunctive therapy [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* IV <u>albumin</u> supplementation DOSAGE [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eDdxWH0)

* Indicated in all patients with <u>SBP</u>   [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xkbEJF)

* Particularly beneficial for patients with:

* Total <u>bilirubin</u> > 5 mg/dL

* <u>Blood urea nitrogen</u> > 30 mg/dL

* <u>Creatinine</u> > 1 mg/dL

* Consider discontinuing <u>beta blockers</u> in patients with <u>hypotension</u> or <u>AKI</u>.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nOb7GF)

* Discontinue <u>diuretics</u>.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)

* Avoid potentially <u>nephrotoxic medications</u> (e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u>). [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ikbJnF)

* Consider discontinuing proton-pump inhibitors.  [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2ObT7F)





### Supportive therapy




* <u>Electrolyte repletion</u>

* <u>Analgesics</u> as needed (see “<u>Pain management</u>”)

* <u>Antipyretics</u> as needed

* <u>IV fluids</u>: Use caution in patients who are volume overloaded.

* See “Treatment” and “Complications” in “<u>Cirrhosis</u>” for other management considerations.





### Monitoring and subsequent management [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)




* <u>Serial abdominal examination</u>

* Repeat <u>paracentesis</u>

* Perform after 48 hours to assess response to <u>antibiotics</u>.  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WCcPIe0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)

* A reduction of ascitic <u>PMNs</u> by less than 25% indicates a poor response.

* Consider <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> in patients with:

* Clinical deterioration despite first-line <u>antibiotics</u>

* AND/OR insufficient reduction of <u>PMNs</u> on repeat <u>paracentesis</u>

* Consider GI/hepatology consult or infectious disease consult.

* Consider referral for a <u>liver transplant</u> evaluation.  [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UObb7F)

## Differential diagnoses

### Secondary bacterial peritonitis [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jkb_nF)




* Definition: inflammation of the <u>peritoneum</u> caused by bacterial infection from a surgically treatable intraabdominal source [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)

* Etiology: 

* Perforation of an intraabdominal <u>viscus</u> (e.g., <u>duodenal perforation</u> due to a <u>peptic ulcer</u>)

* Translocation of bacteria from an abdominal organ inflammation (e.g., <u>appendicitis</u>, <u>diverticulitis</u>, <u>pancreatitis</u>, <u>intraabdominal abscess</u>)

* Trauma (e.g., penetrating wound)

* <u>Iatrogenic</u> (e.g., <u>surgery</u>, <u>anastomosis insufficiency</u>, invasive procedure)

* Clinical features

* Abdominal <u>pain</u>/tenderness

* <u>Peritoneal signs</u> (e.g., local or diffuse rigidity, <u>rebound tenderness</u>, and/or guarding)  [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3q0SyS)

* Gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>ileus</u>)

* Signs of infection (e.g., <u>fever</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>leukocytosis</u>, <u>sepsis</u>, <u>shock</u>)

* Worsening or lack of symptom resolution after initiation of <u>antibiotics</u> in a patient initially suspected of having <u>SBP</u>

* Diagnosis [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)

* Cannot be reliably distinguished from <u>SBP</u> based on the clinical presentation and <u>physical exam</u> alone [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jkb_nF)

* <u>Peritoneal fluid analysis</u>:

* Cell count: <u>neutrophil</u> count of ≥ 250/mm<sup>3</sup>

* <u>Bacterial culture</u> and/or <u>gram stain</u>: positive

* Chemistries: total protein > 1 g/dL, <u>LDH</u> > <u>upper limit of normal</u> for serum, <u>glucose</u> < 50 mg/dL   [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jkb_nF)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sObtFF)

* Other: <u>Carcinoembryonic antigen</u> > 5 ng/mL or <u>alkaline phosphatase</u> > 240 U/L suggests <u>secondary peritonitis</u> with <u>intestinal perforation</u>.  [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GObBFF)

* Imaging:

* <u>CT scan</u> of the abdomen and <u>pelvis</u> with IV contrast: indicated for any patient with suspected <u>secondary bacterial peritonitis</u>

* <u>X-ray abdomen</u> (upright): Free air may be seen if viscous organ perforation is present.

* Management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lkbvLF)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nkb-LF)

* Start empiric <u>broad-spectrum antibiotics</u>: See “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for intraabdominal infections</u>” for approach and regimen options.

* Consult <u>surgery</u>;  for consideration of <u>emergency laparotomy</u>.

* Provide <u>supportive care</u>.

* <u>IV fluids</u>

* <u>Electrolyte repletion</u>

* <u>Analgesics</u> as needed (see “<u>Pain management</u>”)

* <u>Antipyretics</u> as needed

* <u>VTE prophylaxis</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Secondary bacterial peritonitis</u> usually requires imaging and urgent surgical management.

* <u>Skin and soft tissue infection</u>

* <u>Bacteremia</u>

* <u>Peritoneal carcinomatosis</u>

* <u>Pancreatic</u> illness

* Hepatic <u>metastasis</u>

* Cardiac <u>ascites</u>

* <u>Nephrotic syndrome</u>

## Complications

* <u>Sepsis</u>, <u>septic shock</u>, <u>death</u>

* Acute or chronic <u>liver</u> failure

* <u>Acute kidney injury</u> and/or <u>hepatorenal syndrome</u>

* <u>Hepatic encephalopathy</u>

* <u>GI bleed</u>

* Worsening <u>ascites</u>




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
