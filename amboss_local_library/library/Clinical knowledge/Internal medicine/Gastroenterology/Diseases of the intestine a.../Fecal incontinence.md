# Fecal incontinence

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Gastroenterology > Diseases of the intestine and appendix > Fecal incontinence*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Vs0GFh)

## Summary

Fecal incontinence is the involuntary passage of feces over a period of ≥ 3 months. Fecal incontinence affects ≥ 8% of adults in the US and <u>prevalence</u> increases with age. The etiology of fecal incontinence is frequently multifactorial; causes include anal disorders (e.g., anal sphincter <u>weakness</u> following obstetric injury), rectal disorders (e.g., <u>rectal cancer</u>), neurological conditions (e.g., <u>spinal cord injury</u>), and/or abnormal stool consistency (e.g., <u>diarrhea</u>). Clinical evaluation involves a focused history to characterize the fecal incontinence and associated symptoms. For certain causes of fecal incontinence (e.g., <u>spinal cord injury</u> or anorectal cancer), the patient should be promptly referred for specialist management. Otherwise, initial management consists of empiric <u>supportive care</u>, with or without pharmacological treatment. Patients who do not improve after a trial of empiric <u>supportive care</u> should be referred to a specialist for advanced diagnostic studies (e.g., anorectal physiology testing) to determine the best management approach. Advanced management options include bowel management programs (e.g., administering scheduled suppositories), <u>pelvic floor muscle therapy</u>, and/or invasive procedures (e.g., <u>anal sphincteroplasty</u>).




<u>Fecal incontinence in children</u> is detailed separately.

## Definitions

* Fecal incontinence: the involuntary passage of feces over a period of ≥ 3 months  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)

* Fecal urge incontinence: the involuntary passage of feces despite deliberate effort to retain stool [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* Passive fecal incontinence: the involuntary passage of feces without awareness [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

## Epidemiology

* <u>Prevalence</u>: ≥ 8% of adults (median <u>prevalence</u>); up to 50% among <u>nursing home</u> residents  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* Sex: <u>♀</u> > <u>♂</u>  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)

* <u>Risk factors</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)

* Age > 65 years

* Conditions that cause fecal incontinence: See “Etiology.”

* <u>Urinary incontinence</u>




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

## Etiology

### Anal disorders [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




* Anal sphincter <u>weakness</u> or injury

* <u>Obstetric nerve injuries</u> and <u>obstetric lacerations</u> (due to, e.g., instrumental delivery, <u>prolonged second stage of labor</u>)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)

* Proctology and gynecology surgical procedures (e.g., <u>fistula</u> <u>surgery</u>; , <u>hemorrhoidectomy</u>, <u>hysterectomy</u>)

* <u>Systemic sclerosis</u>

* Medications (e.g., <u>antianginal medications</u>, <u>antihypertensives</u>)

* Other

* <u>Anal cancer</u>

* <u>Hemorrhoids</u>

* <u>Anal fistulas</u>





### Rectal disorders [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




* Disorders of rectal reservoir or <u>sensory function</u> (due to, e.g., <u>radiation proctitis</u>)

* <u>Pelvic floor dysfunction</u>: may involve <u>rectal prolapse</u> and/or <u>rectocele</u>

* <u>Rectal cancer</u>





### Neurological disorders [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




* <u>Spinal cord injury</u> and <u>cauda equina syndrome</u>

* <u>Pudendal nerve injury</u>

* <u>Multiple sclerosis</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Major neurocognitive disorder</u>

* <u>Stroke</u>

* Impaired functional status (e.g., decreased mobility) may cause <u>functional incontinence</u> or exacerbate incontinence due to other causes.





### Stool disorders [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




* <u>Constipation</u>, <u>fecal impaction</u>, and/or retention causing overflow

* <u>Causes of chronic diarrhea</u>, e.g.:

* <u>Inflammatory bowel disease</u>

* <u>Irritable bowel syndrome</u>

* Medications (e.g., <u>metformin</u>)





> [!TIP]
> Fecal incontinence is multifactorial in 80% of patients. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)

## Clinical evaluation

### Focused history [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




* Involuntary passage of feces

* Onset and duration: chronic and/or recurring

* Frequency, consistency, and volume (e.g., small amount vs. a complete bowel movement)

* <u>Urge fecal incontinence</u> or <u>passive fecal incontinence</u>

* Impact on quality of life (e.g., <u>activities of daily living</u>, work, relationships)

* <u>Past medical history</u> (i.e., procedures or <u>conditions associated with fecal incontinence</u>; see “Etiology”)

* Additional clinical features

* Stool consistency and volume outside of incontinence episodes

* <u>Hematochezia</u> and/or mucus

* <u>Tenesmus</u>

* Flatus: volume, flatus incontinence

* Abdominal <u>pain</u> and/or distention

* <u>Urinary incontinence</u>

* Neurological symptoms (e.g., lower limb <u>weakness</u>, abnormal <u>sensory function</u>)

* Weight loss





> [!TIP]
> Ask about fecal incontinence in patients with <u>risk factors</u>. Patients may not report it due to embarrassment or a belief that it is a normal part of <u>aging</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




### Focused <u>physical examination</u>




* Perianal area [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)

* Fecal staining

* Surgical scars and/or <u>fistulas</u>

* <u>Rectal prolapse</u> (may only be visible during <u>Valsalva maneuver</u>)

* <u>Skin</u> rashes and/or <u>excoriation</u>

* <u>Anocutaneous reflex</u>

* <u>Digital rectal examination</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)

* <u>Anal sphincter tone</u> and squeeze pressure

* Rectal mass, stricture, and/or <u>hemorrhoids</u>

* <u>Fecal impaction</u>

* <u>Pelvic examination</u>: signs of <u>pelvic floor dysfunction</u> (e.g., <u>pelvic organ prolapse</u>)

* Focused <u>neurological examination</u>: Assess <u>motor function</u> and <u>sensory function</u> for, e.g., <u>clinical features of spinal cord injury</u> or <u>cauda equina syndrome</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

## Management

### Approach [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)




* Fecal incontinence is <u>diagnosed clinically</u>.

* Promptly refer to a specialist for management if there is suspicion for serious <u>conditions associated with fecal incontinence</u> (e.g., <u>colorectal cancer</u>, <u>spinal cord injury</u>).

* Initial <u>management of fecal incontinence</u> consists of <u>supportive care</u>; pharmacological treatment may be considered.

* If initial management is unsuccessful, refer for specialist multidisciplinary management.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)





> [!WARNING]
> Refer urgently for specialist assessment if there are <u>red flags for colorectal cancer</u> (e.g., <u>hematochezia</u>, unexplained weight loss) or neurological symptoms. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)




### Initial management




#### <u>Supportive care</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




Educate patients about self-management techniques.




* Lifestyle measures

* Avoid exacerbating factors (e.g., <u>caffeine</u>, artificial sweeteners, <u>lactose</u>, medications).

* Increase fiber intake and physical activity.

* Regular bowel habits (e.g., attempt defecation daily after breakfast) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* Bowel diary

* <u>Perianal dermatitis</u> prevention and management: e.g., <u>barrier cream</u>, gentle soaps, wet wipes





#### Pharmacological treatment [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




Pharmacological treatment aims to optimize stool consistency and reduce the frequency of defecation.




* <u>Dietary fiber</u> supplementation: <u>bulk-forming laxatives</u> (e.g., <u>psyllium</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Antidiarrheals</u>: only in patients with <u>diarrhea</u> (e.g., <u>loperamide</u> DOSAGE)





### Specialist management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)




Specialist management may be considered if initial management is unsuccessful.




#### Diagnostic studies for fecal incontinence




* Anorectal physiology testing [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* <u>Anorectal manometry</u>: first-line diagnostic test

* Rectal sensory test

* Balloon expulsion test: evaluates the ability to evacuate a simulated stool (i.e., a latex balloon filled with 50 mL of fluid, attached to a catheter) in patients with suspected <u>pelvic floor dyssynergia</u>

* <u>Endoanal ultrasound</u>: to assess anal sphincter structural defects [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)

* <u>Barium defecography</u>: second-line study to assess for <u>pelvic floor dysfunction</u> and structural abnormalities (e.g., <u>rectocele</u>) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)





#### Advanced interventions [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)




* Bowel management program (e.g., scheduled suppositories or enemas, regular <u>transanal irrigation</u>) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* <u>Pelvic floor muscle therapy</u>: may involve <u>biofeedback</u> (e.g., pressure sensors that provide auditory feedback) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g5dFjq0)

* Invasive procedures (e.g., <u>anal sphincteroplasty</u> or <u>sacral neuromodulation</u>)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALdRZI0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ld5ZI0)
