# Serum sickness

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Rheumatology and immunology > Hypersensivity reactions > Serum sickness*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Vt0Gc3)

---

## Summary

Serum sickness is a <u>type III hypersensitivity reaction</u> that usually develops as a complication of <u>antitoxin</u> or <u>antivenom</u> administration, but can also occur after <u>antibiotic</u> administration and <u>hepatitis B infection</u>. Symptoms typically occur 1–3 weeks after exposure to the offending agent and include <u>fever</u>, <u>rash</u>, and <u>arthralgia</u>. It is <u>diagnosed clinically</u> and treatment consists primarily of removing the offending agent.

---

## Definitions

Serum sickness is a classic example of a <u>type III hypersensitivity</u> reaction, which usually develops as a complication of <u>antitoxin</u> or <u>antivenom</u> administration.

---

## Etiology

* <u>Antivenom</u> or <u>antitoxin</u> containing animal <u>proteins</u> or serum (→ “serum” sickness), such as:

* Equine anti-snake venom

* Equine anti-spider venom

* Equine or bovine anti-<u>rabies</u> <u>antitoxin</u>

* Equine <u>botulinum antitoxin</u>

* Medications: most frequently <u>antibiotics</u> (e.g., <u>penicillin</u>, <u>amoxicillin</u>, cefaclor, <u>trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole</u>) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MP1M2S0)

* Infections: <u>Hepatitis B virus</u>

---

## Pathophysiology

Exposure to an <u>antigen</u> (e.g., <u>antivenom</u>, drug) → formation of <u>antibodies</u> → deposition of <u>antibody</u>-<u>antigen</u> complexes in tissue → activation of the <u>complement</u> cascade → tissue damage and systemic inflammation

---

## Clinical features

* <u>Fever</u>

* <u>Rash</u> (<u>urticarial</u> or <u>purpuric</u>)

* <u>Arthralgias</u>, <u>myalgia</u>

* <u>Lymphadenopathy</u>

* Hand <u>edema</u>

* <u>Headache</u>, blurred <u>vision</u>

* Abdominal <u>pain</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>nausea</u>/<u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Mucosal</u> involvement is uncommon





> [!TIP]
> Symptoms appear 1–3 weeks following initial exposure (because <u>antibodies</u> take several days to form) and typically resolve within a few weeks after discontinuation of the offending agent.

---

## Diagnosis

* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u>: see “Clinical features.”

* <u>Laboratory studies</u>: <u>Urinalysis</u> may show mild <u>proteinuria</u>.

---

## Differential diagnoses

See also “<u>Hypersensitivity reactions</u>.”




### Serum sickness-like reaction




* <u>Epidemiology</u>: much more common than actual serum sickness

* Etiology: similar to that of serum sickness

* Infections (e.g., <u>hepatitis B</u>, <u>rabies</u>)

* Medications that can act as <u>haptens</u> (e.g., <u>allopurinol</u>, <u>cephalosporins</u>, <u>penicillin</u>)

* Pathogenesis: unclear (likely not the result of a <u>type III hypersensitivity reaction</u>)

* Clinical features

* Similar to classic serum sickness

* May manifest with severe <u>erythema multiforme</u> and <u>arthralgias</u>

## Treatment

* Stop the offending agent.

* Consider pharmacotherapy, e.g.: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MP1M2S0)

* <u>Systemic glucocorticoids</u>

* <u>H<sub>1</sub> blockers</u>

* <u>NSAIDs</u> as needed for <u>arthralgias</u>





> [!TIP]
> Prognosis is excellent once the offending drug is stopped or the causative infection has resolved.
