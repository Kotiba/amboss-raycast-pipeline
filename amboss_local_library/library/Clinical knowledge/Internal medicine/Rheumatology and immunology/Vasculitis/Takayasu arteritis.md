# Takayasu arteritis

## Summary

Takayasu arteritis (<u>aortic arch</u> syndrome) is a systemic <u>vasculitis of large vessels</u> characterized by <u>granulomatous inflammation</u> of the aorta and its major branches. It most commonly affects Asian women < 40 years of age. Patients typically present with <u>constitutional symptoms</u>, decreased bilateral brachial and radial pulses (the disease is also known as pulseless disease), <u>angina</u>, and <u>syncope</u>. Imaging (e.g., <u>MR angiography</u>) is used to confirm the diagnosis; findings include vascular wall thickening and <u>contrast enhancement</u>, and luminal stenosis or occlusion. <u>Biopsy</u> of the affected vessels can also confirm the diagnosis but is not routinely indicated. Management typically involves <u>immunosuppressive agents</u> (e.g., high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u> plus <u>methotrexate</u>); surgical therapy (e.g., vascular bypass) is indicated for patients with limb <u>ischemia</u>.

## Definitions

<u>Granulomatous inflammation</u> of the aorta and its major branches, resulting in thickening and stenosis of the involved <u>blood vessels</u> and subsequent vascular symptoms [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)

## Clinical features

* Nonspecific symptoms: <u>Fever</u>, <u>malaise</u>, <u>arthralgia</u>, night sweats

* Vascular symptoms

* Decreased bilateral brachial and radial pulses (so-called pulseless disease)

* <u>Syncope</u>, <u>angina pectoris</u>

* Bilateral <u>carotid bruits</u>

* Impaired <u>vision</u>

* Movement-induced muscular <u>pain</u> in one or more limbs

* <u>Raynaud phenomenon</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* <u>Skin</u> manifestations

* <u>Erythema nodosum</u>

* <u>Urticaria</u>





> [!NOTE]
> You can't TAKe a <u>pulse</u> in TAKayasu arteritis (pulseless disease).

## Diagnosis

### General principles [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpXE7_)




* Diagnosis is primarily clinical but always requires confirmation with imaging or <u>biopsy</u>.

* Nonspecific laboratory findings may support the diagnosis: e.g., ↑ <u>ESR</u>, ↑ <u>CRP</u>, <u>leukocytosis</u>, <u>anemia</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zpXrH_)

* Serial measurements of <u>CBC</u> and <u>inflammatory markers</u> may be used for follow-up.





### Imaging studies [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j1b_gs)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_pX5H_)




Imaging of the aorta and major branches involves:




* <u>MR angiography</u> (<u>MRA</u>) 

* Indications: preferred initial study for all patients

* Findings

* Thickening and <u>contrast enhancement</u> of the vascular wall (i.e., inflammation)

* Luminal stenosis or occlusion

* Alternatively

* <u>CT angiography</u> (<u>CTA</u>) 

* More readily available and has higher resolution than <u>MRA</u>

* Findings: similar to <u>MRA</u>

* <u>Angiography</u> ;   [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zpXrH_)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j1b_gs)

* Consider for patients with planned vascular interventions (e.g., <u>angioplasty</u>, <u>stenting</u>).

* Detects vascular stenosis

* <u>Echocardiography</u>: Consider for all patients to assess for <u>aortic regurgitation</u> and ventricular <u>hypertrophy</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Diagnostic confirmation frequently relies on imaging, as <u>biopsy</u> is not always possible because affected vessels are often inaccessible. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)

### <u>Biopsy</u> of affected vessel [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpXE7_)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zpXrH_)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_pX5H_)




* Indications

* Not routinely required

* Consider for patients with planned vascular interventions.

* Supportive findings

* Granulomatous thickening of the <u>aortic arch</u>

* <u>Plasma cells</u> and <u>lymphocytes</u> in the media and adventitia

* Vascular <u>fibrosis</u>

## Treatment

### General principles [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)




* Consult rheumatology for all patients.

* Pharmacotherapy with <u>immunosuppressants</u> is the mainstay of treatment.

* Regimens are guided by disease severity and activity.

* Goal is to achieve and maintain clinical remission

* Surgical therapy is reserved for selected cases.





### Pharmacotherapy [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)




* Initial therapy: Start <u>remission induction therapy</u> for patients with active disease. 

* High-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u>

* PLUS a <u>glucocorticoid-sparing agent</u>, e.g.:  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)

* <u>Methotrexate</u>

* <u>Azathioprine</u>

* <u>Cyclophosphamide</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)

* <u>TNF inhibitor</u> (e.g., <u>infliximab</u>)

* Subsequent therapy: based on response to initial therapy 

* Clinical remission achieved

* Taper and discontinue <u>glucocorticoids</u>.

* Continue <u>glucocorticoid-sparing agent</u>.

* Clinical remission not achieved (relapsed or refractory disease)

* Continue <u>glucocorticoids</u>.

* Consider changing to another <u>glucocorticoid-sparing agent</u>.

* Consider referral for surgical therapy.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Glucocorticoid</u> monotherapy is associated with a high relapse rate (∼ 70%). [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




### Surgical therapy [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zpXrH_)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)




* Indications

* Elective procedures: progressive <u>ischemia</u> despite escalation of <u>immunosuppressive agents</u>

* Emergency procedures

* Critical stenosis of the <u>aortic arch</u> or its branches

* Symptomatic neurological complications (e.g., <u>cranial</u> and/or cervical vessel stenosis)

* <u>Aneurysm</u> dissection

* Surgical interventions include:

* Vascular bypass

* Vascular graft

* <u>Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty</u>

* <u>Stent</u> placement





> [!TIP]
> Except for emergency indications, surgical intervention should be delayed until the patient is stable to decrease the risk of complications and achieve higher patency rates. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




### Long-term monitoring




* Clinical evaluation (e.g., every 3–6 months )

* Regular imaging with noninvasive studies (e.g., <u>MRA</u>, <u>CTA</u>)

* Consider monitoring with <u>inflammatory markers</u>.





### <u>Supportive care</u>




* Educate all patients on <u>ASCVD prevention</u>.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)

* <u>Prevent complications of glucocorticoid therapy</u>.

* Monitor for <u>adverse effects of immunosuppressants</u>.

* Consider <u>pneumocystis pneumonia prophylaxis</u>.
