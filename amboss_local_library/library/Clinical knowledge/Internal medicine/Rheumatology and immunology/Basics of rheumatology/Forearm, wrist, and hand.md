[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3p0S6S)

## Summary

The wrist is comprised of the carpus and the <u>radiocarpal joint</u>. The carpus is the complex of eight <u>carpal bones</u> (<u>scaphoid</u>, lunate, <u>triquetrum</u>, <u>pisiform</u>, <u>trapezium</u>, trapezoid, <u>capitate</u>, and <u>hamate</u>), while the <u>radiocarpal joint</u> is the region of articulation between the carpus and radius. Distally, the carpus articulates with the <u>metacarpal bones</u>, which, together with the <u>phalanges</u>, make up the <u>bones of the hand</u>. The forearm (lower arm or antebrachium) has an <u>anterior</u> compartment, which consists of the flexor group of muscles and is innervated by the ulnar and <u>median nerve</u>, and a <u>posterior</u> compartment, which consists of the extensor group of muscles and is innervated by the <u>radial nerve</u>. The flexor group of muscles is involved in <u>pronation</u> of the forearm and <u>flexion</u> of the wrist and fingers, while the extensor group of muscles is involved in <u>supination</u> of the forearm and extension of the wrist and fingers. Both groups of muscles are also involved in the <u>abduction</u> and <u>adduction</u> of the wrist. The <u>intrinsic muscles of the hand</u> are responsible for hand and finger movement and consist of the thenar, <u>hypothenar</u>, <u>lumbrical</u>, and <u>interossei</u> muscles. The forearm, the wrist, and the hand are <u>perfused</u> by the radial and <u>ulnar artery</u> and their branches. They are drained by the <u>superficial</u> cephalic and <u>basilic veins</u> and the deep radial and <u>ulnar veins</u>.

## Bones and joints

### Bones of the forearm




* The forearm is the portion of the upper limb between the <u>elbow</u> and <u>wrist joint</u>.

* The <u>bones of the forearm</u> are the radius and <u>ulna</u>.

* They articulate with each other at the <u>proximal</u> and <u>distal</u> <u>radioulnar joints</u>.

* The <u>radioulnar joints</u> are responsible for forearm <u>pronation</u> and <u>supination</u>.

* The <u>proximal</u> ends of the radius and <u>ulna</u> articulate with the <u>distal</u> <u>humerus</u> to form the <u>elbow joint</u>.

* The interosseus membrane connects the shafts of the radius and <u>ulna</u> and stabilizes the <u>radioulnar joints</u>.





#### Ulna




* Description: the <u>medial</u> bone of the forearm (when in <u>anatomical position</u>)

* Parts of the <u>ulna</u>

* <u>Proximal</u> <u>ulna</u>

* Olecranon

* An <u>anterior</u>-facing, hook-like bony projection that fits into the <u>olecranon fossa</u> on the <u>dorsal</u> aspect of the <u>distal</u> <u>humerus</u> when the <u>elbow</u> is extended

* Prevents <u>elbow</u> hyperextension (> 180°)

* Coronoid process: an <u>anterior</u>-facing bony projection that forms the lower lip of the <u>trochlear notch</u>

* Trochlear notch: a concavity between the <u>olecranon</u> and the <u>coronoid process</u> that articulates with the <u>trochlea of the humerus</u> (<u>humeroulnar joint</u>)

* Radial notch: a <u>lateral</u> continuation of the <u>trochlear notch</u> that accommodates the <u>head of the radius</u>, forming the <u>proximal radioulnar joint</u>

* Tuberosity of the ulna: a rough area of bone just <u>distal</u> to the <u>coronoid process</u> into which the <u>brachialis muscle</u> inserts

* <u>Diaphysis</u>: triangular in shape, with three surfaces (<u>anterior</u>, <u>posterior</u>, and <u>medial</u>) and three borders (<u>anterior</u>, <u>posterior</u>, and interosseous)

* <u>Distal</u> <u>ulna</u>

* Ulnar head (articular circumference): articulates with the radius to form the <u>distal radioulnar joint</u>

* Ulnar styloid: a bony projection <u>posterior</u> and <u>medial</u> to the <u>ulna</u>

#### Radius (bone)




* Description: the <u>lateral</u> bone of the forearm (when in <u>anatomical position</u>)

* Parts of the radius 

* <u>Proximal</u> radius

* Head of radius

* A disk-shaped structure, the <u>medial</u> rim of which articulates with the <u>radial notch</u> of the <u>ulna</u>, forming the <u>proximal radioulnar joint</u>

* The superior <u>concave</u> surface (articular fovea) articulates with the <u>capitulum of the humerus</u> (<u>humeroradial joint</u> of the <u>elbow</u>).

* Radial tuberosity

* <u>Distal</u> to the <u>medial</u> aspect of the head

* Point of attachment for the <u>biceps brachii</u> <u>tendon</u>

* <u>Distal</u> radius

* Radial styloid: insertion site for the <u>brachioradialis muscle</u> and the radial collateral <u>ligament</u>

* Carpal articular surface of the radius

* Ulnar notch of the radius

* Located on the <u>medial</u> surface

* Articulates with the <u>ulna</u>, forming the <u>distal radioulnar joint</u>

* Dorsal tubercle of the radius (<u>Lister tubercle</u>)

* Bony prominence of the <u>distal</u> radius

* Serves as a pulley for the <u>extensor pollicis longus</u> <u>tendon</u>

### Joints of the forearm




#### Radioulnar joint




* General: the radius and <u>ulna</u> articulate with one another proximally and distally.

* Type of <u>joint</u>: <u>pivot joint</u>, allowing the <u>distal</u> end of the radius to move <u>anterior</u> and medially over the <u>ulna</u> (i.e., forearm <u>pronation</u> and <u>supination</u>)

* Proximal radioulnar joint: allows articulation of the <u>head of the radius</u> and the <u>radial notch</u> on the <u>ulna</u>

* Distal radioulnar joint: allows articulation of the <u>ulnar head</u> and <u>ulnar notch</u> on the radius

* Movement: forearm <u>pronation</u> and <u>supination</u>





> [!TIP]
> Injury to the radial or ulnar <u>diaphysis</u> can disrupt the <u>proximal</u> or <u>distal</u> <u>radioulnar joints</u>.

#### Interosseous membrane of the forearm




* Definition: fibrous <u>connective tissue</u> that connects the <u>lateral</u> <u>ulna</u> to the <u>medial</u> radius

* Function

* Connects the radius and <u>ulna</u> without restricting movement

* Attaches muscles to the radius and <u>ulna</u>

* Stabilizes the <u>radioulnar joint</u> by preventing <u>proximal</u> migration of the radius

### Bones of the wrist (carpal bones)



* Description: Eight <u>carpal bones</u>, arranged in two rows of four bones each, make up the <u>wrist joint</u>. 

* <u>Proximal</u> row (<u>lateral</u> to <u>medial</u>): <u>scaphoid</u>, lunate, <u>triquetrum</u>, and <u>pisiform</u>

* <u>Distal</u> row (<u>lateral</u> to <u>medial</u>): <u>trapezium</u>, trapezoid, <u>capitate</u>, and <u>hamate</u>

#### Anatomical snuffbox




* Definition: a triangular groove on the radial aspect of the <u>dorsal</u> wrist that becomes prominent when extending and <u>abducting</u> the thumb

* Boundaries  

* <u>Lateral</u> (radial): <u>extensor pollicis brevis</u>, <u>abductor pollicis longus</u> <u>tendons</u>

* <u>Medial</u> (ulnar): <u>extensor pollicis longus</u> <u>tendon</u>

* <u>Proximal</u>: <u>distal</u> end of the radius

* Contents

* A branch of <u>radial artery</u>

* A branch of <u>radial nerve</u>

* The <u>proximal</u> end of the <u>cephalic vein</u>

* <u>Scaphoid</u> and <u>trapezium</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Pain</u> and tenderness in the <u>anatomical snuffbox</u> after trauma to the wrist suggest a <u>scaphoid fracture</u>. These <u>fractures</u> can be difficult to see on plain <u>x-ray</u>.




### Bones of the hand




* Metacarpal bones 

* There are five <u>metacarpal bones</u>, which are located <u>distal</u> to the <u>carpal bones</u>.

* Each has a base that articulates with the <u>carpal bones</u> (<u>carpometacarpal joints</u>), a head that articulates with the <u>proximal</u> <u>phalanges</u> (<u>metacarpophalangeal joints</u>), and a shaft in between.

* Phalanges 

* Small, <u>long bones</u> of the digits

* Each digit has three <u>phalanges</u>, except the thumb, which only has two.

## Muscles and fascia

### Muscles of the forearm [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ES08Yf)




* The <u>anterior</u> (<u>volar</u>) compartment

* Contains the flexor group of muscles, which are innervated by the median and ulnar nerves

* Function

* <u>Flexion</u> of the wrist and fingers

* <u>Pronation</u> of the hand

* The <u>posterior</u> (<u>dorsal</u>) compartment

* Contains the extensor group of muscles, which are innervated by the <u>radial nerve</u>

* Function

* Extension of the wrist and fingers

* <u>Supination</u> of the hand

* Most <u>muscles of the forearm</u> move the wrist and hand <u>joints</u> (<u>extrinsic muscles of the hand</u>).

### Muscles of the hand




* Based on where the muscle belly is located, the muscles of the hand are divided into two groups.

* Extrinsic muscles of the hand: The muscle bellies are located in the forearm (see “<u>Muscles of the forearm</u>” above).

* Intrinsic muscles of the hand: The muscle bellies are located within the hand.

* All hand muscles are supplied by the <u>median nerve</u> or <u>ulnar nerve</u>.

* In the resting position, there is a balance between flexors and extensors (both extrinsic and intrinsic) of the hand.

## Vasculature and lymphatic drainage

### <u>Arteries</u>




* The arterial system of the forearm originates from the <u>brachial artery</u> in the <u>cubital fossa</u>, where it bifurcates into the ulnar and <u>radial arteries</u>.

* The <u>radial artery</u> and the <u>ulnar artery</u> are the two major <u>blood vessels</u> that supply blood to the forearm and hand.

* These two <u>arteries</u> <u>anastomose</u> in the hand, forming the <u>superficial palmar arch</u> and the <u>deep palmar arch</u>.

* For further information on the arterial supply of the forearm and hand, see “<u>Neurovasculature of the upper limbs</u>.”





![Arteries of the upper extremity](../../../../../media/pczabpv1mfqwjuigejhxhtfp.jpg)

Arteries of the upper extremity




### <u>Veins</u>




* The venous system of the forearm and hand consists of a <u>superficial</u> and a <u>deep venous system</u>.

* <u>Superficial venous system</u>

* Located within the <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>

* Formed by the <u>cephalic vein</u>, <u>basilic vein</u>, and the <u>median antebrachial vein</u>

* <u>Deep venous system</u>

* Located below the deep <u>fascia</u>

* Formed by:

* Paired <u>veins</u> (<u>venae comitantes</u>), which accompany each <u>artery</u>

* <u>Radial vein</u>

* <u>Ulnar vein</u>

* <u>Brachial vein</u>: formed by the union of the radial and <u>ulnar veins</u> at the <u>elbow</u>

* The <u>deep veins</u> communicate with <u>superficial veins</u> via <u>perforator veins</u>.

* <u>Perforating veins</u> run between the deep and <u>superficial veins</u> of the upper limb, connecting the two systems.

* For further information on the venous supply of the forearm and hand, see “<u>Neurovasculature of the upper limbs</u>.”





![Veins of the upper extremity](../../../../../media/bl2j6lljsiu87rm2l3rbn7wd.jpg)

Veins of the upper extremity




### <u>Lymphatic drainage</u>




* <u>Superficial</u> <u>lymphatics</u>: accompany the <u>superficial veins</u> of the upper limb

* The <u>lymphatics</u> at the <u>lateral</u> side (radial) of the hand and forearm accompany the <u>cephalic vein</u> and drain mainly into the apical group of <u>axillary lymph nodes</u>.

* The <u>lymphatics</u> at the <u>medial</u> side (ulnar) of the hand and forearm accompany the <u>basilic vein</u> and drain first into the <u>cubital lymph nodes</u> (epitrochlear nodes), then terminate in the <u>lateral</u> group of <u>axillary lymph nodes</u>.

* Deep <u>lymphatics</u>: follow the <u>deep veins</u> and drain into the axillary group of <u>lymph nodes</u>

### Sensory innervation




* The sensory innervation of the hand is provided by the <u>median nerve</u>, <u>radial nerve</u>, and <u>ulnar nerve</u>.

* The sensory innervation of the forearm is provided by the <u>lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm</u>, <u>medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm</u>, and <u>posterior</u> cutaneous nerve of the arm.

* For further information on sensory innervation of the forearm and hand, see “<u>Neurovasculature of the upper limbs</u>.”

## Clinical significance

### Bones and <u>joints</u>




* <u>Ulna</u>

* <u>Ulnar fractures</u>

* <u>Ulnar styloid fracture</u> (associated with <u>distal radius fractures</u>)

* Radius

* <u>Distal radius fractures</u> (e.g., <u>Colles fracture</u>, <u>Smith fracture</u>)

* <u>Radial head subluxation</u>

* <u>Radioulnar joint</u>

* <u>Monteggia fracture</u>

* <u>Galeazzi fracture</u>

* <u>Distal radioulnar joint instability</u>

* <u>Carpal bones</u>

* <u>Scaphoid fracture</u>

* <u>Lunate dislocation</u>

* <u>Metacarpal bones</u>: <u>boxer's fracture</u> (<u>metacarpal neck fracture</u>)

* <u>Joints of the wrist and hand</u>

* <u>Trapeziometacarpal osteoarthritis</u> (<u>rhizarthrosis</u>) 

* The <u>saddle joint</u> of the thumb is susceptible to <u>cartilaginous</u> degeneration, resulting in <u>osteoarthritis</u>.

* Approximately 8–12% of the population is affected by carpometacarpal <u>osteoarthritis</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1RX2NB)

* Clinical features: <u>pain</u> and swelling at the base of the thumb

* Diagnostics [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1RX2NB)

* Thumb grind test: performed by applying axial compression along the plane of the <u>metacarpal</u> bone and rotating the thumb <u>metacarpal</u> base. <u>Pain</u> or <u>crepitus</u> indicates a positive grind test, suggesting carpometacarpal <u>osteoarthritis</u>.

* Thumb lever test: performed by grasping the first <u>metacarpal</u> just <u>distal</u> to the base and moving it back and forth in the <u>lateral</u> and <u>medial</u> directions. <u>Pain</u> or <u>crepitus</u> indicates a positive lever test, suggesting carpometacarpal <u>osteoarthritis</u>.

* MCP extension test: performed by placing one finger on the <u>interphalangeal joint</u> and applying resistance against extension while the patient tries to extend the thumb. <u>Pain</u> or <u>crepitus</u> indicates a positive <u>MCP extension test</u>, suggesting carpometacarpal <u>osteoarthritis</u>.

* Treatment: may involve excision of the <u>trapezium</u>.

* <u>Metacarpophalangeal joint</u>: 1<sup>st</sup> <u>MCP</u> <u>joint</u> is affected early by <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>

* <u>Interphalangeal joints</u>

* <u>Bouchard nodes</u> (in <u>osteoarthritis</u>) and <u>Boutonniere deformity</u> (in <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>) affect the <u>PIP</u>.

* <u>Heberden nodes</u> (in <u>osteoarthritis</u>) and <u>Swan neck deformity</u> (in <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>) affect the <u>DIP</u>.
