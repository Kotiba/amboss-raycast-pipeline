# Gout and hyperuricemia

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YT0n62)

## Summary

<u>Gout</u> is an inflammatory <u>crystal arthropathy</u> caused by the precipitation and deposition of <u>uric acid crystals</u> in <u>synovial fluid</u> and tissues. Decreased <u>renal excretion</u> and/or increased production of <u>uric acid</u> leads to <u>hyperuricemia</u>, which is commonly asymptomatic but also predisposes to <u>gout</u>. <u>Acute gout</u> flares typically manifest with a severely painful big toe (<u>podagra</u>) and occur most often in men following triggers such as <u>alcohol</u> consumption. Diagnosis is based on clinical presentation and, ideally, by the demonstration of negatively <u>birefringent</u> <u>monosodium urate (MSU) crystals</u> on <u>synovial fluid analysis</u>. Acute attacks are treated with <u>corticosteroids</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u> (e.g., <u>naproxen</u>, <u>indomethacin</u>), or <u>colchicine</u>. The management of <u>chronic gout</u> includes lifestyle modifications and <u>urate-lowering medications</u> (e.g., <u>allopurinol</u>) to control <u>hyperuricemia</u>.




Acute <u>calcium pyrophosphate crystal deposition</u> (<u>CPPD</u>) disease, sometimes referred to as <u>pseudogout</u>, is another <u>crystal arthropathy</u> that resembles an <u>acute gout</u> flare and is managed similarly. It is covered in detail in a separate article.

## Etiology

### Hyperuricemia




* Can be <u>idiopathic</u> (<u>primary hyperuricemia</u>) or secondary to insufficient excretion or increased production of <u>purines</u>

* Predisposes to <u>gout</u> and <u>acute gout</u> attacks

* Not obligatory for developing <u>gout</u>





#### Primary hyperuricemia




* <u>Idiopathic</u> extracellular supersaturation of <u>uric acid</u>

* No history of comorbidities or medications that affect <u>uric acid</u> formation or excretion [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vnaAEO)





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Primary hyperuricemia</u> can be aggravated by poor dietary habits.




#### Secondary hyperuricemia [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9naNvO)




* Decreased <u>uric acid</u> excretion: most common cause

* Medications (e.g., <u>pyrazinamide</u>, <u>aspirin</u>, loop <u>diuretics</u>, <u>thiazides</u>, <u>niacin</u>)

* <u>Chronic renal insufficiency</u>, lead nephropathy [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dna1vO)

* Ketoacidosis (due to, e.g., <u>starvation</u>; , <u>diabetes mellitus</u>) and <u>lactic acidosis</u>

* <u>Postmenopause</u>

* Increased <u>uric acid</u> production

* High cell turnover, e.g.:

* <u>Tumor lysis syndrome</u>

* <u>Hemolytic anemia</u>  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ena8EO)

* <u>Psoriasis</u>

* <u>Myeloproliferative neoplasms</u>

* <u>Chemotherapy</u>, radiation

* Enzyme defects, e.g.: [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dna1vO)

* <u>Lesch-Nyhan syndrome</u>

* <u>Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate synthetase</u> overactivity

* <u>von Gierke disease</u>

* Diet rich in protein and especially <u>purine</u> (e.g., red meat, seafood)

* <u>Obesity</u>

* <u>Hypercholesterolemia</u>, <u>hypertriglyceridemia</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y6bdnu)

* <u>Sleep apnea</u>  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yGbdbv)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p4XL4y)

* Combined decreased excretion and overproduction: high <u>alcohol</u> consumption

* Organic acids from <u>alcohol metabolism</u> compete with <u>uric acid</u> to be excreted by the <u>kidneys</u>.

* Many alcoholic beverages contain a high level of <u>purines</u>.

## Pathophysiology

* <u>Gout</u>

* An inflammatory <u>crystal arthropathy</u> that is caused by the precipitation and deposition of <u>uric acid crystals</u> in <u>synovial fluid</u> and tissues.

* It is typically associated with <u>hyperuricemia</u>, but can also occur if <u>uric acid</u> levels are normal.

* <u>Uric acid</u>

* An end-product of <u>purine</u> metabolism that is excreted by the <u>kidneys</u>

* Has somewhat poor water solubility

* Predisposes to <u>gout</u>

* Triggers of <u>urate</u> crystal deposition

* ↑ <u>Uric acid</u> levels (due to insufficient excretion or increased production of <u>purines</u>)

* <u>Acidosis</u>

* Low temperature (e.g., cool peripheral <u>joints</u>)

* <u>Crystalline arthritis</u>: supersaturation of <u>uric acid</u> in <u>extracellular fluid</u> → intraarticular uric crystal precipitation (coated by IgGs) → <u>phagocytosis</u> by polymorphonuclear cells → release of inflammatory mediators and enzymes → local <u>joint</u> inflammation

* Chronic effects: repeated attacks → aggregations of <u>urate crystals</u> and <u>giant cells</u> (<u>tophi</u>) → deformities and <u>arthritis</u>

## Clinical features

### Asymptomatic stage




* <u>Hyperuricemia</u> with no symptoms

* May last ≥ 10 years





### Acute gouty arthritis




* Triggers

* Sudden increase in <u>uric acid</u>, e.g.,: consuming a large amount of <u>purine</u>-rich foods or <u>alcohol</u> (see <u>risk factors for gout</u>)

* Trauma, <u>surgery</u>

* Diuresis

* <u>Dehydration</u>

* Most common manifestation

* Acute severe <u>pain</u> with overlying <u>erythema</u>, decreased <u>range of motion</u>, swelling, warmth

* Possibly <u>fever</u>

* Symptoms are more likely to occur at night, typically waking the patient.

* Symptoms peak after 12–24 hours and regress over days to weeks. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xnaEvO)

* Desquamation of the <u>skin</u> overlying the <u>joint</u> may be seen during the recovery from an <u>acute gout</u> flare.

* Location

* Usually <u>monoarthritis</u> during first attacks

* In < 20% of cases, patients present with <u>polyarthritis</u> during first attacks.

* Asymmetrical distribution is common if more than one <u>joint</u> is affected

* Peripheral <u>small joints</u> in the lower extremities are especially affected.

* Podagra: <u>metatarsophalangeal joint</u> (<u>MTP joint</u>) inflammation of the big toe (the most common site)

* Gonagra: inflammation of the knee

* Chiragra: inflammation of finger <u>joints</u>, especially <u>metacarpophalangeal joint</u> of the thumb

* Others: ankle; , tarsus, other toe <u>joints</u>, wrist, <u>elbow</u>

### Intercritical stage




* Asymptomatic

* May last up to several years





### Chronic gouty arthritis




* Uncommon in these times

* Progressive <u>joint</u> destruction

* Tophi formation 

* Multiple painless hard <u>nodules</u> with possible <u>joint</u> deformities [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CnaqvO)

* May appear yellow or white because of overlying attenuated <u>skin</u>

* <u>Ulceration</u> and discharge (chalky white substance) may occur

* Bone tophi: <u>urate</u> crystal deposition in bones (e.g., <u>elbows</u>, knees, extensor surfaces of forearms)

* Soft tissue tophi: <u>urate</u> crystal deposition in the <u>pinna</u> of the external <u>ear</u>, <u>subcutis</u>, <u>tendon sheaths</u> (e.g., at the <u>Achilles tendon</u>), or <u>synovial bursae</u> (e.g., <u>olecranon bursa</u>)

* Renal manifestations with <u>uric acid</u> <u>nephrolithiasis</u> and <u>uric acid</u> nephropathy

## Diagnosis

Identification of <u>monosodium urate (MSU) crystals</u> on <u>synovial fluid analysis</u> of the affected <u>joint</u> is the <u>gold standard</u> to diagnose <u>gout</u> but requires an invasive technique (i.e., <u>arthrocentesis</u>), trained personnel, and specialized instruments to perform the test. In patients with typical clinical features, a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> may be considered with/without supportive diagnostic evidence on imaging and <u>laboratory studies</u>.




### Approach  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yGbdbv)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ilXJ9y)




* <u>Acute gout</u> (<u>gout flare</u>)

* Assess the clinical <u>probability</u> of <u>septic arthritis</u>. 

* If relatively high, perform <u>arthrocentesis</u> for <u>synovial fluid analysis</u>

* Consult orthopedics as needed (see “<u>Diagnosis of septic arthritis</u>” for details).

* Assess the clinical <u>probability</u> of <u>acute gout</u>.  

* If relatively low :

* <u>Arthrocentesis</u> with <u>synovial fluid analysis</u> is recommended.

* Consider <u>differential diagnoses of gout</u>.

* If relatively high : Diagnostic tests are not routinely required.

* <u>Chronic gout</u>: A combination of history (e.g., recurrent episodes), examination and imaging findings (e.g., <u>tophi</u>), and evidence of <u>MSU crystals</u> on aspirates of <u>tophi</u> or <u>synovial fluid analysis</u> confirms the diagnosis of <u>chronic gout</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BGbzYv)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LwbwjD)





### <u>Arthrocentesis</u> and <u>synovial fluid analysis</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yGbdbv)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)




<u>Synovial fluid analysis</u> is the <u>gold standard</u> for diagnosing <u>gout</u> but should only be performed if <u>polarized light microscopy</u> is available and providers are trained in the procedure  and interpretation.




* Indications

* Uncertain <u>clinical diagnosis</u>

* Higher <u>probability</u> of <u>septic arthritis</u>

* Characteristic findings

* <u>Polarized light microscopy</u>: needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals that are negatively <u>birefringent</u> ;    [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xnaEvO)

* Crystals appear yellow when their optical axis is oriented parallel to the polarizer.

* Crystals appear blue when their axis is perpendicular to the polarizer.

* <u>Synovial fluid</u> cell count: <u>WBC</u> > 2000/Î¼L with > 50% <u>neutrophils</u> (see “<u>Interpretation of synovial fluid analysis</u>”)

* <u>Gram stain</u>: negative; useful for ruling out <u>septic arthritis</u>

There is a lack of consensus regarding strict diagnostic criteria for <u>gout</u> in clinical settings. ### <u>Laboratory studies</u>




* Serum <u>uric acid</u> level ;  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JFbsQv)

* <u>Acute gout</u> flare: often elevated (<u>hyperuricemia</u>)  ; may also be normal or low

* Intercritical stage or <u>chronic gout</u>

* Baseline levels are useful to determine the need for <u>urate-lowering therapy</u>.

* A normal or low level has a very low <u>negative likelihood ratio</u> for (but does not rule out) a diagnosis of <u>gout</u>.

* <u>CBC</u> and <u>inflammatory markers</u>: : <u>WBC</u> and <u>ESR</u> are typically elevated in an <u>acute gout</u> attack. [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pxbLxD)

* Urinary <u>uric acid</u> measurement (via <u>24-hour urine collection</u>): not routinely recommended





> [!TIP]
> Serum <u>uric acid</u> levels are not always elevated in <u>acute gouty arthritis</u>.




### Imaging [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yGbdbv)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JFbsQv)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/B9bzpD)




Imaging is indicated if <u>synovial fluid analysis</u> is unsuccessful or cannot be performed, and the diagnosis remains uncertain. It can be used to identify supportive findings of <u>gout</u>  but cannot rule out <u>septic arthritis</u>.




* <u>Ultrasound</u>

* Signs of acute <u>joint</u> inflammation

* Bone <u>erosions</u>

* Signs of <u>urate</u> crystal deposition 

* Double contour sign: a <u>hyperechoic</u> band of crystals covering the surface of the <u>hypoechoic</u> <u>articular cartilage</u>, which is over the <u>hyperechoic</u> bone contour

* <u>Tophi</u> (<u>pathognomonic</u> for <u>chronic gout</u>)

* <u>X-ray</u>

* <u>Acute gout</u>: typically normal; useful for ruling out a <u>fracture</u> in patients with posttraumatic <u>joint</u> inflammation

* <u>Chronic gout</u>

* Punched-out lytic bone lesions with spiky periosteal appositions (overhanging edges), known as “rat-bite <u>erosions</u>”

* <u>Radiopaque</u> in surrounding <u>soft tissue</u>

* <u>Joint</u> space is preserved until late stages.

* Conventional CT and <u>MRI</u>: can identify <u>erosions</u> and <u>tophi</u>; used less frequently

* Dual-energy CT : can detect crystals within deeper anatomical structures (e.g., the <u>spine</u>) and extra-articular sites





> [!TIP]
> <u>Ultrasound</u> or DECT are preferred imaging modalities to detect MSU crystal deposition within affected <u>joints</u>.

## Pathology

### <u>Foreign body granuloma</u>




* Caused by <u>urate</u> crystal deposition in <u>cutis</u> and <u>subcutis</u>

* Findings include:

* <u>Clusters</u> of crystal deposits

* <u>Macrophages</u> in the shape of <u>epithelioid cells</u> or polynuclear <u>giant cells</u>

## Differential diagnoses

* <u>Septic arthritis</u>

* Can occur concomitantly with <u>gout</u> [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qxbCxD)

* <u>Joint</u> damage from <u>chronic gout</u> may increase risk. [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IxbYBD)

* Inflammatory <u>arthritis</u> (other types): See “<u>Differential diagnoses of inflammatory arthritis</u>.”

* <u>Acute calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease</u> (<u>pseudogout</u>)

* <u>Rheumatoid arthritis</u>

* <u>Seronegative spondyloarthropathy</u> (e.g. <u>psoriatic arthritis</u>, <u>reactive arthritis</u>)

* <u>Basic calcium phosphate crystal deposition diseases</u>

* <u>Erosive osteoarthritis</u>




The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Treatment

### General measures  [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)




* Lifestyle modifications may help reduce the risk of flares. 

* Limit <u>alcohol</u> consumption

* Limit intake of <u>purines</u> (e.g., red meat and shellfish)

* Limit high-fructose <u>corn</u> syrup (e.g., sugary foods, juices, and non-diet sodas)

* Weight loss if patient is <u>overweight</u>

* Identify and treat <u>sleep apnea</u>. [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J4Xs4y)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GLXB_A)

* Close management of comorbidities, such as <u>diabetes</u> and <u>hypertension</u>

* Adjust current antihypertensive regimen, if feasible: <u>losartan</u> preferred; avoid <u>hydrochlorothiazide</u>  [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)

## Acute gout flare

### Approach [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_na5DO)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-GbDbv)




* Nonpharmacological measures: Rest and ice the affected <u>joint</u>. [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AnaRDO)

* Pharmacotherapy: Initiate within 24 hours of onset. 

* First-line agents: <u>glucocorticoids</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>, or <u>colchicine</u> (described in detail below)   [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)

* Second-line agents : <u>IL-1</u> inhibitors or <u>ACTH</u>

* Combination therapy options: indicated for attacks involving ≥ 4 <u>joints</u>, > 1 <u>large joint</u>, or severe <u>pain</u>

* <u>Glucocorticoids</u> (any formulation) PLUS <u>colchicine</u>

* <u>NSAIDs</u> PLUS <u>colchicine</u>

* Intraarticular <u>glucocorticoids</u> PLUS <u>NSAIDs</u>

* Consider initiating <u>urate-lowering therapy</u> in select patients (see “Indications” in “<u>Urate-lowering therapy</u>” for details).





![Therapy of acute gout](../../../../../media/bu85wjb54t6zj8poflc5wyi2.jpg)

Therapy of acute gout




> [!TIP]
> If a patient is on long-term <u>urate</u>-lowering therapy, it should be continued during the treatment of an <u>acute gout</u> flare.




> [!WARNING]
> Avoid combining <u>NSAIDs</u> with <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u>. If prescribed together, add a <u>proton pump inhibitor</u> to reduce the risk of <u>gastrointestinal ulcers</u>.




#### <u>Glucocorticoids</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)




* Systemic administration

* Oral (e.g., <u>prednisone</u> DOSAGE or <u>prednisolone</u>;  DOSAGE or equivalent )

* Fixed-dose regimen: Treat for 5 days or until symptom resolution.

* A tapered regimen may reduce the risk of a rebound flare.  [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eBbx-D)

* Parenteral or intramuscular (e.g., <u>methylprednisolone</u>  DOSAGE) if <u>NPO</u>

* Intraarticular administration: : Consider if there are 1–2 <u>joints</u> that are accessible and a trained provider is available.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Glucocorticoids</u> are preferable if there are contraindications (e.g., <u>CKD</u>), intolerance, or inadequate response to <u>colchicine</u> or <u>NSAIDs</u>.




#### <u>NSAIDs</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)




* <u>Naproxen</u>:  DOSAGE  or an alternative (e.g., <u>indomethacin</u>, <u>ibuprofen</u>; see “<u>Pain management</u>” for details)

* Fixed-dose regimen: Treat for shortest duration necessary to resolve symptoms (often at least 3–5 days).

* Tapered regimen  for patients with:

* Hepatic/renal impairment

* Comorbidities

* Severe symptoms

* <u>Selective COX-2 inhibitors</u>: Consider as an alternative to <u>naproxen</u> in patients without contraindications.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Low-dose aspirin</u> can decrease <u>uric acid</u> excretion and trigger recurrent <u>gout</u> flares but it should not be stopped in patients taking it for specific indications (e.g., <u>coronary artery disease</u>, <u>cerebrovascular disease</u>), regardless of the severity of <u>gout</u>. [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RSYlzo)




#### ColchicineDOSAGE [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)




* Mechanism of action: binds and stabilizes <u>tubulin</u> subunits → inhibits <u>microtubule</u> polymerization → inhibits <u>phagocytosis</u> of <u>urate crystals</u>, <u>neutrophil</u> activation, migration, and <u>degranulation</u> [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SiYyIK)

* Administration

* Administer within the first 12 hours of symptom onset to maximize <u>efficacy</u>.

* Reduce dose and monitor closely when prescribed in patients with hepatic impairment or mild-moderate <u>CKD</u>.

* Adverse effects

* Gastrointestinal symptoms, e.g., <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, and abdominal <u>pain</u>, are the most common.

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u> , <u>myopathy</u>

* <u>Polyneuropathy</u> [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uBbpcw)

* Cardiac toxicity, <u>arrhythmias</u>

* <u>Nephrotoxicity</u>

* <u>Myelosuppression</u>

* <u>CNS</u> symptoms (e.g., fatigue, <u>headache</u>)

* Contraindications

* Severe <u>CKD</u>

* Patients on prophylactic <u>colchicine</u> who received an <u>acute gout</u> dose of <u>colchicine</u> in the previous 14 days

* <u>Drug interactions</u>

* <u>Statins</u>: Consider reducing dose of <u>pravastatin</u>, <u>atorvastatin</u>, or <u>simvastatin</u> when prescribed concomitantly.

* Potent <u>cytochrome P450</u> 3A4 substrates or inhibitors

* Reduce <u>colchicine</u> dose when prescribed concomitantly.

* Avoid in patients with <u>CKD</u> or hepatic impairment.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Colchicine</u> is unlikely to be effective when initiated > 24–36 hours after symptom onset. <u>Colchicine</u> is preferable in patients who cannot tolerate <u>NSAIDs</u> or <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u> (e.g., patients with <u>gastrointestinal ulcers</u>)




> [!WARNING]
> Monitor for myotoxicity when prescribing <u>colchicine</u> with <u>statins</u>. Reduce dose of <u>pravastatin</u>, <u>atorvastatin</u>, and <u>simvastatin</u> when prescribed concomitantly.

## Chronic gout

### Approach [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)




* <u>Urate-lowering therapy</u> (<u>ULT</u>) is recommended for <u>chronic gout</u>.

* First-line: <u>xanthine-oxidase inhibitors</u> (<u>allopurinol</u>)

* Second-line: <u>uricosurics</u> (<u>probenecid</u>)

* Third-line: recombinant uricase (<u>pegloticase</u>)

* Administer anti-inflammatory prophylaxis before initiating <u>ULT</u> as <u>ULT</u> may trigger, prolong, or worsen an <u>acute gout</u> flare.





### Initial anti-inflammatory prophylaxis [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_na5DO)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-GbDbv)




* Indication: pre-treatment in patients planned for <u>ULT</u> therapy

* Important consideration: should be initiated 1 week before starting <u>ULT</u> therapy

* Options: low dose of any one of the following agents  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/atbQXv)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_na5DO)

* <u>Colchicine</u> DOSAGE

* <u>NSAIDs</u> (e.g., <u>naproxen</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Glucocorticoids</u> (e.g., <u>prednisone</u> DOSAGE)

* Treatment duration: Should be administered concomitantly with <u>ULT</u> for at least 3–6 months (or longer if flares continue).





> [!TIP]
> Administering <u>XOIs</u> or <u>uricosuric agents</u> during an <u>acute gout</u> flare may worsen symptoms by mobilizing <u>urate crystals</u>. Anti-inflammatory prophylaxis with <u>colchicine</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>, or <u>glucocorticoids</u> must be administered before initiating <u>ULT</u>.




### Urate-lowering therapy (<u>ULT</u>) [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_na5DO)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zGbrbv)




#### Indications




* Absolute indications

* Damage due to <u>chronic gout</u> seen on imaging

* <u>Tophi</u> development

* Frequent <u>gout</u> attacks (≥ 2 per year)

* Relative indications

* < 2 <u>gout</u> attacks per year

* First episode of <u>acute gout</u> flare in patients with any of the following <u>risk factors</u>: 

* <u>CKD</u> ≥ stage 3

* Serum <u>uric acid</u> > 9 mg/dL

* History of <u>urolithiasis</u>





#### Contraindications




* Common to all <u>ULT</u> agents

* <u>Acute gout</u> flare (in the absence of the above-mentioned <u>risk factors</u>)

* Asymptomatic <u>hyperuricemia</u>

* Specific contraindications: see the table below for details





#### Important considerations




* Timing of initiating <u>ULT</u>: at least one week after initiating anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (see above)

* <u>Titration</u> target: serum <u>urate</u> < 6 mg/dL  [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xGbEYv)

* Treatment duration: Continue indefinitely if tolerated and in remission

* Patient adherence: typically low ; monitoring and counseling recommended [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aybQdw)

## Complications

* <u>Nephrolithiasis</u>: <u>uric acid stones</u>

* Uric acid nephropathy

* Acute uric acid nephropathy

* Can occur in <u>tumor lysis syndrome</u>

* Causes tubular obstruction → <u>acute renal failure</u>

* Chronic uric acid nephropathy: a form of chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy with monosodium <u>urate</u> crystal deposition in the stroma of the <u>kidney</u>, which causes inflammation
* Clinical features

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* In rare cases, progressive <u>renal failure</u>




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
