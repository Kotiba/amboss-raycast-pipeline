## Summary

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized by <u>noncaseating granuloma</u> formation, most commonly in the <u>lungs</u> and <u>hilar lymph nodes</u>. Many patients are asymptomatic, especially in the early stages of disease. Symptomatic patients typically present with <u>constitutional symptoms</u>, <u>cough</u>, and <u>dyspnea</u>. The cause of sarcoidosis is unknown but is thought to be related to an environmental exposure in a genetically predisposed individual. <u>Chest x-ray</u> is the preferred initial test for individuals with suspected sarcoidosis since <u>bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy</u> and/or pulmonary involvement is present in ∼ 90% of affected patients. Sarcoidosis is a diagnosis of exclusion that typically requires characteristic clinical features and/or findings on chest imaging, <u>noncaseating granulomas</u> on <u>biopsy</u>, and exclusion of more common causes of granulomatous disease or <u>lymphadenopathy</u>. While most patients with <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u> do not require <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u>, patients with symptomatic or progressive disease are typically treated with <u>glucocorticoids</u>. Spontaneous remission without complications is common in patients with early-stage pulmonary disease, but the risk of irreversible <u>lung</u> <u>fibrosis</u> increases with disease recurrence and progression.

## Pathophysiology

Sarcoidosis is a systemic disorder characterized by widespread, immune-mediated formation of <u>noncaseating granulomas</u>.




* General

* Inhalation of foreign <u>antigen</u> → <u>monocyte</u> differentiation → activation of <u>interstitial</u> <u>dendritic cells</u> and <u>alveolar macrophages</u>

* <u>Interstitial</u> <u>dendritic cells</u> migrate towards <u>mediastinal lymph nodes</u> and <u>alveolar macrophages</u> differentiate, serving as <u>antigen-presenting cells</u> to circulating <u>T helper</u> (Th) cells → differentiation and clonal expansion of <u>T cells</u>

* <u>T cell</u> recruitment and ↑ <u>B-cell</u> activity → local immune hyperactivity and inflammation → formation of <u>noncaseating granulomas</u> in the <u>lungs</u> and <u>the lymphatic system</u>

* <u>Granuloma</u> formation: Mature <u>granulomas</u> are composed of <u>epithelioid cells</u> and <u>macrophages</u> surrounded by <u>lymphocytes</u> and <u>fibroblasts</u>.

* <u>Macrophages</u> activate <u>Th1 cells</u>.

* <u>Th1 cells</u> stimulate the formation of <u>epithelioid cells</u> and <u>multinucleated giant cells</u> by releasing <u>IFN-γ</u>.

* <u>Epithelioid cells</u> produce <u>angiotensin-converting enzyme</u> (<u>ACE</u>) and release <u>cytokines</u> that recruit more <u>immune cells</u>.

* See “<u>Granulomatous inflammation</u>” for more details.

* <u>Fibrosis</u>: <u>Epithelioid cells</u> secrete <u>cytokines</u> to recruit <u>fibroblasts</u>, leading to <u>fibrosis</u> → damage of organs and tissue

* <u>Calcium</u> dysregulation: : activated pulmonary <u>alveolar macrophages</u> → ↑ <u>1-alpha hydroxylase</u> expression and activity → ↑ <u>1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D</u> (<u>calcitriol</u>) → <u>hypervitaminosis D</u> → <u>hyperphosphatemia</u>, <u>hypercalcemia</u>, and, possibly, <u>renal failure</u>  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2m1TUh0)

## Clinical features

### Overview




* Sarcoidosis is a disease with highly variable manifestations, ranging from asymptomatic to progressive, relapsing disease.

* Typically sudden onset with spontaneous remission in approximately two-thirds of patients [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nb1-F20)

* Approx. one-third of patients develop progressive chronic disease.

* Most common presenting signs and symptoms

* Often asymptomatic (in early stages)

* General: <u>fever</u>, <u>malaise</u>, lack of appetite, weight loss, <u>lymphadenopathy</u>

* Pulmonary: <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>cough</u>, <u>chest pain</u>

* Extrapulmonary: <u>arthritis</u>, <u>erythema nodosum</u>, <u>anterior uveitis</u>

Sarcoidosis fact sheet




### Pulmonary sarcoidosis




* <u>Lungs</u> are the most commonly affected organ (90% of affected individuals).

* Often asymptomatic in the early stages

* <u>Interstitial</u> <u>fibrosis</u>

* <u>Cough</u>, <u>exertional dyspnea</u>, <u>chest pain</u>

* Mild <u>rales</u> on <u>pulmonary auscultation</u>





### Extrapulmonary sarcoidosis [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)




* Occurs in approx. 30% of affected individuals

* Extrapulmonary findings usually involve the <u>skin</u>, eyes, <u>heart</u>, and/or the renal and central nervous systems.





#### Cutaneous sarcoidosis [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rX1fz20)




* Occurs in approx. 30% of affected individuals [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* <u>Erythema nodosum</u>: most common nonspecific cutaneous manifestation

* Lupus pernio

* A <u>pathognomonic</u> manifestation of sarcoidosis characterized by extensive, violaceous <u>skin plaques</u> (i.e., <u>epithelioid granulomas of the dermis</u>) on the nose, cheeks, chin, and/or <u>ears</u>

* <u>Butterfly rash</u> as seen in <u>systemic lupus erythematosus</u>

* <u>Papular</u> sarcoidosis 

* A common specific sarcoid cutaneous lesion showing <u>noncaseating granulomas</u> on <u>biopsy</u>

* Multiple scattered or confluent lesions, located on the face (<u>lips</u>, nostrils, eyelids, forehead, and neck hairline)

* <u>Plaque</u>-like lesions 

* Specific sarcoid cutaneous lesions

* Sharply demarcated, <u>scaling</u>, <u>erythematous</u> lesions

* Scar sarcoidosis: <u>papular</u> lesions in preexisting scars (e.g., <u>venipuncture</u> sites) and/or elevation of scars or tattoos

#### <u>Lymph node</u> findings




* Most commonly affected extrapulmonary site

* Occurs in 70–90% of affected individuals

* <u>Mediastinal lymph nodes</u>: bilateral, hilar, and/or paratracheal <u>mediastinal</u> adenopathies (approx. 90% of affected individuals)

* Intraabdominal <u>lymphadenopathy</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)





#### Ocular sarcoidosis




* Occurs in 10–25% of affected individuals [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* Can affect any part of the <u>eye</u> and associated structures

* Common manifestations include <u>uveitis</u>, <u>conjunctival</u> <u>nodules</u>, <u>keratoconjunctivitis sicca</u>

* Sarcoid uveitis 

* Most common ocular manifestation

* Manifests with <u>pain</u>, redness, and <u>photophobia</u>





![Anterior uveitis](../../../../../media/euamwdifk02dd9ygpk46tivj.jpg)

Anterior uveitis




#### Musculoskeletal findings




* Occurs in approx. 10% of affected individuals [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* <u>Arthralgias</u> and <u>arthritis</u>

* <u>Polyarthritis</u> with involvement of the <u>small joints</u> of the hands (resembles <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>)

* Acute <u>oligoarthritis</u> with bilateral involvement of the ankle <u>joints</u>

* Sarcoid myopathy

* Initially asymptomatic with insidious course

* Clinical patterns

* Chronic <u>sarcoid myopathy</u>: progressive, bilateral <u>proximal</u> <u>muscle weakness</u>

* Acute sarcoid <u>myositis</u>: diffuse, bilateral, <u>proximal</u> muscle <u>pain</u> and swelling

* Nodular <u>sarcoid myopathy</u>: one or more tender <u>nodules</u>; typically affects the lower limbs

* Bone lesions

* Hands and feet are most commonly affected.

* Can appear as lytic, <u>sclerotic</u>, or punched-out lesions on <u>x-ray</u>

* <u>Bone biopsy</u> to rule out <u>malignancy</u> and infections





#### Neurosarcoidosis




* Occurs in approx. 5% of affected individuals [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* <u>Cranial nerve</u> palsy (unilateral or bilateral <u>facial nerve palsy</u> is the most common)

* <u>Diabetes insipidus</u>

* <u>Hypopituitarism</u>

* <u>Meningitis</u> (e.g., acute <u>aseptic meningitis</u>, <u>chronic meningitis</u>)

* <u>Peripheral neuropathy</u> (e.g., mononeuropathy, <u>mononeuritis multiplex</u>, <u>polyneuropathies</u>)

* <u>Myopathy</u> (e.g., <u>proximal</u> <u>myopathy</u>, muscle <u>atrophy</u>)





### Other findings [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)




* Cardiac sarcoidosis: : dilated or <u>restrictive cardiomyopathy</u>, <u>myocarditis</u>, <u>pericardial effusion</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u> (e.g., <u>AV block</u>), <u>sudden cardiac death</u>

* <u>Kidneys</u>: <u>acute interstitial nephritis</u>, <u>nephrocalcinosis</u>, <u>nephrolithiasis</u>

* Hepatic sarcoidosis

* Asymptomatic (approx. 80% of affected individuals)

* Common manifestations: hepatic <u>granulomas</u>, <u>hepatomegaly</u> (∼ 30% of affected individuals)

* <u>Spleen</u>: <u>splenomegaly</u> in ∼ 30% of affected individuals

* <u>Exocrine glands</u>: enlarged salivary and <u>lacrimal glands</u>





> [!NOTE]
> Features of sarcoidosis are GRUELING: Granulomas, aRthritis, Uveitis, Erythema nodosum, Lymphadenopathy, Interstitial <u>fibrosis</u>, Negative <u>TB</u> test, and Gammaglobulinemia.

## Subtypes and variants

### Lofgren syndrome [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cw1a3Q0)




* An acute clinical manifestation with <u>fever</u> and the following triad of symptoms:

* Migratory <u>polyarthritis</u>: symmetrical <u>arthritis</u> that primarily affects the ankles

* <u>Erythema nodosum</u>: primarily affects the extensor surface of the lower legs

* <u>Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy</u>

* Prognosis is good (spontaneous remission occurs in 70–80% of affected individuals). [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cw1a3Q0)

### Heerfordt syndrome



* A rare form of sarcoidosis characterized by <u>fever</u> and the following triad of symptoms:

* <u>Parotitis</u>

* <u>Uveitis</u>

* <u>Facial palsy</u>

## Diagnosis

### General principles [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)




* Sarcoidosis is a diagnosis of exclusion supported by:

* Characteristic clinical features and/or findings on chest imaging

* <u>Noncaseating granulomas</u> on <u>biopsy</u>

* Exclusion of more common causes of granulomatous disease or <u>lymphadenopathy</u>

* Obtain initial studies to:

* Rule out differential diagnoses (e.g., other <u>granulomatous diseases</u>).

* Assess pulmonary and extrapulmonary disease (including ophthalmological evaluation).

* Consider additional studies (e.g., <u>biopsy</u>) under specialist guidance based on suspected organ involvement.





> [!TIP]
> Pulmonary involvement and/or hilar <u>lymphadenopathy</u> are present in ∼ 90% of patients. Obtain chest imaging in all patients. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)




> [!TIP]
> The <u>eye</u> and the <u>skin</u> are the most common sites of extrathoracic disease. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)




### Initial studies [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cw1a3Q0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u> and differential: <u>leukocytopenia</u>

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u>: ↑ <u>ESR</u>, <u>CRP</u>

* <u>CMP</u>

* <u>Hypercalcemia</u> (due to elevated 1,25-dihydroxy-<u>vitamin D</u>)

* ↑ <u>BUN</u>, <u>creatinine</u>: may indicate <u>tubulointerstitial disease</u>

* ↑ <u>ALP</u> and/or <u>GGT</u> (> 3 × <u>ULN</u>): may indicate hepatic involvement  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* <u>Urinalysis</u>: <u>hypercalciuria</u>

* 1,25-OH and 25-OH <u>vitamin D</u> levels in patients with <u>hypercalcemia</u>

* ↑ <u>IgG</u>

* <u>TB</u> screening: <u>interferon-γ release assay</u>

* 12-lead <u>ECG</u>: concerning findings include <u>heart block</u>, frequent <u>PVCs</u>, <u>tachyarrhythmias</u>





> [!TIP]
> Increased <u>ACE</u> levels may be seen. However, they are not specific for sarcoidosis and should not be used in isolation for diagnosis or monitoring. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cw1a3Q0)




> [!WARNING]
> <u>Cardiac sarcoidosis</u> can lead to <u>sudden cardiac death</u>. Perform an <u>ECG</u> in all patients with suspected sarcoidosis regardless of symptoms. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)




#### Imaging



* <u>Chest x-ray</u>  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)

* Preferred initial test for all patients with suspected sarcoidosis

* Classic findings: <u>bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy</u> with or without pulmonary infiltrates (i.e., reticular and/or <u>ground glass opacities</u>)

* <u>Pulmonary sarcoidosis</u> is staged according to radiographic findings.




| <br>Radiographic stages of pulmonary sarcoidosis [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)<br> |  |
| --- | --- |
| Stages | <u>Chest x-ray</u> findings |
| Stage 0 | <br>* Normal<br><br> |
| Stage I | <br>* Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy: bilateral enlargement of the <u>lymph nodes</u> of the <u>pulmonary hila</u> without pulmonary infiltrates<br><br> |
| Stage II | <br>* <u>Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy</u> with pulmonary infiltrates<br><br> |
| Stage III | <br>* Pulmonary infiltrates without <u>bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy</u><br><br> |
| Stage IV | <br>* Substantial <u>pulmonary fibrosis</u><br><br> |





> [!TIP]
> Stage I <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u> is often an incidental finding detected on chest imaging. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)



* <u>High-resolution CT</u> (<u>HRCT</u>) [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)

* Obtain an <u>HRCT</u> in all patients with confirmed or suspected <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u>.

* Findings

* Extensive hilar and <u>mediastinal</u> <u>lymphadenopathy</u>

* Bronchocentric and perilymphatic micronodules (i.e., bronchovascular beading)

* <u>Peribronchial thickening</u>

* <u>Fibrosis</u>




> [!TIP]
> Patients with <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u> may have minimal to no symptoms despite significant radiographic abnormalities.

Sarcoidosis




#### <u>Pulmonary function testing</u> (<u>PFTs</u>) [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)




Obtain <u>PFTs</u> to assess disease severity in patients with <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u>.




* <u>Spirometry</u>

* Typically shows a restrictive pattern

* Obstructive and mixed patterns may also be seen. (See “<u>Obstructive vs. restrictive lung disease</u>.”)

* <u>Diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide</u> (<u>DLCO</u>) is decreased.





> [!TIP]
> <u>PFTs</u> show an early reduction of <u>DLCO</u> and <u>lung compliance</u>.




### Additional studies [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)




Consider the following studies under specialist guidance based on suspected organ involvement:




* <u>Bronchoalveolar lavage</u> (<u>BAL</u>):↑ <u>CD4:CD8 ratio</u>  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cw1a3Q0)

* <u>TTE</u> for <u>pulmonary hypertension</u> screening if ↓ <u>DLCO</u> or if enlarged <u>pulmonary artery</u> is present on <u>HRCT</u>

* <u>Cardiac MRI</u> and/or cardiac <u>FDG-PET</u> if there are concerning findings on 12-lead <u>ECG</u> or suspected <u>cardiac sarcoidosis</u>

* <u>Cross-sectional imaging</u> (abdomen) to assess for splenic involvement if <u>CBC</u> demonstrates <u>cytopenias</u>





### <u>Biopsy</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)




* <u>Gold standard</u> for the diagnosis

* Tissue should be obtained from the most easily accessible lesions (e.g., cutaneous lesions, <u>superficial</u> <u>lymph nodes</u>).

* Request fungal and <u>mycobacterial</u> staining and culture to rule out differential diagnoses. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)

* <u>Histopathology</u> findings

* <u>Noncaseating granulomas</u> with <u>giant cells</u>

* <u>Asteroid bodies</u>

* <u>Schaumann bodies</u>





> [!TIP]
> Histopathological confirmation is not required in patients with <u>pathognomonic</u> presentations, i.e., <u>Lofgren syndrome</u>, <u>Heerfordt syndrome</u>, and <u>lupus pernio</u>. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)

## Treatment

### General principles [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HX1Kz20)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)




* Consult a pulmonologist and/or other specialists as required.

* Most patients with <u>pulmonary sarcoidosis</u> do not require <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u>.

* <u>Glucocorticoids</u> with/without <u>DMARDs</u> are indicated for patients with any of the following:

* Symptomatic progressive disease

* Persistent pulmonary infiltration

* Progressive decline of <u>lung function</u>

* <u>Organ transplantation</u> (e.g., <u>lung</u>, <u>heart</u>, <u>liver</u>) may be indicated in patients with severe, refractory disease.





### <u>Immunosuppressive therapy</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sX1tz20)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)




* First-line: <u>glucocorticoids</u>, e.g., <u>prednisone</u> DOSAGE [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1w123Q0)

* If no response or adverse effects to <u>glucocorticoids</u>: <u>DMARDs</u> may be added.

* <u>Methotrexate</u> (preferred choice) or <u>azathioprine</u> (alone or with <u>glucocorticoids</u>)

* <u>Chloroquine</u> or <u>hydroxychloroquine</u>  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)

* <u>Biologics</u> (e.g., <u>TNF-alpha inhibitors</u>)  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)





### Supportive therapy [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mD1VUQ0)




* <u>Prevent complications of glucocorticoid therapy</u> and <u>adverse effects of immunosuppressants</u>.

* Provide individualized <u>supportive care</u> (e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u> for <u>pain</u> relief, <u>noninvasive ventilation</u>, <u>supplemental oxygen</u>).

## Complications

* Individuals with sarcoidosis have an increased risk of <u>malignancy</u> (especially <u>lung cancer</u> and malignant <u>lymphomas</u>).

* Pulmonary complications 

* <u>Bronchiectasis</u>

* <u>Pulmonary hypertension</u> (<u>PH</u>) due to <u>pulmonary fibrosis</u>

* <u>Aspergilloma</u>

* <u>Chronic renal failure</u>  (see “Clinical features” above)

## Prognosis

* Increased <u>calcium</u> indicates renal involvement with the risk of <u>chronic renal failure</u>.

* Remission rate according to staging [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cTXapx)

* Stage I: approx. 70%

* Stage II: approx. 50%

* Stage III: approx. 20%

* Stage IV: <u>Life expectancy</u> is limited because of severely impaired <u>lung function</u>.
