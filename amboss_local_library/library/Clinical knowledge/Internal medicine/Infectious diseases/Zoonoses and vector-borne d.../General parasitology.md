## Summary

<u>Parasites</u> are <u>symbiotic</u> organisms that live on or in a larger organism (host) in order to feed, develop, and/or multiply, causing harm to the host in the process. <u>Parasitism</u> is distinguished from <u>commensalism</u>, in which the symbiont benefits from the relationship without harming or benefiting the host in return, and <u>mutualism</u>, in which the symbiont and the host equally benefit from the relationship. A <u>definitive host</u> is a host in whom a <u>parasite</u> reaches its adult form and/or undergoes a sexual phase of development; an <u>intermediate host</u> is a host in whom a <u>parasite</u> passes one or more phases of its asexual development. Intermediate hosts often function as <u>vectors</u> that carry the <u>parasite</u> from one host to the next, but <u>parasites</u> themselves can also function as <u>vectors</u> for <u>viruses</u>, bacteria, and other <u>parasites</u>. <u>Parasites</u> can be classified by size as microparasites (unicellular) or macroparasites (multicellular); by the class of organism as <u>protozoans</u>, <u>helminths</u>, and <u>arthropods</u>; by dependency on a host for survival as obligate (require a <u>definitive host</u> and may go through one or more intermediate hosts to complete their life cycle) or facultative (do not rely on a host to complete their life cycle but may adopt parasitic activity); and by their relation to the host as <u>endoparasites</u>, which “infect” the host and live inside their body (mostly <u>protozoa</u> and <u>helminths</u>), and <u>ectoparasites</u>, which “infest” the host and attach to, burrow into, or temporarily feed off a host's <u>integumentary system</u> (mostly <u>arthropod</u> macroparasites). <u>Arthropod</u> <u>infestations</u> are clinically significant in their own right, but play a far greater role in the transmission of other pathogens (e.g., <u>Plasmodium</u> by mosquitoes and <u>Borrelia</u> by ticks).

## Basics of parasitology

### Symbiosis




* Definition: a long-term interaction between dissimilar species living together in biological association

* Depending on the degree of dependence and the advantages or disadvantages derived from the relationship, <u>symbiosis</u> can be classified as:

* Parasitism

* A relationship between a symbiont (<u>parasite</u>) and its host, in which the <u>parasite</u> benefits from the relationship and the host is harmed, possibly even causing <u>death</u>

* Examples: <u>tapeworms</u>, head <u>lice</u>, <u>Plasmodium</u> spp.

* Commensalism

* A relationship between a symbiont (<u>commensal</u>) and its host, in which the <u>commensal</u> benefits from the relationship and the host derives neither benefit nor harm

* Some organisms can be <u>commensal</u> under certain conditions (e.g., as intestinal or <u>skin</u> <u>microbiota</u> in a healthy individual) but become pathogenic if these conditions change (e.g., host <u>immune deficiency</u>, <u>colonization</u> of other sites, changes in temperature or <u>humidity</u>).

* Example: E. Coli, which is part of the physiological intestinal flora, but can cause infections when it colonizes, for example, the <u>urinary tract</u>

* Mutualism

* A relationship between a symbiont and its host that benefits both parties

* Like <u>commensals</u>, <u>mutualism</u> can become pathogenic under certain conditions.

* Example: <u>symbiosis</u> between humans and intestinal <u>Enterobacter</u> agglomerans, in which the bacteria benefit from the <u>nutrient</u> supply and the human benefits from the menaquinones (<u>vitamin K</u>) that the bacteria produce





### Classification of <u>parasites</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uqXpZz)




<u>Parasites</u> can be categorized by location, dependence on host for survival, size, and type of organism.




* Location

* Ectoparasites: live and/or feed on the <u>integumentary system</u> of the host (e.g., ticks, mosquitoes)

* Endoparasites: live inside the host (e.g., <u>helminths</u>)

* Class of organism

* <u>Protozoan</u>

* <u>Helminth</u>

* <u>Arthropod</u>

* Host dependence

* Obligate parasites: cannot complete their life cycle without a host (e.g., <u>Plasmodium</u>, <u>Toxoplasma</u>)

* Facultative parasites: do not require a host to complete their life cycle and adopt parasitic behavior only if necessary or opportunity presents itself (e.g., <u>Naegleria fowleri</u>)

* Size

* Macroparasite: multicellular organisms

* Microparasite: unicellular organisms





### Life cycle [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)




The life-cycle of <u>obligate parasites</u> can be divided into a parasitic stage, during which the organism lives in or feeds on the host, and a free-living stage, during which it lives outside the host and is not dependent on the host. Transmission generally occurs during the free-living stage.




* Microparasites (<u>protozoa</u>):
* The life cycles of microparasites vary greatly from species to species, but for most clinically relevant <u>protozoans</u> a simple division into two stages applies:

* <u>Trophozoite</u>

* Cyst

* <u>Helminths</u>

* <u>Ovum</u>

* Larva

* Adult

* <u>Arthropods</u>

* <u>Ovum</u>

* Larva

* <u>Nymph</u>/<u>pupa</u>

* Adult

* Some <u>arthropods</u> adopt parasitic behavior only during specific phases of development (e.g., adult female mosquitoes, who require a blood meal to produce eggs, or ticks, who require a blood meal to transition from one stage of development to the next)





#### Diagnostic and <u>infective stage</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EqX8Zz)




* For clinical purposes, the life cycle of <u>endoparasites</u> can be divided into two stages:

* Infective stage: the stage during which the <u>parasite</u> assumes a form in which it can invade its host.

* Diagnostic stage: the stage during which the <u>parasite</u> can be detected using the naked <u>eye</u> or <u>laboratory methods</u>. The <u>diagnostic stage</u> typically coincides with the stage during which the <u>parasite</u> leaves the host (via stool, urine, or <u>sputum</u>) to proliferate.

* <u>Protozoans</u> are typically infective while encysted and noninfective while in <u>trophozoite</u> form, while <u>helminths</u> are typically infective in the oval or larval form and noninfective as adults. However, the <u>diagnostic stage</u> varies greatly between the species of both classes.





### Hosts and <u>vectors</u>




#### Host




A host is an organism that harbors a smaller parasitic, <u>commensal</u>, or mutualistic organism. The life cycle of any <u>obligate parasite</u> involves at least one host. The <u>parasite</u> uses a host for nutrition, protection, and/or conditions for reproduction.




* Reservoir host

* A host (e.g., dogs and cats for <u>Leishmania</u>) that can carry the <u>pathogen</u> indefinitely without any ill effects (i.e., asymptomatic/<u>latent infection</u>)

* Provides an ecological niche for the <u>pathogen</u> when active transmission to a definite or <u>intermediate host</u> is not possible

* Transport host: a host that mechanically transfers a <u>parasite</u> to another host (e.g., rodents that transport certain <u>helminths</u> to cats and dogs)

* Dead-end host: a host that can be affected by a <u>parasite</u> but cannot act as a <u>vector</u> (e.g., humans for the <u>West Nile virus</u>)

* Intermediate host: a host in which the <u>parasite</u> undergoes one or several asexual phases of its development (e.g., humans for <u>Plasmodium</u>)

* Definitive host: a host in which a <u>parasite</u> undergoes development into its adult form and, in some cases, undergoes the sexual phase of its development (e.g., humans for <u>Wuchereria bancrofti</u>)





#### Vector




<u>Vectors</u> are organisms that can carry a <u>pathogen</u> and transmit it to another organism. They can be intermediate or <u>transport hosts</u>.




### Pathological effects of <u>parasites</u>




* Direct

* Mechanical destruction of tissue

* Production of substances (e.g., debris, feces) that can cause <u>allergic reactions</u>/diseases

* Metabolic/nutritional interference (e.g., <u>tapeworms</u> such as <u>Diphyllobothrium latum</u> can cause <u>vitamin B<sub>12</sub></u> deficiencies by interfering with the <u>vitamin B<sub>12</sub></u>-intrinsic factor complex)

* Indirect: <u>immune response</u> to the <u>parasite</u>

## Overview of protozoa

### Definition




Protozoa are microscopic, single-celled nucleated organisms with complex life cycles, involving multiple stages and forms of development. <u>Protozoa</u> can cause a variety of gastrointestinal, <u>visceral</u>, hematological, neurological, genitourinary, and ocular diseases.




### Classification




Clinically significant <u>protozoa</u> can be classified into four groups, depending on their means of movement.




* Mastigophora (flagellates)

* Means of motion: <u>flagella</u>

* Examples: Giardia, <u>Trichomonas</u>, <u>Leishmania</u>, Trypanosoma

* Sarcodina (amoeba)

* Means of motion: <u>pseudopodia</u> formed by cell protoplasm

* Examples: Entamoeba, <u>Acanthamoeba</u>, <u>Naegleria</u>

* Ciliophora (ciliates)

* Means of motion: <u>cilia</u>

* Example: Balantidium

* <u>Sporozoa</u>

* Means of motion: gliding motility

* Possess an apical complex that allows them to enter the host cells

* Examples: <u>Plasmodium</u>, <u>Toxoplasma</u>, <u>Babesia</u>

### Overview [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nUX7dx)




* Population at risk

* Travelers to the <u>endemic</u> regions

* Individuals with close contact to persons with a recent history of living in <u>endemic</u> regions (e.g., migrant workers, recently immigrated individuals)

* Individuals in crowded conditions

* Transmission

* <u>Intestinal protozoa</u> may also be transmitted sexually to the insertive partner during anal intercourse.

* Diagnostics

* Stool analysis

* Immunoassay to detect <u>parasite</u> <u>antigens</u>

#### Hemoflagellates [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nUX7dx)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eCXxIZ0)




All hemoflagellates have similar morphological stages.




* Trypomastigote

* Elongated S- or C-shape

* Undulating membrane runs along one side of the body

* <u>Flagellum</u> at the <u>anterior</u> end

* Amastigote: round or oval with a peripherally located <u>nucleus</u>

* Epimastigote: resembles <u>trypomastigotes</u>, but smaller and with undulating membrane only along half of one side of the body

* Promastigote: resembles <u>epimastigote</u> without the undulating membrane

## Urogenital protozoa

### <u>Trichomonas vaginalis</u>




* Taxonomy: flagellate

* Life cycle: only has <u>trophozoite</u> stage (cannot live outside a host); <u>trophozoite</u> morphology is characterized by the following features:  

* Ovoid shape

* Undulating membrane extending half of the body length

* One <u>nucleus</u>

* Three to five <u>flagella</u> anteriorly and one posteriorly

* Other properties: motile, anaerobic

* Transmission: sexual

* Disease: <u>trichomoniasis</u>

* Diagnosis: detection of motile <u>trophozoites</u> of <u>T. vaginalis</u> on <u>wet mount preparation</u>

* Treatment: oral <u>metronidazole</u> or <u>tinidazole</u>

## Cestodes (tapeworms)

* <u>Cestodes</u> (<u>tapeworms</u>) are long, flat, ribbon-like worms composed of numerous segments and a single scolex at the head with which they anchor themselves to the intestine.

* Since they do not have a digestive tract, all <u>nutrients</u> are absorbed through the tegument.

* <u>Cestodes</u> are hermaphroditic (they contain both male and female organs).

* See "<u>Overview of cestode infections</u>."

## Arthropods

### General characteristics




* Overview

* Arthropods are a group of <u>ectoparasites</u> that comprises arachnids and insects.

* Feed on the blood, <u>skin</u> cells and oils, and/or organic debris of their hosts

* Infestation is a disease in its own right, but the role of <u>ectoparasites</u> as <u>vectors</u> for other diseases is generally more clinically significant.

* Morphological features

* Common features include a chitin exoskeleton and segmented bodies with paired appendages (e.g., legs, wings, antennae)

* Life cycle involves several stages and may involve molting and metamorphosis.
* Most <u>arthropods</u> undergo a transformation from larva to an adult organism through several life stages characterized by distinct morphology.

* No metamorphosis: egg → adult

* Simple metamorphosis: egg → <u>nymph</u> → adult

* Complete metamorphosis: egg → larva → <u>pupa</u>/<u>nymph</u> → adult

## Insects

### Lice [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)




For more information on the various species of <u>lice</u>, see “<u>Lice infestation</u>.”




* Examples

* <u>Pediculus humanus capitis</u>

* <u>Pediculus humanus corporis</u>

* <u>Pthirus pubis</u>

* Life cycle

* <u>Adult lice</u> lay eggs (nits) at the base of the <u>hair shaft</u>.

* Hatched larvae undergo three <u>nymph</u> stages (N1–N3) before reaching adulthood after 3 to 4 weeks.

* <u>Lice</u> can be transmitted through direct contact with the host and their personal belongings (e.g., combs, clothes, bedsheets).

* Associated conditions

* <u>Infestation</u> causes <u>pediculosis</u>.

* <u>Pediculus humanus corporis</u> also serves as a <u>vector</u> for the following pathogens:

* <u>Bartonella quintana</u>: causes <u>trench fever</u>

* <u>Rickettsia prowazekii</u>: causes <u>epidemic typhus</u>

* <u>Borrelia recurrentis</u>: causes <u>louse-borne relapsing fever</u>

### Fleas [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)




* Examples 

* Xenopsylla cheopis

* Tunga penetrans (jigger <u>flea</u>)

* Life cycle

* Usually involves 4 stages of development: egg → larva → <u>pupa</u> → adult

* After adult <u>fleas</u> have found a host and have taken a blood meal, they mate and lay eggs

* Hatching of eggs takes 1–10 day

* Larvae feed on blood and <u>flea</u> feces (“<u>flea</u> dirt”) and will spin a cocoon within 5–20 days to enter the <u>pupa</u> stage

* After several days/weeks, adult <u>fleas</u> are ready to emerge from the cocoon following the detection of a host (e.g., via movement or body heat)

* Associated conditions

* A <u>flea</u> bite can cause <u>pruritus</u> and dermatitis and may be followed by secondary bacterial infections

* Can serve as <u>vectors</u> for the following pathogens:

* <u>Rickettsia</u> typhi, <u>Rickettsia</u> felis

* <u>Yersinia pestis</u>

* <u>Hymenolepis nana</u>

### Flies [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bJXHs_)




Parasitic flies are clinically relevant mainly as <u>vectors</u> for pathogens, which they transmit during a blood meal.




* Main types of parasitic flies

* <u>Tsetse</u> flies (e.g., <u>Glossina</u> spp.)

* Sandflies (e.g., Phlebotomus spp.)

* Black flies (e.g., Simulium spp.)

* Deer flies (e.g., Chrysops spp.)

* Botflies (e.g., Dermatobia hominis)

* Associated conditions

* Flies serve as <u>vectors</u> for a range of pathogens (see the table below).

* Myiasis: <u>infestation</u> of human tissue by fly larvae

* Female fly lays eggs onto a hematophagous insect, which acts as <u>intermediate host</u>, or onto the host directly (e.g., into uncovered wounds). → The larvae hatch and penetrate the <u>skin</u>, digging tunnels into the host's <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>. → Once mature, the larvae drop from host to pupate.

* D. hominis is the only species of fly that routinely parasitizes humans, causing <u>myiasis</u>.

* However, other species of the family Oestridae (botflies) as well as the families Calliphoridae (blowflies) and Sarcophagidae (fleshflies) may infest humans if the opportunity presents itself (e.g., uncovered wounds).

* Obligatory <u>myiasis</u> is most common in species found in Central and South America and sub-Saharan Africa, where humans are most commonly affected.

* Treatment involves the removal of larvae and <u>surgical debridement</u> of the wound.

* Complications: Bacterial infection, potentially leading to <u>sepsis</u>, is common.

### Heteroptera




* Examples

* Triatoma spp. (<u>kissing bug</u>): found predominantly in Central and South America

* <u>Cimex lectularius</u> (<u>bed bug</u>)

* Populates facilities with a high guest turnaround (e.g., hostels, correctional facilities)

* Feeds on human blood

* Life cycle: simple metamorphosis

* Associated conditions

* <u>Bed bug</u> bites can cause a pruritic <u>maculopapular rash</u>.

* Triatoma serve as <u>vectors</u> for <u>Trypanosoma cruzi</u>, which causes <u>Chagas disease</u>
