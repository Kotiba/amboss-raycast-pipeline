## Summary

Zoonotic diseases are infections that are transmitted from animals to humans. While animals may transmit infection directly (e.g., via saliva) or indirectly (e.g., via contaminated objects), they usually serve as hosts for a <u>pathogen</u> that is then transmitted to humans by a <u>vector</u> (e.g., ticks, <u>fleas</u>) or via contaminated water or food. Zoonoses are usually <u>endemic</u> to certain geographical regions; peaks in <u>incidence</u> often correlate with the life cycle of the transmitting <u>vector</u>. Some examples of zoonotic diseases in the US are <u>zoonotic influenza</u>, <u>salmonellosis</u>, <u>West Nile virus</u>, <u>plague</u>, <u>coronavirus</u> infection, <u>rabies</u>, <u>brucellosis</u>, and <u>Lyme disease</u>. Although these conditions differ in their exact presentation, symptomatic cases typically present with <u>fever</u>, <u>flulike symptoms</u>, and possibly <u>skin</u> rashes. In some cases of fulminant disease, there may be complications such as <u>disseminated intravascular coagulation</u>, <u>shock</u>, and organ failure. Most zoonoses are treated with <u>antibiotics</u> and respond well to treatment.
