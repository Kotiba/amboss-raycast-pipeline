## Summary

Prostatitis is an inflammation of the <u>prostate gland</u> that is of either infectious (<u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u> and <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u>) or noninfectious (<u>chronic pelvic pain syndrome</u>) origin. Bacterial prostatitis is most often caused by <u>Escherichia coli</u>. Patients with <u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u> typically present with spiking <u>fevers</u>, chills, perineal <u>pain</u>, and symptoms of <u>bladder</u> irritation. The presentation of <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u> is more subtle, including symptoms of <u>urinary tract infections</u> such as <u>dysuria</u> and increased <u>urinary frequency</u> and urgency. The <u>prostate</u> is typically tender and boggy in <u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u> and mildly tender or normal in <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u>. For bacterial prostatitis, diagnosis aims to identify the causative organism via <u>urine culture</u>, and <u>empirical antibiotic treatment</u> is the primary approach. Acute prostatitis can lead to life-threatening complications (e.g., <u>acute urinary retention</u>, <u>prostatic abscess</u> formation, <u>sepsis</u>) that may require additional treatment, such as <u>suprapubic catheterization</u> or <u>ultrasound</u>-guided <u>abscess</u> drainage. <u>Chronic pelvic pain syndrome</u> (<u>CPPS</u>) is characterized by chronic urogenital <u>pain</u> without evidence of <u>urinary tract infection</u>. Affected individuals may also experience <u>lower urinary tract symptoms</u> and <u>erectile dysfunction</u>. The etiology is unknown. A multimodal treatment approach with a symptomatic focus is recommended.

## Epidemiology

* Common urologic diagnosis in men < 50 years of age [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5Rain4)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MRaMn4)

* In men, there is an ∼ 8% lifetime risk of developing prostatitis.

* Bacterial prostatitis (2–5% of cases): most commonly men between  20 and 50 years of age

* <u>Chronic pelvic pain syndrome</u> (90–95% of cases): primarily men between 40 and 60 years of age

## Etiology

### Acute prostatitis [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)




* <u>E. coli</u> (most common)

* Other <u>bacteria that cause UTI</u>

* <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u> (e.g., <u>C. trachomatis</u> and <u>N. gonorrhoeae</u>)

* Rarer pathogens: <u>mycobacteria</u>, fungi, <u>staphylococci</u>, <u>streptococci</u>





### Chronic prostatitis




* Bacterial

* <u>E. coli</u>

* Other <u>Enterobacteriaceae</u>

* Nonbacterial

* <u>Immune response</u> to a prior <u>UTI</u>

* Nerve damage in the <u>pelvic</u> region

* Chemical irritation (chemical prostatitis)

* <u>Pelvic floor</u> muscle dysfunction

* Parasitic or viral infections





#### Other causes for acute or <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u>




* Other genitourinary tract infections (e.g., <u>urethritis</u>, <u>cystitis</u>, <u>epididymitis</u>)

* Genitourinary tract interventions (e.g., indwelling catheter, transurethral <u>surgery</u>, <u>prostate biopsy</u>)

* Voiding dysfunction and <u>bladder outlet obstruction</u>

## Subtypes and variants

### Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (<u>CPPS</u>) [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dC1oIQ0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WC1PIQ0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nia-74)




* Definition: chronic urogenital <u>pain</u> with no evidence of <u>urinary tract infection</u>

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: 90–95% of prostatitis cases

* Etiology

* Exact etiology unknown

* Possible factors: infection, inflammation, autoimmune processes, neurological damage

* Clinical features

* <u>Lower urinary tract symptoms</u>

* See “<u>Overview of clinical features of bacterial prostatitis and chronic pelvic pain syndrome</u>.”

* Diagnostics

* <u>CPPS</u> is a diagnosis of exclusion.

* Evaluate for bacterial prostatitis.

* Consult urology for further testing, e.g.:

* <u>CPPS</u>-specific assessments

* Imaging (e.g., <u>transrectal ultrasound</u>, <u>pelvic</u> CT and/or <u>MRI</u>)

* Semen culture and analysis

* <u>Urodynamic studies</u>

* <u>Cystoscopy</u>

* Management

* Multimodal approach combining pharmacological and supportive treatment

* Pharmacological treatment options

* <u>Alpha blockers</u> (e.g., <u>tamsulosin</u>, <u>doxazosin</u>)

* <u>5-alpha-reductase inhibitors</u> (e.g., <u>finasteride</u>)

* <u>Antibiotics</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotics for prostatitis</u>.”)

* Additional options: <u>anticonvulsants</u> (e.g., <u>pregabalin</u>), <u>tricyclic antidepressants</u> (e.g., <u>nortriptyline</u>)

* Supportive treatment options

* <u>Cognitive behavioral therapy</u>

* <u>Physical therapy</u>

* <u>Biofeedback</u>

* Sacral neurostimulation

* Thermal therapy

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)




* Bacterial prostatitis is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> based on history and <u>physical examination</u>, including <u>digital rectal examination</u> (<u>DRE</u>).

* Obtain urine studies to support the diagnosis and identify the causative <u>pathogen</u>.

* Consider additional studies based on individual risk, e.g., <u>STI testing</u>, <u>laboratory studies</u>, and imaging.

* Consult urology as needed for specific diagnostic tests (e.g., <u>fractional urine examination</u>).





> [!WARNING]
> Exercise special caution when performing <u>DRE</u> in patients with suspected <u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u> to avoid causing <u>bacteremia</u>. When in doubt, consult a urologist.




### Urine studies [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)




* <u>Urinalysis</u> (<u>midstream urine</u>);  may show characteristic <u>urinalysis findings of UTI</u> (e.g., ↑ <u>WBC</u>).

* Urine <u>Gram stain</u> may be used to visualize bacteria.

* <u>Urine culture</u>: <u>E. coli</u> is most common <u>pathogen</u> (approx. 80% of cases). [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MRaMn4)





### <u>STI testing</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* Indications

* Sexually active patients < 35 years old

* Adults of any age with non-age-related <u>STI risk factors</u>

* Test: <u>NAAT</u> for <u>C. trachomatis</u> and <u>N. gonorrhoeae</u> via urine or urethral swab





### Additional evaluation




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: patients at risk of complications (e.g., age > 65 years, <u>febrile</u>, <u>BPH</u>, <u>transurethral catheterization</u>)  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

* <u>BMP</u>: patients with suspected <u>UTO</u> and/or <u>acute kidney injury</u>

* <u>Blood cultures</u>: patients with <u>signs of sepsis</u>

* Imaging

* <u>Transrectal ultrasound</u>: patients with suspected <u>prostatic abscess</u>  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

* <u>Bladder ultrasound</u>: patients with signs of <u>lower UTO</u> (to assess for <u>postvoid residual volume</u>)

* Localization tests for <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u> (based on <u>fractional urine examination</u>)

* Two-glass test (preferred): cultures of two urine samples obtained before and after <u>prostatic</u> massage  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dC1oIQ0)

* Four-glass test (historical): cultures of four urine samples obtained before, during, and after <u>prostate</u> massage  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dC1oIQ0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WC1PIQ0)





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Prostate-specific antigen</u> may be significantly elevated in patients with acute prostatitis but should not be used for diagnostic purposes. Perform an evaluation for <u>prostate cancer</u> if <u>PSA</u> remains elevated 8 weeks after treatment. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

## Treatment

### Empiric antibiotics for prostatitis [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WC1PIQ0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)




Initiate empiric therapy as soon as possible based on suspected etiology, duration, severity, and local resistance patterns (consult local <u>antibiogram</u> if available).




#### Mild acute and chronic infections




* Options

* Oral <u>fluoroquinolones</u> (e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u>; , DOSAGE <u>levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE) (<u>off-label</u> for acute prostatitis) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

* <u>Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole</u> DOSAGE (<u>off-label</u> for both acute and chronic prostatitis) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

* Alternative options for <u>chronic bacterial prostatitis</u> include <u>macrolides</u> (e.g., <u>azithromycin</u>) and <u>tetracyclines</u> (e.g., <u>doxycycline</u>). [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WC1PIQ0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5C1isQ0)

* Duration

* 2–4 weeks for mild acute infections

* 4–12 weeks for chronic infections

* Suspected <u>STI</u>: <u>Ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE followed by <u>doxycycline</u> DOSAGE) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)





#### Severe acute infections  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5C1isQ0)




* Options (<u>beta-lactam</u> and <u>aminoglycoside</u> use is <u>off-label</u>)  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5C1isQ0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RSWlzk0)

* IV <u>fluoroquinolones</u> (e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u> DOSAGE OR <u>levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE) with or without an <u>aminoglycoside</u> (e.g., <u>gentamicin</u> DOSAGE)  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)

* IV <u>beta-lactam</u> (e.g., <u>piperacillin/tazobactam</u> DOSAGE OR <u>ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE ) with or without an <u>aminoglycoside</u> (e.g., <u>gentamicin</u> DOSAGE)  [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RSWlzk0)

* Duration: 4–6 weeks





### Further management




* Adjust <u>antibiotics</u> to culture results.

* Repeat <u>urine culture</u> ≥ 1 week after the end of treatment to ensure resolution. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)

* Evaluate for <u>prostatic abscess</u> if no improvement in 36 hours and arrange drainage if present. [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RSWlzk0)





### <u>Supportive care</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)




* <u>NSAIDs</u> for <u>pain</u> and inflammation

* <u>Alpha blockers</u> (e.g., <u>tamsulosin</u>) for <u>lower urinary tract symptoms</u>

* Catheterization for <u>acute urinary retention</u>

* Short-term: <u>urethral catheterization</u>

* Long-term: <u>suprapubic catheterization</u>

* <u>Cognitive behavioral therapy</u> and/or <u>physiotherapy</u> for reduction of chronic prostatitis symptoms

* <u>Counseling on STI prevention</u>





### Disposition [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D9a1p5)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/idXJpC)




* Consider inpatient management for patients with any of the following:

* <u>Signs of sepsis</u>

* <u>Urinary retention</u>

* <u>Risk factors</u> for <u>antibiotic</u> resistance (e.g., recent <u>fluoroquinolone</u> therapy)

* Oral treatment unsuccessful, contraindicated, or not tolerated

* Consider urology consult for all patients, especially those with chronic or severe acute prostatitis.

## Complications

* Prostatic abscess

* Clinical features: genitourinary symptoms similar to <u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u>

* Rectal exam finding: <u>fluctuant</u> <u>prostate</u>

* Treatment: <u>antibiotics</u> and <u>transrectal ultrasound</u>-guided drainage

* <u>Acute urinary retention</u>

* <u>Pyelonephritis</u> and <u>sepsis</u>

* <u>Epididymitis</u>




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
