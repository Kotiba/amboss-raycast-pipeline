# Intravascular catheter-related bloodstream infections

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Infectious diseases > Fever and sepsis > Intravascular catheter-related bloodstream infections*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/g70FOh)

---

## Summary

<u>Intravascular</u> catheter-related <u>bloodstream infection</u> (CRBSI) is a <u>primary bloodstream infection</u> that is attributable to the presence of an <u>intravascular</u> catheter, typically a <u>central venous catheter</u> or an <u>arterial catheter</u>. Peripheral intravenous catheters (with or without <u>suppurative thrombophlebitis</u>) are rarely responsible for <u>bloodstream infections</u>. <u>Coagulase-negative staphylococci</u> and <u>Staphylococcus aureus</u> are the most common causative pathogens; however, <u>gram-negative bacilli</u> and <u>Candida</u> spp. are also frequently responsible, especially in critically ill patients and those with femoral <u>intravascular</u> catheters. CRBSI can manifest with <u>fever</u> with or without <u>features of sepsis</u> and evidence of infection at the catheter insertion site (e.g., localized <u>erythema</u>, induration, <u>exudate</u>). Isolation of the same <u>pathogen</u> on cultures obtained from at least 2 different sites (e.g., <u>blood cultures</u> from a peripheral <u>venipuncture</u> and the suspected infected catheter) confirms the diagnosis. <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> should be initiated as soon as appropriate cultures are obtained. Inadequate response to <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> should prompt evaluation for <u>complications of CRBSI</u> (e.g., <u>infective endocarditis</u>, <u>suppurative thrombophlebitis</u>) and typically necessitates removal of the catheter and prolonged <u>antibiotic therapy</u>.




See also “<u>Sepsis</u>,” “<u>Bacteremia</u>,” “<u>Blood cultures</u>,” “<u>Device-related infections</u>,” and “<u>Hospital-acquired infections</u>.”

## Definitions

* <u>Intravascular</u> catheter-related <u>bloodstream infection</u> (CRBSI): <u>primary BSI</u> in a patient with an <u>intravascular</u> catheter accessed > 48 hours prior or within 48 hours of catheter removal that can be attributed to the presence of an <u>intravascular</u> catheter  (see also “<u>Criteria for CRBSI</u>”)

* Central line-associated bloodstream infection (<u>CLABSI</u>) : <u>primary BSI</u> in patients with a <u>central line</u> accessed > 48 hours prior or within 48 hours of <u>central line</u> removal





> [!TIP]
> The <u>criteria for CRBSI</u> are used for diagnosis and management, while the criteria for <u>CLABSI</u> are used primarily for epidemiologic surveillance to track <u>healthcare-associated infections</u>.

## Etiology

### Common pathogens [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




* Gram-positive: <u>coagulase-negative staphylococci</u> and <u>S. aureus</u> (most common)

* Fungal: <u>Candida</u> spp.

* Gram-negative: <u>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</u>





### Risk factors for CRBSI [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6aXjl9)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/paXLl9)




* Patient-related factors

* <u>Immunosuppression</u>

* <u>Bone marrow transplant</u>

* <u>Burns</u>

* Catheter-related factors

* <u>Central venous catheters</u> (especially nontunneled)

* <u>Pulmonary artery</u> catheters

* Multiple lumens

* Femoral and inguinal insertion sites

* Prolonged duration of catheter placement

* Frequent catheter access

* Catheter used for <u>parenteral nutrition</u> (e.g., <u>TPN</u>)





### Mechanism of infection [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/paXLl9)




* Extraluminal migration

* Pathogens (e.g., bacterial or fungal) at the percutaneous exit site migrate along the outer surface of the catheter to the bloodstream.

* Tends to occur in short-term catheters (placed for < 14 days)

* Intraluminal migration

* Pathogens (e.g., bacterial or fungal) infect the catheter hub and migrate within the lumen of the catheter into the bloodstream.

* Can occur in both long-term catheters (placed for ≥ 14 days) and short-term catheters

* Infusion contamination (e.g., infected <u>blood transfusion</u>, <u>TPN</u>)

* Hematogenous seeding (from other sites)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Biofilms</u> help pathogens avoid <u>host defense mechanisms</u> and play a role in the development of CRBSIs. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6aXjl9)

## Clinical features

* Local features: swelling, <u>pain</u>, redness, <u>exudates</u>, and/or <u>purulence</u> at catheter insertion sites

* Systemic features: <u>features of sepsis</u> (e.g., <u>fever</u>, <u>hemodynamic instability</u>, <u>altered mental status</u>)

---

## Diagnosis

The following recommendations relate primarily to CRBSI secondary to <u>central lines</u> and <u>arterial catheters</u>. Peripheral intravenous catheters (with or without <u>suppurative thrombophlebitis</u>) are rarely responsible for <u>bloodstream infections</u>. However, <u>exudates</u> from any <u>intravascular</u> line (including peripheral lines) should be submitted for <u>Gram stain</u> and culture.




### Routine initial diagnostics [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




* <u>CBC</u>, <u>inflammatory markers</u>, <u>BMP</u>, <u>liver chemistries</u> (similar to the <u>diagnostic workup for sepsis</u>)

* Consider additional tests (e.g., <u>urinalysis</u>, <u>chest x-ray</u>) as needed to rule out other potential primary sources of infection.





### Cultures




* Should be obtained preferably prior to starting <u>antibiotics</u> from two different sites simultaneously

* One of the cultures should be either a <u>blood culture</u> from the catheter lumen or, if the catheter has been removed, the catheter tip.

* Any <u>exudate</u> at the catheter insertion site should also be cultured.

* The same organism should be isolated from the different sites to confirm CRBSI.

> If the <u>diagnostic criteria for CRBSI</u> are not fulfilled, investigate for alternative sources of infection. If another primary source cannot be found and signs and symptoms persist, remove the catheter and culture the catheter tip. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




### <u>Transesophageal echocardiogram</u> (<u>TEE</u>)




* Indications: to evaluate for <u>infective endocarditis</u> in patients who have any of the following [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)

* <u>Clinical features of infective endocarditis</u>

* <u>Risk factors for infective endocarditis</u>

* Radiographic imaging to suggest <u>septic</u> <u>pulmonary embolism</u>

* Prolonged <u>fever</u> or <u>bacteremia</u> despite ≥ 72 hours of appropriate <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* Confirmed <u>S. aureus</u> <u>bacteremia</u> if considering a shorter duration of treatment (i.e., < 4–6 weeks)

* Important consideration: <u>TEE</u> should ideally be performed 5–7 days after the onset of <u>BSI</u> to minimize the likelihood of <u>false negatives</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> A negative <u>TEE</u> alone does not rule out <u>infective endocarditis</u>. <u>TEE</u> may need to be repeated if concern for <u>endocarditis</u> remains. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)

## Treatment

### Overview




* <u>Signs of sepsis</u> or <u>septic shock</u>: Initiate <u>hemodynamic support</u> (see “<u>Acute management checklist for sepsis</u>”).

* Obtain appropriate cultures before administering <u>antibiotics</u>.

* Initiate <u>empiric antibiotic therapy for CRBSI</u>.

* Remove the catheter if <u>indications for intravascular catheter removal</u> are met.

* Start <u>supportive care</u> and monitor response to treatment.





### <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




* Should be administered as soon as appropriate cultures have been obtained

* Coverage required:

* All patients require gram-positive coverage.

* Further <u>pathogen</u> coverage (e.g., gram-negative, <u>pseudomonal</u>, fungal) may be required based on the following factors

* Severity of illness

* Sites of catheter insertion

* Patient <u>risk factors</u> and comorbidities

* Local antimicrobial susceptibility data

> [!TIP]
> <u>Empiric antibiotics</u> for CRBSI should always provide gram-positive coverage. <u>Empiric antibiotics</u> for CRBSI in patients with femoral catheters should include coverage for gram-positive pathogens, <u>gram-negative bacilli</u>, and <u>Candida</u> spp. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




> [!TIP]
> In patients with <u>fungemia</u>, obtain an ophthalmologic examination to rule out fungal retinitis. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Efc8Lb0)




### Indications for intravascular catheter removal [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




* Any peripheral venous catheter with signs of local infection

* Long-term (≥ 14 days) <u>central lines</u> or <u>arterial catheters</u>: if any of the following are present

* Severe <u>sepsis</u> or <u>septic shock</u>

* <u>Suppurative thrombophlebitis</u>

* <u>Infective endocarditis</u>

* Evidence of persistent <u>BSI</u> (symptoms or positive cultures) despite 72 hours of appropriate therapy

* Obvious signs of infection at the catheter site

* Confirmed infection with <u>S. aureus</u>, <u>P. aeruginosa</u>, <u>enterococci</u>, fungi, or <u>mycobacteria</u>

* Catheter dysfunction (e.g., cracked and/or clotted lumen)

* Short-term (< 14 days) central access and confirmed infection with <u>S. aureus</u>, <u>Enterococci</u>, fungi, <u>mycobacteria</u>, or <u>gram-negative bacilli</u>

* Consider removal of long-term or short-term catheters with CRBSI due to less <u>virulent</u> pathogens that are difficult to eradicate (e.g., <u>Bacillus</u> spp., Micrococcus spp., propionibacteria, <u>coagulase-negative</u> <u>Staphylococci</u>)





> [!WARNING]
> Hemodynamically instability or <u>end-organ damage</u> likely due to CRBSI necessitates immediate catheter removal. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




> [!TIP]
> In patients who require continued <u>vascular access</u>, establish <u>intravascular</u> access at a new site or consider catheter salvage in patients with limited venous access. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




### Catheter salvage [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)




* Consider in patients with long-term catheters who do not have any <u>indications for intravascular catheter removal</u>

* Consider in patients who fulfill indications for removal but require long-term <u>vascular access</u> (e.g., for <u>hemodialysis</u>) and have limited <u>vascular access</u>

* Consider <u>antibiotic lock therapy</u> under specialist guidance.





> [!WARNING]
> If <u>blood cultures</u> remain positive despite 72 hours of appropriate <u>antibiotic therapy</u>, the catheter should be removed.




### <u>Supportive care</u>




* Appropriate <u>strategies for IV fluid therapy</u>, <u>specialized nutritional support</u>, <u>antipyretic therapy</u>, <u>VTE prophylaxis</u> (see “Supportive therapy” in “<u>Sepsis</u>”)

* Monitor patients in an appropriate care setting  with repeat clinical assessments and <u>laboratory studies</u>.





### Further management




Switch to culture-specific <u>antibiotic therapy</u> in consultation with a specialist once <u>antibiotic sensitivities</u> are known. Obtain surveillance <u>blood cultures</u> to confirm eradication of infection.




* Uncomplicated CRBSI [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE) 

* Resolution of symptoms and negative <u>blood cultures</u> within 72 hours of appropriate <u>antibiotic therapy</u> in a patient without any of the features of <u>complicated CRBSI</u>

* Continue <u>antibiotics</u> for 7–14 days after the first negative <u>blood culture</u>, depending on the cultured organism.

* There is insufficient evidence to recommend repeat cultures following the completion of <u>antimicrobial therapy</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)

* Complicated CRBSI [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE) 

* Defined as CRBSI in a patient with any of the following:

* Persistent <u>fever</u> and/or <u>bacteremia</u> or <u>fungemia</u> despite 72 hours of appropriate treatment

* Active <u>malignancy</u>, <u>immunosuppression</u>, or an <u>intravascular</u> device

* Development of <u>complications of CRBSI</u>

* Management

* Imaging studies to rule out complications: e.g., <u>TEE</u> for <u>infective endocarditis</u>; <u>ultrasound</u> or CT for <u>suppurative thrombophlebitis</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Efc8Lb0)

* Remove the catheter and send the tip for culture. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/paXLl9)

* Prolonged (4–8 weeks) <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* Surgical intervention may be required to manage complications.





> [!TIP]
> Specialists (e.g., in infectious disease) should be actively involved in the management of all patients with complicated CRBSIs.




> [!TIP]
> A new <u>central venous catheter</u> can be placed once follow-up <u>blood cultures</u> are negative.

* <u>Septic emboli</u>

* Port <u>abscess</u>
