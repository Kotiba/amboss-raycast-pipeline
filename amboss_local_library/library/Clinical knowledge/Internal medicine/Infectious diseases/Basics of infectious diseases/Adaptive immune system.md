## Summary

Adaptive (acquired) <u>immunity</u> is a part of the <u>immune system</u> that provides an <u>antigen</u>-specific response following exposure to a microbial <u>pathogen</u> or foreign substance (e.g., <u>antigen</u>). The adaptive <u>immune system</u> primarily involves <u>B cells</u>, <u>T cells</u>, and circulating <u>antibodies</u>, all of which mount a targeted <u>immune response</u> to a particular <u>antigen</u>/invading <u>pathogen</u>. An important component of adaptive <u>immunity</u> is <u>immunologic memory</u>, a mechanism by which the <u>immune system</u> forms <u>memory B cells</u> and <u>memory T cells</u>. These cells are able to trigger a more rapid and extensive response following subsequent <u>antigen</u> exposure. Adaptive <u>immunity</u> can be conferred via <u>vaccination</u>, which induces <u>immunity</u> through selective exposure to <u>antigens</u> that have been rendered innocuous. <u>Autoimmunity</u> is a disorder of the adaptive <u>immune system</u> and is characterized by <u>immune responses</u> to the body's own tissue. <u>Immunodeficiency</u> conditions, in which a compromised <u>immune system</u> leaves the body highly susceptible to infections, can be either congenital (see “<u>Congenital immunodeficiency disorders</u>” for more information) or acquired (e.g., <u>HIV infection</u>, <u>iatrogenic</u> <u>immunosuppression</u>).

## T cells (T lymphocytes)

### Overview [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XJ09sS)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GgaB9P)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QHbuJE)




* A major component of the adaptive <u>immune response</u>

* Essential for cell-mediated immunity

* Directly involves <u>cytotoxic T cells</u> and other <u>phagocytes</u>, but not <u>B cells</u> or <u>antibodies</u>

* Important in the recognition and destruction of intracellular pathogens (e.g., <u>viruses</u>, intracellular bacteria)

* All mature <u>T cells</u> express specific surface <u>proteins</u> that distinguish them from other <u>lymphocytes</u> and allow them to recognize <u>antigens</u> presented by <u>MHC</u> molecules of <u>antigen-presenting cells</u>.





#### T-cell receptors (<u>TCRs</u>)




* Complex of <u>proteins</u> of the <u>immunoglobulin</u> superfamily

* Each <u>T cell</u> expresses a <u>TCR</u> variant that binds to one specific <u>antigen</u>. 

* Binding of a <u>TCR</u> to its specific <u>antigen</u> initiates <u>T cell activation</u> (see “T-activation” below).

* The <u>antigen</u> fragment has to be presented by a <u>MHC</u> molecule of an <u>antigen-presenting cell</u> in order to be recognized by the <u>TCR</u>.

* Important for positive and negative selection during <u>T-cell development</u>

### T-cell development [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EYY8In)




<u>T cells</u> originate from lymphoid progenitor cells in the <u>bone marrow</u> and mature in the <u>thymus</u>.




* Positive selection of T cells: ensures that the <u>thymus</u> produces functional <u>T cells</u>

* Location: <u>thymic cortex</u>

* <u>Thymic</u> cortical cells express <u>MHC class I</u> and <u>MHC class II</u> <u>antigens</u>.

* Tests if <u>T-cell receptors</u> can bind to <u>MHC</u> appropriately (not too strongly or too weakly)

* <u>T cells</u> (<u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u>/<u>CD8<sup>+</sup></u>, double-positive <u>thymocytes</u>) receive survival signal.

* Dysfunctional <u>T cells</u> then undergo <u>apoptosis</u>.

* Negative selection of T cells: ensures that the <u>thymus</u> does not produce self-reacting <u>T cells</u>

* Location: <u>thymic medulla</u>

* Tests if <u>T cells</u> bind to tissue-restricted self-antigens presented on <u>MHC</u> by <u>thymic</u> medullary cells

* <u>T cells</u> that do not bind receive survival signal.

* <u>T cells</u> that bind self-<u>antigens</u> undergo <u>apoptosis</u>, except for a few that become regulatory <u>T cells</u>.

* Self-<u>antigen</u> presentation is mediated by the <u>autoimmune regulator protein</u> (<u>AIRE protein</u>), dysfunction of which can lead to:

* <u>Adrenal insufficiency</u>

* <u>Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis</u>

* <u>Hypoparathyroidism</u>

* In addition, <u>T cells</u> bind with their cluster of differentiation (CD).
* The type of CD that the <u>T cell</u> has a higher affinity for is kept, while the other is downregulated to either (<u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u>/<u>CD8</u><sup>-</sup>) or (<u>CD4</u><sup>-</sup>/<u>CD8<sup>+</sup></u>). 

* <u>CD4</u> binds to <u>MHC II</u>.

* <u>CD8</u> binds to <u>MHC I</u>.

* Immunocompetent, but still naive <u>T cells</u> leave the <u>thymus</u> and migrate within and between peripheral tissues, <u>blood vessels</u>, and <u>secondary lymphoid organs</u> (e.g., <u>lymph nodes</u>, <u>spleen</u>, <u>MALT</u>).





> [!NOTE]
> Defective negative <u>T-cell</u> selection can cause autoimmune disorders (e.g., <u>type 1 autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome</u>).




> [!NOTE]
> “Life is ACHe without <u>AIRE</u>”: <u>Autoimmune regulator protein</u> (<u>AIRE</u>) dysfunction can lead to Adrenal insufficiency, chronic mucocutaneous Candidiasis, and Hypoparathyroidism.

### T-cell activation




#### Overview




* <u>Antigens</u> are processed by antigen-presenting cells (i.e., <u>macrophages</u>, <u>monocytes</u>, <u>B cells</u>, <u>Langerhans cells</u>, and <u>dendritic cells</u>).

* <u>Dendritic cells</u> travel through different tissues, <u>phagocytize</u> and process <u>antigens</u>;   and then migrate through afferent lymphatic vessels to a <u>lymph node</u> to present these <u>antigens</u> via <u>MHC I</u> and II.

* <u>T cell</u> activation (“priming”);   mainly occurs in <u>secondary lymphoid organs</u>, such as <u>lymph nodes</u>.

* Activation requires an initial signal, followed by a second, costimulatory signal.





#### Mechanism



1. * <u>Antigen</u> presentation

* Performed by <u>antigen-presenting cells</u>

* <u>Exogenous</u> <u>antigens</u> are presented via <u>MHC II</u> to <u>TCR</u>/<u>CD4</u>.

* <u>Endogenous</u> <u>antigens</u> are presented via <u>MHC I</u> to <u>TCR</u>/<u>CD8</u> (cross-presentation of <u>antigens</u>).
2. * Costimulatory signal: mediates survival and proliferation of T cells

* B7 protein (<u>CD80</u> or <u>CD86</u>) on the <u>dendritic cell</u>: interacts with CD28 on the naive <u>T cell</u>.

* <u>Antigen</u> presentation without this co-stimulatory signal will lead to <u>T-cell</u> <u>anergy</u>:

* Important <u>self-tolerance</u> mechanism

* <u>The cell</u> will not be activated even though it is exposed to its <u>antigen</u>

* Superantigens (e.g., <u>toxic shock syndrome toxin 1</u>, <u>enterotoxin B</u>) link <u>MHC II</u> <u>antigen-presenting cells</u> and <u>T-cell receptors</u> on <u>T cells</u> and lead to <u>activation of T cells</u> without a costimulatory signal.
3. * Effect

* <u>T-helper cell</u>: activation of NFAT (<u>nuclear factor of activated T cells</u>) → <u>cytokine</u> production → activation of other cells, e.g., <u>B cells</u>, <u>macrophages</u>, and cytotoxic <u>T cells</u>

* Cytotoxic <u>T cell</u>: recognizes and destroys cells that possess <u>antigens</u> (signals <u>malignancy</u> or viral infection)

### <u>T cell</u> effects



1. * <u>T cells</u> (<u>CD8</u><sup>+</sup>): direct cell lysis or induction of <u>apoptosis</u> via <u>perforin</u> and <u>proteases</u>

* Activated via <u>antigen</u> presentation by <u>MHC class I</u> <u>receptors</u>

* Induce <u>apoptosis</u> of <u>virus</u>-infected or malignant cells using mechanisms as those used by <u>NK cells</u>: release of granules that contain <u>perforin</u>, <u>granzyme B</u>, <u>granulysin</u>

* Release <u>cytokines</u> (including <u>IFN‑γ</u>, TNF, and <u>lymphotoxin-α</u>) → <u>macrophage</u> activation

* Clinical relevance: involved in organ rejection (acute and chronic), induce <u>apoptosis</u> of donor graft cells
2. * <u>T<sub>h</sub>1 cell</u> (<u>CD4</u><sup>+</sup>): cell‑mediated response

* Activated via <u>antigen</u> presentation by <u>MHC class II</u> <u>receptors</u>

* <u>Immune response</u> to intracellular pathogens (<u>viruses</u>, intracellular bacteria)

* Release <u>cytokines</u> (including <u>IFN‑γ</u>, IL‑2, and TNF) → stimulation of <u>macrophages</u> (positive feedback) and <u>CD8<sup>+</sup></u> cytotoxic <u>T cells</u>;   ; effect is amplified by binding of <u>T-cell</u> <u>CD40L</u> to <u>CD40</u> <u>receptors</u> on <u>macrophages</u>

* <u>IFN‑γ</u>, <u>IL-2</u>, and TNF‑α induce <u>granuloma</u> formation against foreign bodies that cannot be eliminated by the <u>immune cells</u>.

* Clinical significance:

* <u>Tuberculosis</u> in <u>HIV infection</u>

* Delayed cell-mediated <u>type IV hypersensitivity</u> reactions (see the corresponding section in ”<u>Hypersensitivity reactions</u>”)
3. * <u>T<sub>h</sub>2 cell</u> (<u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u>): cell‑mediated response

* Activated via <u>antigen</u> presentation by <u>MHC class II</u> <u>receptors</u>

* <u>Immune response</u> to extracellular pathogens (bacteria, <u>parasites</u>)
* Release <u>cytokines</u> (including <u>IL-4</u>, <u>IL-5</u>, <u>IL-13</u>) which stimulate <u>eosinophils</u>, <u>basophils</u>, and <u>mast cells</u>

#### Surface markers




Surface protein expression determines the specific function of <u>T cell subtypes</u>.




* General <u>T cell</u> markers

* All <u>T cells</u> carry membrane-bound marker <u>proteins</u> that distinguish them from other <u>lymphocytes</u>.

* These general <u>T-cell</u> markers are <u>CD3</u>, <u>CD28</u>, and the <u>T-cell receptor</u> (<u>TCR</u>).

* Specific markers of <u>T cell</u> subtypes

* <u>T cells</u> are largely divided into <u>CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells</u> (<u>cytotoxic T cells</u>) and <u>CD4<sup>+</sup> T cells</u> (<u>T helper cell</u> subpopulations).

* Besides <u>CD4</u>, <u>T helper cells</u> express <u>CD40L</u>, <u>CXCR4</u>, and <u>CCR5</u>.

* See also “<u>CD4/CD8 ratio</u>.”

* Subtypes within the group of <u>CD4<sup>+</sup> T cells</u> may be identified by the <u>cytokines</u> they secrete and/or by their specific surface markers (see table below; the list is not exhaustive).

## B cells (B lymphocytes)

### Overview [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XJ09sS)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GgaB9P)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QHbuJE)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2gYTuo)



* Major component of the adaptive <u>immune system</u>: The humoral immune response of the adaptive <u>immune system</u> mainly consists of <u>B cells</u> and <u>antibodies</u>.




### B-cell development




* Originate and mature in the <u>bone marrow</u>

* Naive <u>B cell</u>: a mature <u>B cell</u> that has not come in contact with <u>antigens</u> yet

* Express numerous surface <u>proteins</u>

* <u>CD19</u>, <u>CD20</u>  , <u>CD21</u> (used by <u>EBV</u>), and <u>CD40</u>

* <u>MHC II</u>

* <u>B7</u>

* <u>B cell receptor</u> with <u>immunoglobulin</u> component (binds <u>antigens</u>)

* Mature <u>B cells</u> circulate between the blood and <u>secondary lymphatic organs</u> (e.g., <u>lymph nodes</u>, <u>spleen</u>, <u>MALT</u>).

* After activation, <u>B cells</u> differentiate into plasma cells that produce and secrete <u>antibodies</u> (see “Immunoglobulins” below).





> [!NOTE]
> “Mr. Epstein, you have to be 21 to Be in this Barr!”: The Epstein-Barr <u>virus</u> uses the <u>CD21</u> <u>receptor</u> to invade B cells.




#### B-cell receptors (<u>BCRs</u>)




* Type I <u>transmembrane proteins</u> on the <u>B cell</u> surface that are composed of <u>immunoglobulins</u>  and signal-transmitting subunits

* Necessary for <u>B cell</u> activation and maturation

* The <u>immunoglobulin</u> parts of mature <u>BCRs</u> are highly specific to certain <u>antigens</u>.

* Naive <u>BCR</u>: a <u>BCR</u> that has not interacted with an <u>antigen</u> yet





### B cell activation




<u>B cell activation</u> and <u>class switching</u> require an initial signal, as well as a costimulatory signal. Activated <u>B cells</u> migrate to <u>germinal centers</u> of <u>secondary lymphoid organs</u>.




* T cell-dependent activation of B cells

* Requires activation of <u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u> <u>T-helper cells</u>, which, in turn, requires prior <u>antigen</u> presentation via <u>MHC II</u> by the <u>B cell</u>

* Initiated as a response to protein or <u>peptide</u> <u>antigens</u> (<u>thymus</u>-dependent <u>antigens</u>), e.g.:

* <u>Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines</u>

* <u>Diphtheria toxin</u>-like protein with conjugated <u>polysaccharides</u>

* <u>B lymphocytes</u> recognize <u>antigens</u> via their <u>B-cell</u> <u>receptors</u> (membrane‑bound <u>immunoglobulins</u>, <u>IgD</u> or <u>IgM</u>) → <u>B cell</u> <u>receptor</u>-mediated <u>endocytosis</u> of the <u>BCR</u>/<u>antigen</u> complex → breakdown of <u>antigen</u> into small fragments by <u>lysosomal</u> <u>proteases</u> → presentation of <u>antigen</u> fragment via <u>MHC class II</u> <u>receptors</u> on <u>B cell</u> surface to <u>T<sub>h</sub> cells</u> ;   plus costimulation of <u>B cell</u> <u>CD40</u> <u>receptor</u> by <u>Th cell</u> <u>CD40L</u> → T cell‑dependent activation of <u>B cells</u> (<u>plasma cells</u>) → immunoglobulin production

* T cell-independent activation of B cells

* Initiated as an immediate response to nonprotein <u>antigens</u> (<u>thymus</u>-independent <u>antigens</u>; e.g., gram-negative <u>lipopolysaccharide</u>)

* Leads to production of <u>IgM</u> <u>antibodies</u>

* No prior <u>T-cell</u> activation since <u>MHC</u> are unable to present nonprotein <u>antigens</u> to <u>T cells</u>

* <u>Thymus</u> independent <u>antigens</u> are considered to be only weakly immunogenic, which is why <u>vaccines</u> containing nonprotein <u>antigens</u> need adjuvants (like the <u>PPSV23</u> <u>Streptococcus pneumoniae</u> <u>vaccine</u>, containing a capsular <u>polysaccharide</u> subunit) and boosters.

#### Affinity maturation




* Definition: : A process in which <u>B cells</u> interact with <u>T<sub>h</sub> cells</u> within the <u>germinal center</u>;  of secondary lymphoid tissue in order to secrete <u>immunoglobulins</u> with higher affinity for specific <u>antigens</u>.

* Mechanisms

* Somatic hypermutation: <u>point mutations</u> that create random alterations in the variable region of the <u>antibody</u> <u>gene</u>

* Clonal selection: <u>B cells</u> that possess <u>antibodies</u> with higher affinity for the <u>antigen</u> have a survival advantage through a positive selection that allows them to proliferate and predominate within the follicle.

#### Isotype switching (<u>class switching</u>)




Within the <u>germinal centers</u> of <u>lymph nodes</u>, activated <u>B cells</u> change the <u>antibody isotype</u> in response to specific <u>cytokines</u> that are released by <u>T<sub>h</sub> cells</u>. <u>IgM</u>, the primary <u>antibody</u> on <u>B cells</u> before getting activated, is switched to <u>IgA</u>, <u>IgE</u>, or <u>IgG</u>. <u>IgM</u> is also secreted by <u>plasma cells</u> (stimulated by <u>IL-6</u>).




* <u>B cell</u> <u>class switching</u> occurs via two signaling mechanisms:

* First signal = activation: <u>Antigen</u> bound to <u>MHC II</u> molecule binds to <u>T-cell receptor</u> on the surface of <u>T-helper cells</u>.

* Second signal = costimulation: CD40 <u>membrane receptor</u> on the <u>B cell</u> binds to CD40 ligand (<u>CD40L</u>) on the surface of <u>CD4<sup>+</sup> T cell</u> (CD40L/CD40) → <u>cytokine</u> release → <u>gene</u> rearrangement, resulting in <u>class switching</u>

* <u>IL-4</u>, <u>IL-13</u>: stimulate <u>class switching</u> to <u>IgE</u>.

* <u>IL-5</u>, <u>TGF-β</u>: stimulate <u>class switching</u> to <u>IgA</u>.

* <u>IFN-γ</u>, <u>IL-4</u>, <u>IL-21</u>: stimulate <u>class switching</u> to <u>IgG</u>.

* The resulting <u>antibody</u> has the same affinity for the <u>antigen</u> but a different function.

* <u>Isotype switching</u> is irreversible.

## Immunoglobulins

### Overview




<u>Immunoglobulins</u> (<u>antibodies</u>) have two functional parts: the <u>Fc region</u> and the <u>Fab region</u>; . The two enzymes papain and <u>pepsin</u> can be used to identify the different functional parts. Every <u>immunoglobulin</u> can have monomeric structure. In context of <u>immunoglobulins</u>, the term affinity refers to individual interaction of <u>antibody</u> and <u>antigen</u>, whereas avidity characterizes the accumulated binding strength of all <u>antigen</u>-binding sites combined.




* Fc region  

* Contains the constant region

* Formed by <u>heavy (H) chains</u>

* Determines the antibody isotype (e.g., <u>IgA</u>, <u>IgG</u>, <u>IgM</u>)

* Binds <u>complement</u> (<u>IgG</u>, <u>IgM</u>)

* Binds various immunological cells, such as <u>macrophages</u>, to stimulate phagocytic or cytotoxic activity (<u>opsonization</u>)

* Contains the carboxyl terminal

* Has many <u>carbohydrate</u> side chains

* Fab region

* Contains the variable/hypervariable region

* Formed by <u>light (L) chains</u> and <u>heavy (H) chains</u>

* Recognizes and binds to <u>antigens</u> via epitope

* Determines the idiotype

* Binding site with <u>specificity</u> for one particular kind of <u>antigen</u>

* Every <u>B cell</u> expresses exactly one <u>antigenic</u> <u>specificity</u>





> [!NOTE]
> Fc → Complement, Constant, Carboxy terminal, Carbohydrate side chains
> Fab → Antigen binding

### Immunoglobulin properties




#### <u>Specificity</u>




* Requires <u>antigens</u>

* Occurs by <u>somatic hypermutation</u> and <u>affinity maturation</u>

* Alterations take place in the variable region.

* Normal response to <u>antigenic</u> stimulation: <u>B lymphocytes</u> with varying <u>immunoglobulin</u> <u>alleles</u> (i.e., polyclonal proliferation)

* Malignant <u>lymphocyte</u> <u>proliferation</u>: predominance of <u>B lymphocytes</u> with a single <u>immunoglobulin</u> variable domain (i.e., monoclonal proliferation)

* <u>Class switching</u> (e.g., <u>IgA</u>, <u>IgM</u>, <u>IgG</u>)
* Alterations take place in the sequence of the <u>heavy chain</u> constant domain.





#### Diversity




* Does not require <u>antigens</u>

* Recombination-activating genes (<u>RAG</u>): a set of <u>genes</u> that encode <u>RAG</u> <u>proteins</u> 1 and 2, which together form V(D)J recombinase, an enzyme required for <u>V(D)J recombination</u> in <u>T cells</u> and <u>B cells</u>

* Random recombination of certain <u>genes</u> during <u>B cell</u> maturation in <u>bone marrow</u>

* Light chain: VJ <u>genes</u>

* Heavy chain: V(D)J <u>genes</u>

* <u>Autosomal recessive</u> <u>RAG1</u> and <u>RAG2</u> <u>loss-of-function mutations</u> are a rare cause of <u>severe combined immunodeficiency</u>.

* <u>Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase</u> (<u>TdT</u>) randomly adds <u>nucleotides</u> to the <u>DNA</u>.

* Recombination of <u>light chains</u> with <u>heavy chains</u> occurs randomly

### Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (<u>ADCC</u>) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sc1tdf0)




* Definition: an <u>immune response</u> in which <u>Fc receptor-bearing</u> immune effector cells (e.g., <u>NK cells</u>, <u>eosinophils</u>) bind to and lyse <u>target cells</u> that have specific <u>antibodies</u> attached to their surface <u>antigens</u> (<u>pathogen</u> or <u>tumor</u>-derived)

* Characteristics

* Allows innate <u>immune cells</u> to recognize pathogens that do not express <u>pathogen-associated molecular patterns</u> (<u>PAMPs</u>)

* Part of the <u>immune response</u> to <u>parasites</u>

* Effector cells

* <u>NK cells</u> (most common): interact with <u>IgG</u>-coated <u>target cells</u> and subsequently release cytotoxic substances (<u>perforins</u>, <u>granzymes</u>)

* <u>Eosinophils</u>: interact with <u>IgE</u>-coated <u>helminths</u> and subsequently release <u>major basic proteins</u> and other cytotoxic substances

* Other: <u>monocytes</u>, <u>macrophages</u>, <u>neutrophils</u>

* Mechanism

* Binding of <u>antibodies</u> (e.g., <u>IgG</u> or <u>IgE</u>) produced by <u>B-cells</u> to <u>antigens</u> on <u>target cells</u> (e.g., <u>tumor</u> cells, <u>viruses</u>, or <u>parasites</u>)

* Recognition and binding of the <u>Fc</u> portion of bound <u>IgG</u> or <u>IgE</u> by <u>Fc receptors</u> expressed on the surface of effector cells (e.g., CD16 <u>Fc receptors</u> on <u>NK cells</u>)

* Activation of signaling pathways in the effector cell (e.g., <u>NK cell</u>), leading to cytotoxic granule release

* Destruction of the <u>target cell</u> (e.g., via the <u>perforin</u>/<u>granzyme</u> <u>cell death</u> pathway)

* Clinical significance: <u>monoclonal antibody</u> therapy





> [!TIP]
> Anticancer <u>monoclonal antibodies</u> (e.g., <u>trastuzumab</u>, <u>rituximab</u>) act by neutralizing extracellular targets (e.g., <u>membrane receptors</u>, channels) or promoting <u>immune system</u> recognition via <u>ADCC</u> by <u>NK cells</u>.

## Immunologic memory

* Definition

* The ability of the <u>immune system</u> to recognize <u>antigens</u> after an initial exposure and to quickly and efficiently mount an <u>immune response</u> in subsequent exposures.

* Allows for the conservation of a specific acquired <u>immune response</u>

* Process
1. * Initial exposure to a foreign <u>antigen</u>
2. * Primary <u>immune response</u>: activation of <u>B cells</u> and <u>T cells</u> (see the sections on <u>B cells</u> and <u>T cells</u> above)
3. * Formation of memory B cells and <u>memory T cells</u>

* Memory B cells: specialized <u>plasma cells</u> that have the ability to persist for decades following the elimination of an <u>antigen</u> and produce high-affinity <u>antibodies</u> throughout their lifespan [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7MY4Jp)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HMYKJp)

* Undergo <u>proliferation</u>, <u>somatic hypermutation</u>, <u>clonal selection</u>, and <u>immunoglobulin class switching</u> (see T cell‑dependent <u>B cell activation</u>)

* Arrested in their differentiation and persist in the marginal zone of follicles

* Memory T cells: specialized <u>T cells</u> that persist following a primary <u>immune response</u> to an <u>antigen</u> and have the ability to elicit an immediate and more potent <u>immune response</u> in the event of subsequent exposures to the same <u>antigen</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HMYKJp)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uMYpqp)

* Following a primary <u>immune response</u>, ∼ 90% of effector <u>T cells</u> die via <u>apoptosis</u>; a small fraction of the effector <u>T cells</u> survive to become <u>memory T cells</u>.

* Can be <u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u> or <u>CD8<sup>+</sup></u>

* Effector <u>memory</u> <u>T cells</u> (T<sub>EM</sub> cells, <u>CCR7</u> negative cells): <u>CD4<sup>+</sup> T cells</u> or <u>CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells</u> that persist in the circulation and peripheral tissue.

* <u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u> T<sub>EM</sub> cells produce <u>proinflammatory cytokines</u> (e.g., <u>IFN-γ</u>).

* <u>CD8<sup>+</sup></u> T<sub>EM</sub> cells contain preformed <u>perforin</u> granules for immediate cytotoxicity.

* Central <u>memory</u> <u>T cells</u> (CCR7 positive cells): Persist in secondary lymphoid tissue and are able to differentiate into effector <u>memory T cells</u> upon activation.

* Subsequent <u>immune response</u>: Re-exposure to the <u>antigen</u> activates the <u>memory</u> cells.

* <u>Memory B cells</u> secrete high-affinity <u>antibodies</u> and accelerate the <u>secondary immune response</u> to the <u>antigen</u>.

* <u>Memory T cells</u> mature to T<sub>EM</sub> cells and trigger an immediate release of <u>cytokines</u> or cytotoxicity.

* Repeated exposure to the <u>antigen</u> leads to more efficient <u>immune responses</u>.





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Memory</u> cells are a large pool of <u>antigen</u>-specific <u>lymphocytes</u> that can respond faster and more efficiently than naive <u>lymphocytes</u> when re-exposed to the <u>antigen</u>. These cells form the basis for the immunologic response to <u>vaccinations</u>.

## Immune tolerance and autoimmunity

### Immune tolerance [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EYY8In)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hnccG10)




The unresponsiveness of an organism’s <u>immune system</u> to <u>antigens</u> in an effort to prevent harmful over-reactivity is called <u>immune tolerance</u>.





#### Central tolerance




* Overview

* A type of <u>immune tolerance</u> that occurs at an early stage of lymphoid cell development

* Ensures that <u>T cells</u> and <u>B cells</u> that recognize self-<u>antigens</u> (i.e., <u>antigens</u> expressed by the organism's own cells) are identified before they develop into fully immunocompetent cells

* Mechanisms

* Negative selection of <u>T cells</u>;  and <u>B cell</u> clonal deletion: <u>Lymphocytes</u> that express <u>receptors</u> with a high affinity for self-<u>antigens</u> will undergo <u>apoptosis</u>.

* Development of <u>regulatory T cells</u> (<u>CD4<sup>+</sup></u>)

* <u>Receptor</u> editing: <u>B-cell</u> <u>specificity</u> is reprogrammed through secondary recombination of <u>antibody</u> <u>genes</u>





#### Peripheral tolerance




* Overview

* A type of <u>immune tolerance</u> that occurs in the immune periphery (e.g., <u>spleen</u>, <u>lymph nodes</u>) following the egression of <u>T cells</u> and <u>B cells</u> from their <u>primary lymphoid organs</u>

* Ensures the deletion of or induction of <u>anergy</u> in autoreactive T and <u>B cells</u> that <u>escape</u> into the periphery

* Mechanisms  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3ncSG10)

* Anergy: a lack of a normal <u>immune response</u> to particular <u>antigens</u>, <u>cytokines</u>, and <u>allergens</u>, usually due to the absence of costimulatory signals

* Deletion of autoreactive <u>T cells</u> through <u>apoptosis</u>

* Suppression: blocking activation by inducing <u>regulatory T cells</u> through exposure to <u>TGF-β</u> in peripheral tissues

* Inhibitory <u>receptors</u>: recognition of self-<u>antigens</u> prevents <u>B-cell</u> activation by triggering inhibitory <u>receptors</u>





### Autoimmunity




<u>Autoimmunity</u> refers to an immune reaction against the body's own cells that occurs as a result of a loss of <u>immune tolerance</u>. Women have a disproportionately higher <u>incidence</u> of autoimmune diseases than men.




#### Presumed pathogenesis




* Autoreactive <u>B cells</u> are physiologically eliminated in the <u>bone marrow</u>, <u>spleen</u>, and <u>lymph nodes</u>.

* <u>T cells</u> that attack the body's own cells undergo negative selection in the <u>thymus</u> or undergo <u>apoptosis</u> in peripheral lymphoid tissues (e.g, <u>lymph nodes</u>, <u>adenoids</u>, <u>Peyer's patches</u>) due to a lack of stimulation.

* If the selection mechanisms fail, <u>immune cells</u> can attack the body's own cells, which leads to autoimmune inflammation.





#### Causes of autoimmune conditions




* Mostly <u>idiopathic</u>

* Sometimes elicited by a previous infection (e.g., <u>Guillain-Barré syndrome</u>, <u>rheumatic fever</u>).
* The underlying pathomechanism is molecular mimicry (the <u>antigenic</u> resemblance between molecules on some pathogens and those of normal cells in the body)

* Persistent <u>antigenic</u> stimuli

* Breach of <u>central tolerance</u>

* Genetic predisposition, e.g.:

* HLA-B8, e.g., <u>myasthenia gravis</u>, <u>Graves disease</u>, <u>Addison disease</u>

* HLA‑B27, e.g., <u>ankylosing spondylitis</u>, <u>reactive arthritis</u>, <u>psoriatic arthritis</u>, <u>ulcerative colitis</u>

* HLA-DR4, e.g., <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>, <u>type 1 diabetes</u>, <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u>, <u>Addison disease</u>

* HLA-DR3, e.g., <u>type 1 diabetes</u>, <u>SLE</u>, <u>Hashimoto thyroiditis</u>, <u>Graves disease</u>

* HLA-DR2, e.g., <u>SLE</u>, <u>Goodpasture syndrome</u>, <u>multiple sclerosis</u>

* HLA‑DQ2 and <u>HLA-DQ8</u>, e.g., <u>gluten‑sensitive enteropathy</u>

* <u>HLA</u>-DR5, e.g., <u>Hashimoto thyroiditis</u>

* <u>HLA-A3</u>, e.g., <u>hemochromatosis</u>





#### Consequences




* The presence of autoreactive <u>B cells</u> results in the production of irregular <u>antibodies</u>, which can trigger various diseases.

* <u>Autoantibodies</u> can also be used as a diagnostic tool (see the table below).

* In <u>T-cell</u> mediated autoimmune reactions, there are usually no detectable specific <u>antibodies</u> (e.g., in <u>multiple sclerosis</u>).

## Immune deficiency

* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* <u>Primary immunodeficiency</u> diseases are rare

* Approx. 1–2/1,000 general population are immune deficient.

* Most common types 

* Primary

* <u>Fanconi anemia</u>

* <u>Selective IgA deficiency</u>

* <u>Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome</u>

* <u>Ataxia telangiectasia</u>

* <u>DiGeorge syndrome</u>

* See “<u>Congenital immunodeficiency disorders</u>” for more information.

* Secondary

* <u>HIV</u>/<u>AIDS</u>

* <u>Iatrogenic</u> immune suppression

* Clinical findings

* The main symptom of <u>primary immunodeficiency</u> is a pathological susceptibility to infection.

* The type of susceptibility is characterized by the invading <u>pathogen</u>, localization, course, severity, and number of infections.

* Not all <u>immune deficiencies</u> are clinically apparent.

* <u>B-cell</u> deficiencies (decreased number of <u>B cells</u> and/or impaired <u>B-cell</u> function) typically results in recurrent bacterial infections.

* <u>T-cell</u> deficiencies typically result in recurrent viral and <u>fungal infections</u>.
