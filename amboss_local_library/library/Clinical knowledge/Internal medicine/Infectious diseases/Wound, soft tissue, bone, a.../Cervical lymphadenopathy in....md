# Cervical lymphadenopathy in children

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Internal medicine > Infectious diseases > Wound, soft tissue, bone, and joint infections > Cervical lymphadenopathy in children*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/do0oaS)

---

## Summary

<u>Cervical lymphadenopathy</u> (<u>LAD</u>) is a > 1 cm enlargement of <u>lymph nodes</u> in the neck. In children, cervical <u>LAD</u> is common and most often represents an inflammatory response to an acute infection, such as a viral <u>upper respiratory infection</u> or <u>strep throat</u>. Less commonly, it can signal a more serious underlying condition (e.g., <u>malignancy</u>, <u>mycobacterial</u> infection, autoimmune disease). Benign nodes are typically small and mobile, while concerning nodes may be large, firm, fixed, and/or accompanied by <u>constitutional symptoms</u>. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, but diagnostic evaluation (e.g., <u>laboratory studies</u>, imaging, <u>lymph node</u> <u>biopsy</u>) may be required in the case of <u>red flag features</u> or diagnostic uncertainty. Management is directed at the underlying cause (e.g., observation for viral infections; <u>antibiotics</u> for suspected bacterial <u>lymphadenitis</u>). Prompt specialist referral is indicated for features suggesting <u>malignancy</u> or other systemic conditions.

---

## Etiology

Cervical <u>LAD</u> in children is most often caused by an infectious etiology. Approximately 45% of otherwise healthy children have isolated cervical <u>LAD</u> on <u>physical examination</u>.  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fOck7c0)




### Acute cervical <u>LAD</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




Cervical <u>LAD</u> in children is considered acute if present < 2 weeks. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2mVTUE0)




#### Infectious causes




* <u>Viruses</u> 

* Common respiratory <u>viruses</u> (e.g., <u>adenovirus</u>, <u>influenza virus</u>, <u>respiratory syncytial virus</u>, <u>rhinovirus</u>, <u>coronavirus</u>)

* <u>Enterovirus</u> (i.e., <u>coxsackievirus</u>)

* <u>Rubella</u>

* <u>Measles</u>

* <u>HSV</u>

* <u>Varicella zoster virus</u>

* Bacteria 

* <u>Staphylococcus aureus</u>

* <u>Streptococcus pyogenes</u>

* Oral <u>anaerobes</u>

* Less commonly, <u>Haemophilus influenzae</u>, Group B <u>Streptococcus</u> , and <u>Corynebacterium diphtheriae</u>





#### Noninfectious causes




* <u>Kawasaki disease</u>

* <u>Serum sickness</u>

* Immunizations (e.g., <u>DTaP</u>, <u>polio</u>, <u>typhoid fever</u> <u>vaccines</u>)





> [!TIP]
> Most acute cervical <u>LAD</u> in children is due to viral respiratory infections or acute bacterial <u>lymphadenitis</u> caused by <u>Staphylococcus aureus</u> or <u>Streptococcus pyogenes</u>. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)




### Subacute and chronic cervical <u>LAD</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




Cervical <u>LAD</u> in children is considered subacute if present for 2–6 weeks and chronic if present for ≥ 6 weeks. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2mVTUE0)




#### Infectious




* <u>Viruses</u> 

* <u>EBV</u> and <u>CMV</u>: most common

* <u>HIV</u>

* Bacteria

* <u>Bartonella henselae</u>

* <u>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</u>

* <u>Nontuberculous mycobacteria</u> (e.g., <u>Mycobacterium</u> <u>avium-intracellulare</u>, <u>Mycobacterium scrofulaceum</u>)

* <u>Pasteurella multocida</u>

* <u>Nocardia brasiliensis</u>

* <u>Treponema pallidum</u>

* <u>Protozoa</u>: <u>Toxoplasma gondii</u>

* Fungi (e.g., <u>Candida</u>, <u>Histoplasma</u>, <u>Coccidioides</u>, and <u>Aspergillus</u> spp.)





#### Noninfectious




* Autoimmune conditions, e.g.:

* <u>Juvenile idiopathic arthritis</u>

* <u>Systemic lupus erythematosus</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u>, e.g.: 

* <u>Leukemia</u>

* <u>Lymphoma</u>

* <u>Neuroblastoma</u>

* <u>Rhabdomyosarcoma</u>

* <u>Metastatic</u> <u>nasopharyngeal cancer</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UXVbxG0)

* <u>Self-limited</u> inflammatory conditions, e.g.:

* <u>PFAPA syndrome</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TaV6PG0)

* <u>Necrotizing lymphadenitis</u>

* <u>Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease</u>





> [!TIP]
> Cervical <u>LAD</u> lasting > 6 months without change in size and character or associated systemic symptoms has a low likelihood of being <u>neoplastic</u> in origin. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fOck7c0)

## Diagnosis

### General principles [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




* Diagnostic studies are not routinely recommended.

* Consider targeted diagnostics as needed based on the most likely underlying etiology.

* Refer to a pediatric specialist (e.g., infectious disease, ENT, <u>hematology</u>-oncology, rheumatology) for:

* Atypical infection (e.g., unresponsive to <u>antibiotics</u>)

* <u>Red flags for cervical LAD in children</u>

* Diagnostic uncertainty

* See "<u>Common causes of cervical LAD in children</u>" for characteristic diagnostic findings.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Reactive LAD</u> caused by a viral <u>URTI</u> is the most common cause of acute cervical <u>LAD</u> in children and is <u>diagnosed clinically</u>.[[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2XVTxG0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




Perform <u>laboratory studies</u> based on suspected underlying <u>etiology of cervical LAD in children</u>, e.g.:




* Bacterial infection

* <u>CBC</u>

* <u>Blood culture</u> (in <u>febrile</u> or ill-appearing patients)

* Specific diagnostics for suspected causative organism, e.g.:

* <u>Diagnostics of acute tonsillitis</u>

* <u>Diagnostics for tuberculosis</u>

* <u>Diagnostics for cat scratch disease</u>

* <u>Fine-needle aspiration</u> and <u>Gram stain</u>, <u>acid-fast stain</u>, and cultures of <u>lymph node</u> material for anaerobic and <u>aerobic bacteria</u> and <u>mycobacteria</u>

* Viral infection

* <u>EBV serology</u>

* <u>Diagnostics for CMV</u>

* <u>HIV testing</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u>: <u>CBC</u>, <u>peripheral smear</u>, <u>LDH</u>

* Inflammatory conditions: <u>inflammatory markers</u> (e.g., <u>ESR</u>, <u>CRP</u>)





### Imaging [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eXVxCG0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RMVloE0)




Imaging is not routinely indicated in the evaluation of cervical <u>LAD</u> in children.




* Diagnostic uncertainty: <u>ultrasound</u> neck with or without <u>color Doppler ultrasound</u>

* Preferred first-line modality in children

* Useful to differentiate between <u>reactive lymphadenopathy</u> and <u>malignancy</u>

* See "<u>Lymph node ultrasound findings</u>" for details.

* Suspected <u>malignancy</u> or <u>deep neck infection</u> 

* <u>CT scan</u> neck with IV contrast

* <u>MRI</u> neck with or without IV contrast

* Suspected <u>lymphoma</u> or granulomatous disease: <u>chest x-ray</u>





### <u>Biopsy</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A5VR5E0)




* Indications

* <u>LAD</u> of unclear etiology lasting > 4 weeks and unresponsive to <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* <u>Red flags for cervical LAD in children</u>

* Diagnostic uncertainty

* Modalities

* <u>Excisional biopsy</u>: preferred if <u>lymphoma</u> is suspected

* <u>Core needle biopsy</u>: Consider as an alternative to <u>excisional biopsy</u> in selected patients.

* <u>Fine-needle aspiration</u> (<u>FNA</u>) <u>biopsy</u>: Consider for persistent <u>LAD</u> of unclear etiology.

## Management

Outpatient management is usually appropriate. Consider symptomatic and <u>supportive care</u> (e.g., <u>antipyretics</u>, <u>nonopioid oral analgesia in children</u>) as needed. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




> [!WARNING]
> Admit acutely ill children to hospital and consult a specialist (e.g., infectious diseases, critical care). Begin <u>management of pediatric sepsis</u>, if indicated.




### Treatment of the underlying cause [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




* Isolated cervical <u>LAD</u> with no <u>red flag features</u> in an otherwise healthy child: reassurance and observation

* Acute <u>lymphadenitis</u> likely due to a viral <u>URTI</u>: observation with follow-up in 2–4 weeks [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)

* Acute <u>lymphadenitis</u> likely due to a bacterial infection

* Initiate <u>antibiotic therapy</u> (e.g., <u>empiric antibiotic therapy for acute unilateral pyogenic cervical lymphadenitis</u>, <u>antibiotic therapy for GAS pharyngitis</u>).

* Tailor <u>antibiotics</u> when culture and <u>sensitivities</u> are available.

* See "<u>Common causes of cervical LAD in children</u>" for details.





> [!WARNING]
> Avoid <u>corticosteroids</u> in patients with suspected acute <u>lymphadenitis</u> until a definitive diagnosis is made; <u>corticosteroids</u> can alter the histopathological appearance of <u>lymphoma</u> or <u>malignancy</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




### Referrals [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOVPru0)




* Suspected <u>malignancy</u>: urgent referral to <u>hematology</u>-oncology

* Suspected <u>periodontal disease</u>: pediatric dentist

* Other specialists (e.g., infectious diseases, <u>surgery</u>, rheumatology) for additional studies and management of:

* Persistent <u>LAD</u> after observation for suspected viral etiology

* Inadequate response to <u>antibiotic therapy</u> within 72 hours

* Atypical infection (e.g., <u>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</u>, <u>nontuberculous mycobacteria</u>, fungal infection) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)

* Suspected rheumatologic condition

## Mimics

<u>Cervical lymphadenopathy</u> is the most common <u>neck mass in children</u>. Other causes of <u>neck masses in children</u> include:




* Congenital and developmental neck masses [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UXVbxG0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eXVxCG0)

* <u>Thyroglossal duct cyst</u> (second most common <u>neck mass in children</u>) [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/01Ve2t0)

* <u>Branchial cleft cyst</u>

* <u>Dermoid cyst</u>, <u>epidermoid cyst</u>

* <u>Cystic hygroma</u>

* <u>Laryngocele</u>

* <u>Fibromatosis colli</u> and <u>congenital muscular torticollis</u>

* Vascular <u>malformations</u> and tumors

* <u>Neoplastic</u> masses [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UXVbxG0)

* <u>Thyroid cancer</u>

* <u>Tumors of the salivary glands</u>

* Benign <u>soft tissue</u> <u>tumor</u> (e.g., pilomatrixomas, <u>lipomas</u>, <u>fibromas</u>, <u>neurofibromas</u>)

* <u>Rhabdomyosarcoma</u>

* <u>Neuroblastoma</u> [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/01Ve2t0)

* <u>Metastatic</u> <u>nasopharyngeal carcinoma</u>

* Other neck masses [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VXVGCG0)

* <u>Parotitis</u> (e.g., due to <u>mumps</u>)

* <u>Cervical rib</u>

* See also "Mimics" in “<u>Lymphadenopathy</u>.”
