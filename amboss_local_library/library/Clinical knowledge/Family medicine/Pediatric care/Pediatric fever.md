## Summary

<u>Fever</u>, which is defined as a core body temperature ≥ 100.4°F or 38°C, is one of the most common causes of pediatric health care visits. <u>Fever</u> is the body's normal response to an underlying infection and/or inflammatory process (e.g., rheumatologic conditions, <u>malignancy</u>). Following the introduction of routine childhood <u>pneumococcal vaccines</u> and <u>H. influenzae</u> type b (<u>Hib</u>) <u>vaccines</u>, acute pediatric <u>fevers</u> in children aged > 60 days are most commonly caused by <u>self-limited</u> viral infections. Diagnostic studies are not usually required but should be performed if any <u>red flags for pediatric fever</u> are present to identify serious bacterial infections. Children who have <u>risk factors for life-threatening pediatric infection</u> require additional evaluation, even if they appear well on examination, and may require <u>antibiotics</u> and hospital admission. <u>Supportive care</u> is the mainstay of treatment for children with <u>fever</u> and should include adequate hydration and <u>antipyretics</u> for discomfort. Education for caregivers is important to reduce <u>anxiety</u> over <u>fever</u> in children and help manage future episodes at home.




<u>Fever</u> in <u>infants</u> ≤ 60 days is more often caused by serious bacterial infections; diagnostics and management for this age group are covered in a separate article, “<u>Fever in infants ≤ 60 days</u>.”
