## Summary

Suicide is the act of intentionally ending one's own life. If this action does not have a fatal outcome, it is called a <u>suicide attempt</u>. Thoughts of wanting to die, or a preoccupation with suicide, is referred to as <u>suicidal ideation</u>, which can involve concrete plans and suicidal intent (active <u>suicidal ideation</u>). Suicide is significantly more common in men than women, while the rate of <u>attempted suicide</u> is higher in women. Many factors increase the risk of suicide, most importantly, a history of previous <u>suicide attempts</u>. <u>Suicidal ideation</u> is often associated with psychiatric illness (e.g., <u>major depressive disorder</u>, <u>bipolar disorder</u>). The most important diagnostic step is the evaluation of <u>suicidal ideation</u> and the risk of suicide. Acute management is focused on stabilization and preventing imminent acts of suicide (e.g., by admitting the patient to a psychiatric institution). Once there is no longer an immediate risk of self-harm, underlying conditions and <u>risk factors</u> must be addressed (e.g., with treatment with <u>antidepressants</u>).

## Definitions

* Suicide: the act of intentionally ending one's own life

* Suicidality: feelings of <u>suicidal ideation</u> or intent, indicating a risk of suicide

* Suicidal behavior: any thoughts of or attempts to end one's own life  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-hdDgp0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jRd_5p0)

* Suicide attempt: : a potentially injurious behavior intended to end one's own life that does not have a fatal outcome

* Suicidal ideation [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-hdDgp0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QRdu5p0)

* Passive <u>suicidal ideation</u>: thoughts of wanting to die that do not include preparatory behavior or intent

* Active <u>suicidal ideation</u>: thoughts of or plans to intentionally end one's own life

## Epidemiology

### Suicide




* <u>Incidence</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/57Yimq)

* There are ∼ 47,000 suicides every year in the US.

* Suicide is the 10<sup>th</sup> leading <u>cause of death</u> for all ages and the 2<sup>nd</sup> leading cause in individuals 15–34 years of age.

* Age

* Peak <u>incidence</u>: 45–64 years

* <u>Incidence</u> significantly increases after age 15, with another significant increase after age 45. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/57Yimq)

* Sex: <u>♂</u> > <u>♀</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/67Yj5q)

* Method: > 50% of suicides are by firearm. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8vaOb5)





### <u>Suicide attempts</u>




* <u>Incidence</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97YNLq)

* > 1 million <u>suicide attempts</u> per year

* ∼ 25 suicide attempts per suicide

* Age: Peak <u>incidence</u> is 15–19 years. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97YNLq)

* Sex: <u>♀</u> > <u>♂</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/67Yj5q)





> [!NOTE]
> Suicide deaths are more common in men, while <u>suicide attempts</u> are more common in women. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RtYlWI)




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

## Etiology

### <u>Risk factors</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QRdu5p0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)




#### Individual




* Age > 45 years  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)

* <u>Cisgender</u> male individuals  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)

* <u>CNS</u> disorders: <u>epilepsy</u>, <u>traumatic brain injury</u>

* Chronic and/or serious diseases (e.g., cancer, <u>chronic pain</u>)  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)





#### Psychosocial




* Previous <u>suicide attempt</u> (most important <u>risk factor</u>) [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* <u>Family history</u> of suicide, particularly childhood loss of a parent to suicide

* Psychiatric disorders, e.g. : [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QRdu5p0)

* <u>Mood disorders</u>: <u>major depressive disorder</u>, <u>bipolar disorder</u>

* <u>Anxiety</u>

* <u>Schizophrenia spectrum disorders</u>

* <u>Borderline personality disorder</u>

* <u>Alcohol</u> or <u>substance use disorder</u>

* <u>Eating disorders</u>

* Impulsive aggression

* Recent psychiatric hospitalization [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* History of <u>adverse childhood experiences</u> (e.g., <u>child sexual abuse</u>)

* History of <u>sexual assault</u>

* History of <u>nonsuicidal self-injury</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4id37p0)

* Access to lethal means (e.g., firearms)

* Major life events

* Socioeconomic disadvantage (e.g., unemployment, homelessness)

* Poor social support

* Unmarried

* Bullying, cyberbullying [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KNdUXq0)





### At-risk populations [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




The following populations are at increased risk of suicide due to a variety of factors, including social isolation, access to lethal means, and/or higher rates of psychiatric disorders.




* LGBTQ+ community

* Rural communities

* Incarcerated individuals

* Professions with access to lethal means (e.g., <u>health care providers</u>, farmers)

* Non-Hispanic White, American Indian, and Alaska Native individuals





> [!NOTE]
> SAD PERSONS are at risk for suicide: Sex (male), Age (> 45 years), Depression, Previous <u>suicide attempt</u>, Ethanol/substance use, Rational thinking loss (<u>psychosis</u>), Sick (chronic disease), Organized plan (acquisition of weapons/tools), No spouse or social support, Stated intent.

## Clinical evaluation

The optimal approach to evaluating patients with increased suicide risk is unclear; follow local protocols and use a combination of clinical judgment, decision support tools, and specialist consultation. Appropriate care is decided following an evaluation of the imminent risk of suicide.




### Approach [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Identify patients at high risk of suicide, including:

* Patients with positive screening for suicide (see “Screening”)

* Patients with <u>suicidal ideation</u>

* Clinical suspicion of <u>suicidal behavior</u>

* Perform a <u>brief suicide safety assessment</u> using decision support tools.

* Consult a mental health professional to perform a <u>comprehensive suicide risk assessment</u> as needed.

* Perform a focused medical assessment to determine stability for treatment in a psychiatric setting.





### Brief suicide safety assessment [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Do not use decision support tools to rule out suicide risk; they are not a substitute for clinical judgment. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Description: a series of questions to assess suicide risk and guide next steps (e.g., inquiring about <u>suicidal ideation</u>, prior attempts at self-harm, social support, stressors)

* Decision support tools

* <u>Ask Suicide-Screening Questions</u> (<u>ASQ</u>) <u>brief suicide safety assessment</u>

* <u>Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale</u> (<u>C-SSRS</u>)





![Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale](../../../media/x76cdcnommxa7ungijklpekw.jpg)

Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale




### Comprehensive suicide risk assessment




* Comprehensive suicide risk assessments are usually performed by a mental health professional.

* Assessment frameworks vary but generally include a detailed history, including <u>suicidal ideation</u>, intent, <u>risk factors</u>, and protective factors.   [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9OdNuJ0)





#### <u>Patient history</u>




It may be necessary to involve the patient's family, caregivers, and/or friends to obtain a full history. Patient consent should be obtained first, but communication may proceed without consent if there is imminent danger to the patient or others. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3idSrp0)




* Ask specifically and directly about <u>suicidal ideation</u>.  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)

* Assess whether there is a concrete intent or plan and whether the patient can act on it (e.g., possession of firearms).

* Ask about previous <u>suicide attempts</u>, including aborted attempts.

* Inquire about potential underlying conditions and contributing factors, including:

* Psychiatric conditions (e.g., <u>mood disorders</u>, <u>schizophrenia</u>) and prior psychiatric care

* Recent severe psychological <u>stress</u> and/or trauma

* <u>Alcohol</u> and substance use

* Medical conditions

* Current medications

* Ask about protective factors (e.g., social contacts, strong reasons for living).





> [!TIP]
> <u>Confidentiality</u> may need to be breached if there is clear evidence of imminent danger or harm to the patient or others. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Unexpected, sudden improvement of symptoms in patients with <u>depression</u> and a high risk of suicide may indicate an imminent suicide plan. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/didoqp0)




### Focused medical assessment [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3idSrp0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cacq5a0)




Assess the patient for concurrent medical issues and comorbid psychiatric conditions prior to discharge or transfer. This is commonly referred to as medical clearance.




* Perform a focused history and <u>physical examination</u>.

* Manage active medical issues, e.g.:

* Injuries, including <u>nonsuicidal self-injuries</u>

* <u>Substance-induced disorders</u>

* Chronic medical conditions

* Assess for other psychiatric conditions using, e.g.:

* <u>Screening for depression</u>

* <u>Evaluation and management of acute psychosis</u>

* <u>Screening for unhealthy alcohol use</u>

* Obtain targeted diagnostic studies as clinically indicated, e.g.:

* <u>Diagnostics for altered mental status</u>

* Evaluation of <u>depressive disorder due to another medical condition</u> or <u>substance/medication-induced depressive disorder</u>

* Obtain routine diagnostic studies required by receiving psychiatric facilities.  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cacq5a0)





> [!TIP]
> Obtaining routine diagnostic studies in patients with a primary psychiatric concern who are otherwise healthy is unlikely (< 1% chance) to alter a patient's disposition. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dNdoZq0)

## Differential diagnoses

### Nonsuicidal self-injury (<u>NSSI</u>) [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kidm7p0)




* Definition: the intentional infliction of harm or <u>pain</u> on oneself (e.g., cutting, burning, excessive scratching, hitting) without suicidal intent

* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* Highest <u>prevalence</u> in <u>adolescents</u>  [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QQcuDX0)

* Increased <u>prevalence</u> in child and <u>adolescent</u> psychiatric inpatients [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QQcuDX0)

* Etiology

* Psychiatric conditions (e.g., <u>borderline personality disorder</u>, <u>eating disorders</u>, <u>major depressive disorder</u>)

* Environmental factors (e.g., <u>adverse childhood experiences</u>, negative peer influences, disrupted attachment)

* Clinical features: depend on the type of self-injury

* Patients may present with acute injuries (e.g., <u>open wounds</u>, <u>burns</u>).

* Patterns of scars from previous self-injuries (e.g., parallel cutting or grouped scars from cigarette <u>burns</u>)

* Diagnosis

* Primarily clinical

* <u>Screen for suicidal intent</u> in all individuals presenting with suspected <u>NSSI</u>.

* Differential diagnoses: <u>sexual violence</u>, <u>intimate partner violence</u>, <u>older adult abuse</u>, <u>suicide attempts</u>

* Treatment [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kidm7p0)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FjcgXc0)

* Ensure that all providers use nonjudgmental communication.

* Acute management depends on the type of injury, e.g., <u>wound treatment</u> or <u>treatment of burns</u>.

* Assess for and initiate treatment of underlying psychiatric disorders, e.g., <u>treatment of depression</u>.

* Further management depends on the risk of severe injury and <u>suicidal behavior</u>.

* <u>Confidentiality</u> may need to be breached (e.g., by informing the parents of a minor about the behavior) if there is a risk of suicide or continued or severe injury.

* <u>Psychotherapy</u> may be indicated if there is repetitive and/or dangerous self-harm behavior (e.g., inflicting wounds that require medical attention).

* Potential treatments include <u>cognitive behavioral therapy</u> and <u>dialectical behavioral therapy</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> Rule out <u>suicidal ideation</u> in all patients presenting with intentional self-injury.




### Other differential diagnoses [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Unintentional self-injury

* Unintentional ingestion

* <u>Malingering</u>

* Colloquialisms





The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Initial management

### Approach [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Begin immediate stabilization using the <u>ABCDE approach</u> and manage intoxication and injuries. See:

* “<u>ABCDE approach in poisoning</u>”

* “<u>Management of trauma patients</u>”

* “<u>Acute wound management</u>”

* Initiate <u>safety precautions for patients at risk of suicide</u>.

* Consult <u>psychiatry</u> for further help (e.g., transfer to a psychiatric facility, inpatient evaluation) if patients require a <u>comprehensive suicide risk assessment</u>.   [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3idSrp0)

* Determine a safe disposition.





### Safety precautions for patients at risk of suicide [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3idSrp0)




* Establish close monitoring; do not leave the patient alone.

* Ensure that the patient is not carrying any weapons or other potentially lethal items (e.g., a belt).

* Remove all potentially dangerous items from the patient's room (e.g., sharp instruments, glass).

* <u>Manage agitation</u>, ideally without <u>calming medication</u> or restraints.





### Disposition [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Use a combination of clinical judgment, decision support tools, and specialist consultation to determine appropriate disposition.




#### Psychiatric hospitalization




* Indicated for all patients with <u>thoughts of suicide</u> and a concrete plan and/or other high-risk features (e.g., <u>substance use disorder</u>, lack of a support system, access to lethal means)

* Voluntary admission is preferred, but <u>involuntary commitment</u> may be necessary if the patient is uncooperative and there is evidence of immediate danger to themselves or others.

* For pediatric patients, <u>involuntary commitment</u> may be necessary if the parents are unavailable or do not consent.

* Ensure that all medical and surgical problems have been addressed before transfer (e.g., <u>closure of acute open wounds</u>).





#### Medical hospitalization




* May be necessary to treat underlying or concurrent medical problems (e.g., <u>substance intoxication or withdrawal</u>)

* Continue <u>safety precautions for patients at risk of suicide</u> until cleared by <u>psychiatry</u>.





#### Discharge




* May be considered for patients with <u>suicidal ideation</u> if the risk of suicide is deemed low (e.g., no concrete plan, good support system, presence of protective factors)

* When in doubt, consult a mental health professional before discharge.

* With patient consent, involve close contacts to ensure discharge to a safe environment.

* Discharge planning should include: [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-hdDgp0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3idSrp0)

* Patient education: e.g., on individual warning signs, protective factors, and necessary follow-up

* Safety planning: formulating a plan for future crises together with the patient, including <u>coping strategies</u> and who to reach out to.

* Lethal means counseling: e.g., recommending the removal of firearms or prescription medications

* Referral for follow-up, ideally within 72 hours  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Asking close contacts to check on the patient, e.g., with a text message





> [!WARNING]
> If there is any doubt about whether a patient is at risk of suicide, consult a mental health professional before discharge. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Counsel patients at risk for suicide and their families to remove firearms from the home. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

## Long-term management

Long-term management of patients with <u>thoughts of suicide</u> mainly involves treating the underlying psychiatric disorder, counseling, and frequent follow-ups.




### Pharmacological treatement [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)




Consider the following pharmacological treatment for underlying psychiatric conditions.




* <u>Major depressive disorder</u>

* <u>SSRIs</u>; : can initially increase energy and motivation, which may increase <u>suicidal behavior</u>;  in <u>adolescents</u> and young adults, especially within the first few months of treatment  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-hdDgp0)

* <u>Tricyclic antidepressants</u>, <u>MAO inhibitors</u>, <u>venlafaxine</u>: not preferred due to higher toxicity in overdose [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jid_rp0)

* See “Management of <u>major depressive disorder</u>.”

* <u>Bipolar disorder</u>

* <u>Mood stabilizers</u>: e.g., <u>lithium</u>, <u>valproic acid</u>

* See “<u>Management of bipolar disorder</u>.”

* <u>Schizophrenia</u>

* <u>Antipsychotics</u>: e.g., <u>risperidone</u>, <u>aripiprazole</u>

* See “<u>Management of schizophrenia</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> <u>Lithium</u> has been shown to reduce suicide rates in patients with <u>major depressive disorder</u> and <u>bipolar disorder</u>. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)




> [!WARNING]
> As <u>antidepressants</u> such as <u>SSRIs</u> can increase energy and motivation before an improvement in mood occurs, the risk of suicide may increase during the first weeks of treatment. Close observation and frequent follow-ups are vital! [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-hdDgp0)




### Nonpharmacological treatment [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zhdrgp0)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Z3dZSp0)




* Psychotherapeutic interventions [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JRdsLp0)

* <u>Cognitive behavioral therapy</u>

* <u>Dialectical behavioral therapy</u>

* <u>Mindfulness</u>-based <u>cognitive therapy</u>

* Counseling, e.g.:

* Improvement of social circumstances;  that constitute <u>risk factors for suicidal behavior</u> (e.g., changing living conditions and increasing social contacts and a sense of connectedness)

* Development of;  personal skills that have a protective effect (e.g., problem-solving and coping skills; ) and encouraging reflection about religious and/or cultural beliefs to promote a sense of purpose
