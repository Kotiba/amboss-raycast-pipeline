# Capacity, competence, and consent

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Legal medicine and professionalism > Capacity, competence, and consent*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7u04s3)

---

## Summary

<u>Decision-making capacity</u>, <u>legal competence</u>, and <u>informed consent</u> are pillars of patient <u>autonomy</u> and essential components of health care provision. To maintain patient <u>autonomy</u>, the informed decisions of patients with <u>decision-making capacity</u> must be respected whenever possible. Because <u>decision-making capacity</u> can become impaired in many circumstances, clinicians must be able at all times to evaluate whether a patient has <u>decision-making capacity</u> and to what degree it is impaired. For patients who lack <u>decision-making capacity</u>, a <u>surrogate decision-maker</u> may be appointed. In most circumstances, a parent or guardian is required to make decisions for <u>unemancipated minors</u>; exceptions include decisions related to reproductive health, mental health, and <u>substance use disorders</u>. <u>Legal competence</u> is related but not identical to <u>decision-making capacity</u>. It applies to a broader context than medical decision-making and is assessed by a court of law. Clinicians must obtain <u>informed consent</u> from patients before providing medical care whenever possible. This involves <u>assessing decision-making capacity</u> and voluntariness, <u>full disclosure</u> of the details of proposed care (e.g., indications, risk-benefit profile, and alternatives), and ensuring that these are fully understood by the patient. In exceptional cases, care may be provided without <u>informed consent</u>, e.g., <u>implied consent</u> for life-saving care, <u>involuntary commitment</u>, or court-ordered treatment. Ethical dilemmas involving decision-making may require consultation with a medical ethicist and/or institutional lawyer to ensure that decisions adhere to applicable laws and ethical best practices.




See also “<u>Principles of medical law and ethics</u>.”

## Decision-making capacity

### Overview [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ezbx7w)




* Definition: a patient's ability to understand and communicate a health care decision based on their preferences and values

* Key considerations

* Generally, a patient must be ≥ 18 years of age or a <u>legally emancipated minor</u> to have <u>decision-making capacity</u>.

* Capacity should be assessed and determined by the treating clinician.

* A patient may demonstrate capacity to make some health care decisions, but not others.

* <u>Decision-making capacity</u> is not automatically precluded by <u>intellectual disability</u> (e.g., <u>trisomy 21</u>), mental illness, <u>low health literacy</u>, or <u>neurodegenerative disease</u> (e.g., <u>dementia</u>).

* Unstable <u>manic</u> or depressive episodes or <u>altered mental status</u> may transiently preclude <u>decision-making capacity</u>.

* Required components: All of the following are required for a patient (or their <u>surrogate</u>) to demonstrate capacity. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rd1frf0)

* Communication of a choice: the ability to clearly and consistently communicate the decision

* Demonstration of understanding: the ability to comprehend the information provided, including the different options available

* Appreciation of relevant facts: the ability to recognize and evaluate the facts relevant to the situation

* Reasoning in medical decision-making: the ability to describe the <u>thought process</u> behind the decision





### Assessing decision-making capacity [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rd1frf0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Oe1I_f0)




<u>Decision-making capacity</u> for low-risk medical decisions  can be assumed if the patient demonstrates understanding during a conversation.




* Indications

* <u>Informed consent</u> is required (e.g., prior to <u>blood transfusions</u>, invasive procedures).

* Incapacity is suspected.

* Prior to assessment

* Address <u>low health literacy</u> and communication barriers (e.g., <u>hearing aids</u>, medical interpreter).

* Treat reversible causes of incapacity (e.g., intoxication, <u>delirium</u>).

* Consider the cultural context of the decision and its impact on the patient's values.

* Directed interview: There are four required components.

* Choice: “What decision have you come to?”

* Understanding: “In your own words, can you describe the risks and benefits of the available options?”

* Appreciation 

* “How would you describe your current health situation?”

* “Do you think you would benefit from some form of medical intervention?”

* Reasoning: “How did you come to your decision?”

* Further assessment: indicated if capacity remains uncertain after a directed interview

* Consider using a formal assessment tool.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Id1Yrf0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/M21MRT0)

* Repeat the assessment to evaluate for changes in capacity.





> [!TIP]
> Identify and treat reversible causes of incapacity (e.g., <u>delirium</u>, infection, intoxication, medication) before assessing capacity.




> [!WARNING]
> Do not mistake pseudoincapacity (i.e., a lack of understanding due to insufficient <u>patient counseling</u> and/or use of jargon) or a decision against medical advice for lack of <u>decision-making capacity</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rd1frf0)




### Shared decision-making [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Yzbnrw)




* Definition: a process in which the patient and clinician work together to make a health care decision

* Indication: nonemergency situations in which there is <u>clinical equipoise</u>

* Goals

* Patient empowerment by considering their values, cultural beliefs, and preferences when making a medical decision

* Improved patient satisfaction and buy-in (e.g., <u>medication adherence</u>)





#### Three-step model for <u>shared decision-making</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hHXcJz)




* Choice talk: an introductory discussion in which the patient is informed that there are choices available and that they can be involved in the decision-making process

* Invite the patient to participate in the decision-making process.

* Explain that there are different ways to approach the decision.

* Assess the patient's current knowledge and understanding.

* Option talk: a discussion about all of the available options

* Discuss the risks and benefits of the available options, using <u>decision aids</u> if available.  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7d14rf0)

* Ask the patient about their preferences.

* Conclude by checking the patient's understanding of all of the options.

* Decision talk: a discussion in which the patient either makes a decision based on their preferences or defers the decision

* Ask the patient to confirm or defer their decision.

* Review the decision using the <u>teach-back method</u>.





> [!TIP]
> If a patient defers a decision, revisit the “decision talk” in subsequent conversations.




### Surrogate decision-making [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GCaBt5)




* Definition: a model in which another person makes treatment decisions for the patient because they lack <u>decision-making capacity</u> and/or <u>legal competence</u>

* General

* A <u>surrogate</u> may be appointed by the patient (e.g., <u>medical power of attorney</u>), legally appointed (e.g., court-ordered guardian), or <u>next of kin</u> (if no <u>advance directive</u> exists).

* <u>Advance directives</u> or surrogates are only used if the patient has lost the ability to make their own decisions. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/K0XUS9)

* <u>Advance directives</u> may be revoked by the patient at any time if they retain decision-making competence.

* <u>Surrogate</u> decisions should be based on what the patient would have wanted.

* Hierarchy of medical decision-making  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ezb8uw)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vzbAuw)
1. * A mentally competent patient capable of making their own decisions
2. * Advance health care directive: prespecified legal instructions from the patient used to guide medical decision-making

* Living will: a legal document in which individuals describe their wishes regarding their health care (e.g., to maintain, withhold, or withdraw life-sustaining care) should they become incapacitated

* Durable medical power of attorney (<u>health care proxy</u>): a legal document through which an individual designates a <u>surrogate</u> to make specific health care decisions

* Oral advance directive: an oral statement made by a patient regarding their preferences prior to incapacitation
3. * Next of kin

* Spouse

* Adult child

* Parent

* Adult sibling

* A close friend (in approx. 50% of US states)
4. * Ethics committee or legal consult

* Caveats: if the patient's preferences cannot be determined and there is a disagreement regarding the course of action (e.g., the wishes of a designated <u>surrogate</u> who is not a family member conflict with the wishes of family members)

* The clinician should facilitate a meeting between the disagreeing parties with the aim of reaching an agreement about what the patient would have desired.

* No matter what the outcome of the conflict, the wishes of the designated <u>surrogate</u> should be followed.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Oral advance directives</u> may pose problems of interpretation, as oral statements are not as specific or easy to confirm as written statements. The <u>validity</u> of an <u>oral advance directive</u> increases if the patient has made an informed choice, the instructions were specific, and the directive was confirmed by multiple people.




> [!TIP]
> Patients with <u>decision-making capacity</u> and competence have the right to provide or withdraw <u>informed consent</u> at any time (even during a procedure).




> [!NOTE]
> The Spouse ChiPS in: Spouse, Children, Parents, Siblings, other relatives/close friends (priority of <u>surrogate decision-making</u>)




### Special situations [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sd1trf0)




* Harm to others: A decision to decline treatment may be disregarded if that decision endangers others.

* Pregnant individuals

* A person has the right to refuse certain treatments even if their decision poses a risk to the unborn fetus (e.g., declining a <u>cesarean section</u>).  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-61Dn30)

* For patients with severe mental health disorders, a psychiatric professional should be integrated into the care team to ensure the <u>core ethical principles</u> are upheld. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1aX2j9)

* Changes in capacity: If a patient with capacity makes a decision, it cannot be reversed if the patient becomes incapacitated unless a designated <u>surrogate</u> weighs in to reverse the decision.





#### Medical decision-making in pediatrics




* Pediatric definitions [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ynYdDp)

* Minor: any person < 18 years of age (in most states)

* Emancipated minor: a minor who fulfills at least one of the following criteria

* Lives separately from parents and is financially self-reliant

* Is married

* Is on duty in the armed forces

* Mature minor

* An <u>unemancipated minor</u> who is deemed to have capacity

* The status of a <u>mature minor</u> is decided by individual state courts.

* The exact definition of a <u>mature minor</u> varies state-by-state.

* General [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_nY5Dp)

* <u>Unemancipated minors</u> do not possess <u>decision-making capacity</u>.

* The consent for medical procedures or treatments of <u>unemancipated minors</u> is given by the patient's surrogates (i.e., parents or caretakers).

* See “<u>Informed consent</u>” below for exceptions and more information.

* <u>Emancipated minors</u> are considered to be capable of medical decision-making.

* Mature minor doctrine: a common-law rule that allows <u>mature minors</u> to consent to treatment under certain conditions  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/azbQrw)

* The minor is an older <u>adolescent</u> (the age varies by state law).

* The minor is capable of understanding the information regarding the medical procedure.

* The benefits of the procedure clearly outweigh the risks, and the risks are not high.

## Legal competence

* Definition: the legal assessment of a patient's ability to freely make conscious decisions (including those regarding their care)  [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/K7XU5z)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bzbHrw)

* General

* Assessed by a court of law (with input from the patient's family and clinicians as needed)

* Clinicians do not have the authority to pronounce individuals legally incompetent. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ezbx7w)

* If an individual is determined legally incompetent, the court will assign a guardian to make decisions on their behalf. ;  [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p7XL5z)

* The court may waive the appointment of a guardian or grant a limited guardianship  if there is a durable power of attorney.

* Generally, a guardian cannot issue the commitment of their ward to a mental health facility.

* The directives of a guardian override the directives of family members.

* Questions of <u>legal competence</u> arise in the presence of reduced mental capacity (e.g., severe mental illness, intoxication, impulsive/constantly changing decisions, decisions that are inconsistent with the patient's values)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Legal competence</u> assesses an individual's <u>global</u> decision-making ability (e.g., relating to financial, property, and health care decisions), whereas <u>decision-making capacity</u> is a functional assessment that can vary depending on the situation. For example, a patient may have the capacity to choose between blood pressure medications but not to consent to complex <u>surgery</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Oe1I_f0)

## Informed consent

### Overview [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/W0XPU9)




* Definition: the process of attaining the patient's authorization for a medical test, treatment, or procedure

* Key considerations

* Ensure that there is sufficient time before the intervention for the patient to make a well-considered decision.

* A patient with <u>decision-making capacity</u> is free to provide or revoke their consent at any time; it does not need to be in writing.

* Required components: applies to the patient or their <u>surrogate</u> [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d0XoU9)

* Voluntariness: The decision must be made without <u>coercion</u>.

* Capacity: <u>Decision-making capacity</u> must be demonstrated before consenting to the intervention.

* Comprehension: The decision-maker must demonstrate understanding of the ramifications of the proposed intervention.

* Disclosure: Relevant medical information regarding the intervention must be disclosed.





### Obtaining <u>informed consent</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sd1trf0)




<u>Informed consent</u> should be obtained by the <u>health care provider</u> performing the intervention.



1. * Inform the patient of the benefits, risks, alternatives, and indications of the intervention and the nature of their illness, including:

* Known complications and estimated risk of <u>death</u> and <u>morbidity</u>

* Alternative interventions

* The expected nature of the disease course without any intervention

* If any aspect of the proposed intervention is experimental
2. * Notify the patient about the identity and level of training of each potential participant in the intervention, allowing them to either give or withhold <u>informed consent</u>.
3. * <u>Assess the patient's decision-making capacity</u> ; (e.g., using the <u>teach-back method</u>) and ability to consent voluntarily.
4. * Document the decision that the patient (or their <u>surrogate</u>) has clearly articulated.




> [!NOTE]
> Use your BRAIN when obtaining <u>informed consent</u>: Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Indications, Nature




> [!WARNING]
> Performing an intervention without having obtained <u>informed consent</u> may legally constitute battery and/or negligence. [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z21r4T0)




> [!TIP]
> The amount of information shared when obtaining <u>informed consent</u> depends on the frequency and severity of the risks involved, e.g., less information needs to be disclosed for <u>venipuncture</u> than for <u>cardiac catheterization</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sd1trf0)




### Exceptions to standard informed consent [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60XjS9)




* Life-threatening emergencies (e.g., an unconscious <u>trauma patient</u> without a <u>surrogate</u> present)

* The patient lacks <u>decision-making capacity</u>, but their <u>surrogate</u> has authorized intervention.

* The patient decided to waive the legal right of <u>informed consent</u>.

* Disclosing may pose a threat to the patient or affect their <u>decision-making capacity</u> (i.e., <u>therapeutic privilege</u>).





> [!TIP]
> Difficulties in obtaining consent should not delay life-saving procedures.




### Language and use of an interpreter [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60XjS9)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/10X2U9)




* Discuss health care decisions with patients in terms they can relate to.

* Communicate in a language that the patient understands.

* Request an interpreter if you are unable to communicate with the patient in a language in which you can have a comprehensive discussion and assess the patient's understanding of the relevant information.

* Both in-person and remote (e.g., phone, video) interpreter services are appropriate.

* Communicating without an interpreter can result in patients unknowingly consenting to unwanted procedures, misunderstanding their diagnosis, and/or complying poorly with medical advice.

* For more information about particular instances of the use of medical interpretation, see “<u>General concepts of patient counseling</u>” in the “<u>Patient communication and counseling</u>” article.





> [!TIP]
> Multilingual relatives are not acceptable alternatives to professional interpreters in the nonemergency medical setting.




### Parental consent for minors [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aaXQQ9)




* Overview

* Minors are considered legally incompetent to make medical decisions.

* Parental consent is generally required before a minor receives medical care; exceptions are listed below.

* Although not legally mandatory, it is recommendable that clinicians obtain the minor's approval for medical care.

* For children to participate in medical research, documented consent must be obtained from parents or guardians and assent must be obtained from minors. [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/znYrDp)

* If the parents of the patient are themselves minors, grandparents may give consent for their grandchildren.

* For minors who have been removed from their parental care and whose parent's right to consent has been revoked by a juvenile court, the court must assign a guardian (e.g., grandparent) who can provide consent.

* In the absence of another guardian, <u>child protective services</u> authorize all health care services for children whose parents have had their parental rights terminated.

* Exceptions to the requirement of parental consent

* Emergency and/or life-saving interventions (e.g., severe trauma, <u>suicidal ideation</u>, <u>blood transfusion</u> for life-threatening hemorrhage). [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZaXZQ9)

* The minor is legally emancipated.

* Care regarding sex (e.g., <u>contraception</u>, <u>STIs</u>, <u>pregnancy</u> care except for abortion in most states)  [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YaXnQ9)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XaX9Q9)

* Addiction care (e.g., health services to treat drug and/or <u>alcohol</u> dependency) [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YaXnQ9)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/baXHQ9)

* Minors who are parents themselves or who are married

* Minors should be encouraged to discuss medical issues with their parents regardless of the exceptions that apply.

* Refusal to consent [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z0Xri9)

* Generally, parents and legal guardians may refuse any treatment for a minor under their care in non-life-threatening situations.

* A parent cannot refuse an emergency life-saving intervention for a minor for any reason (e.g., religious refusal). [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZaXZQ9)

* This refusal is only acceptable if that decision does not pose a risk of serious harm to the minor. Legal intervention (e.g., court order) may be necessary to mandate treatment for a non-emergency but fatal medical condition against the parent's or legal guardian's refusal to consent. [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-0XDi9)

* Clinicians should always attempt to address concerns motivating the refusal of treatment (e.g., misunderstanding of the procedure, fear of potential side effects).

* Clinicians should respect religious beliefs and/or cultural values of patients that may affect treatment and make therapeutic decisions accordingly within the legal scope of what treatment may be refused.

* Parents are legally permitted to refuse <u>vaccinations</u> for their children. [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iYXJK9)

* In rare cases, it may be appropriate to overrule a parental decision to decline <u>immunization</u> (e.g., in emergencies such as a child with a contaminated <u>puncture wound</u> and signs of life-threatening <u>tetanus</u> infection).  [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYXuK9)

* Efforts should be made to understand the parents' refusal to vaccinate their children and, where possible, to help them understand the advantages of <u>vaccination</u>.





### Unexpected findings during <u>surgery</u> [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xHXEsz)




* The patient should be informed about the possibility of intraoperative findings that may require more intervention than originally planned.

* If consent was not obtained

* If a finding requires immediate action (e.g., <u>appendicitis</u> is found during <u>surgery</u> for <u>ectopic pregnancy</u>), the procedure can be performed without obtaining the patient's consent.

* If a finding does not require immediate action (e.g., findings concerning for pulmonary <u>malignancy</u> during <u>surgery</u> for <u>tension pneumothorax</u>), the patient should give <u>informed consent</u> before any other procedures are performed.
