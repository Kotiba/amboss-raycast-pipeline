# Approach to the agitated or violent patient

## Summary

<u>Agitation</u> is a common, nonspecific symptom of many medical, psychiatric, psychosocial, and substance-related conditions. Initial management should aim to ensure the safety of the patient and staff and identify and treat critical <u>causes of agitation</u>. Once safety measures are initiated, <u>deescalation techniques</u> should be prioritized to help the patient calm down, avoid progression to aggressive and/or violent behavior, and allow a full medical and psychiatric evaluation. Stabilization, treatment of critical illnesses, alleviation of distressing symptoms, prevention of complications, and maintenance of patient dignity are major priorities for care. <u>Calming medications</u> (e.g., sedatives, tranquilizers) and <u>physical restraints</u> should only be used to prevent harm to the patient or others if <u>deescalation techniques</u> are ineffective. Diagnostic studies may be required to evaluate for suspected underlying causes or complications. Optimal management requires familiarity with local institutional protocols and, in some cases, the involvement of a <u>behavioral emergency response team</u>.
