## Summary

<u>Childbirth</u> begins with the onset of <u>labor</u>, which consists of contractions that lead to progressive <u>cervical dilation</u> and effacement, eventually resulting in the <u>birth</u> of the <u>infant</u> and expulsion of the <u>placenta</u>. The process of normal <u>childbirth</u> depends on a high degree of anatomical and physiological compatibility between the mother and child. The <u>birth canal</u> is the passage consisting of the mother's bony <u>pelvis</u> and <u>soft tissues</u> through which a fetus passes during vaginal delivery. Fetal orientation during <u>childbirth</u> is described in terms of lie, presenting part, position, attitude of the presenting part, and station. The clinical status of the mother and fetus should be consistently monitored during <u>labor</u> and delivery. <u>Obstetric analgesia</u> should be offered, and <u>prophylaxis for neonatal GBS infection</u> should be given during <u>labor</u> if indicated. While vaginal delivery is typically preferred, <u>cesarean delivery</u> may be indicated under certain circumstances. Complications of <u>normal vaginal delivery</u> include <u>perineal lacerations</u>, hemorrhage, nerve injuries, and <u>coccydynia</u>.




See “<u>Abnormal labor and delivery</u>” for <u>intrapartum</u> complications and their management.

### Fetal lie




* Definition: relation of the fetal long axis to the long axis of the maternal <u>uterus</u>

* Types

* Longitudinal lie: fetus is in the same axis (most common)

* Transverse lie: fetus is at a 90° angle

* Oblique lie: fetus is at a 45° angle

### Fetal presentation




* Definition: part of the fetus that overlies the maternal <u>pelvic inlet</u>

* Types

* Cephalic presentation: head (most common)

* Breech presentation: buttocks or feet

* Frank breech: flexed hips and extended knees (buttocks presenting)

* Complete breech: thighs and legs flexed (cannonball position)

* Single footling breech: hip of one leg is flexed and the knee of the other is extended (one foot presenting)

* Double footling breech: both thighs and legs are extended (feet presenting)

* Compound presentation: ≥ 1 anatomical presenting part (e.g., cephalic or <u>breech presentation</u> with presentation of an extremity)

* Shoulder presentation: shoulder presentations combined with a transverse or <u>oblique lie</u>

### Fetal position




* Definition: relationship and orientation (i.e., fetal occiput pointing towards maternal left or right) of the presenting fetal part to the maternal <u>pelvis</u>

* Types

* Occiput anterior position: Fetal occiput points towards maternal <u>symphysis pubis</u>; fetus faces downwards.

* Left occiput anterior (<u>LOA</u>): Fetal back faces the maternal left, <u>anterior fontanelle</u> faces the maternal right, <u>sagittal</u> suture lies in the right oblique diameter (most common position).

* Right occiput anterior (ROA): Fetal back faces the maternal right, <u>anterior fontanelle</u> faces the maternal left, <u>sagittal</u> suture lies in the left oblique diameter.

* Occiput posterior position: Fetal occiput points towards the maternal sacral promontory with face to <u>pubis</u> <u>symphysis</u>; the fetus faces upward

* <u>Sacrum</u> in <u>breech presentation</u>

* Mentum (chin) in extended cephalic (face) presentation

### Fetal attitude [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Yuanpm)




* Definition: degree of extension/<u>flexion</u> of the fetal head during <u>cephalic presentation</u>

* Types

* Vertex presentation (maximally flexed); most common attitude

* Brow presentation (partially extended)

* Face presentation (maximally extended)

* Mentum <u>anterior</u>  <u>face presentation</u> : <u>Spontaneous vaginal delivery</u> is possible .

* Mentum <u>posterior</u> <u>face presentation</u>

* Forehead presentation (partially flexed; military attitude): <u>Spontaneous vaginal delivery</u> is possible .





### Station (Obstetrics) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xCaE85)



* Definition: measurement (in cm) of the presenting part above and below the maternal <u>ischial spine</u>




| Station | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | The presenting part is at the level of the <u>ischial spines</u> |
| -1, -2, -3 | 1, 2, and 3 cm above the level of the <u>ischial spines</u>, respectively |
| +1, +2, +3 | 1, 2, and 3 cm below the level of the <u>ischial spines</u>, respectively |




* Engagement (Obstetrics)

* When the widest transverse diameter of the head (presenting part) passes through the <u>pelvic inlet</u>

* Use the rule of fifths: engagement is clinically identified when ≤ 2/5 of the fetal head are felt above the <u>symphysis pubis</u> through the maternal abdomen

### Synclitism




* Definition: parallelism between the <u>pelvic</u> plane and the plane of the fetal head

* In asynclitism, the <u>sagittal</u> suture is in the transverse diameter of the <u>pelvic inlet</u> and not between the <u>symphysis pubis</u> and sacral promontory.

* Anterior asynclitism (Naegele obliquity)

* The <u>sagittal</u> suture is positioned towards the sacral promontory

* <u>Spontaneous vaginal delivery</u> possible

* Posterior asynclitism (Litzmann obliquity)

* The <u>sagittal</u> suture is positioned towards the <u>symphysis pubis</u>

* <u>Normal vaginal delivery</u> is impossible. → premature <u>cesarean delivery</u>

### Rupture of membranes (<u>ROM</u>)




* Definition: the rupture of the <u>amniotic sac</u> followed by the release of <u>amniotic fluid</u>

* Spontaneous rupture of membranes: : <u>ROM</u> that usually occurs at the onset of <u>labor</u> and is unprovoked by health practitioners

* Artificial rupture of membranes (<u>amniotomy</u>): A procedure in which the <u>amniotic sac</u> is ruptured in order to release <u>amniotic fluid</u>.

* Delayed rupture of membranes: <u>ROM</u> that occurs during fetal expulsion, after <u>cervical dilation</u> and effacement

* <u>Abnormal rupture of membranes</u>

* <u>Premature rupture of membranes</u> (<u>PROM</u>)

* <u>Preterm premature rupture of membranes</u> (<u>PPROM</u>)

* <u>Prolonged rupture of membranes</u>

* Clinical features: sudden “gush” of pale yellow or clear fluid from the <u>vagina</u> (may also be a constant leaking sensation)

* Diagnostics

* Consider sterile <u>speculum examination</u> if the diagnosis is uncertain.

* Suggestive findings include pooling, positive <u>litmus test</u> or <u>nitrazine test</u>, and <u>ferning</u>.

### Management of <u>labor</u> by stage




* <u>First stage of labor</u>

* <u>Analgesia</u> upon request

* <u>Fetal heart rate</u> monitoring

* Determine <u>fetal position</u> via abdominal (see <u>Leopold maneuvers</u>) and <u>pelvic</u> (palpation of fetal sutures/<u>fontanelles</u>) examination.
* If the <u>fetal position</u> cannot be determined by examination, perform <u>ultrasound</u>.

* Regular assessment of <u>cervical dilation</u> and descent of the fetal head

* In case of heavier bleeding but normal maternal <u>vital signs</u> and <u>fetal heart tracing</u> (e.g., increased <u>bloody show</u>), delivery should proceed as planned with frequent observation.

* <u>Second stage of labor</u>

* Help the mother to find comfortable and safe positions.

* Guide the delivery of the fetus through the vaginal canal (See “<u>Mechanics of childbirth</u>” for expected fetal movements).

* <u>Clamp the umbilical cord</u> after no less than 30–60 seconds.  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PUWWWk0)

* See “Delivery of the <u>infant</u>” in “<u>Manually assisted vaginal delivery</u>” for detailed instructions.

* Third stage of <u>labor</u>

* <u>Active management of the third stage of labor</u> (reduces the risk of <u>postpartum hemorrhage</u>)

* <u>Oxytocin</u> reduces blood loss by inducing stronger uterine contractions.

* <u>Controlled cord traction</u> (<u>Brandt-Andrews maneuver</u>) if <u>placenta</u> is not delivered spontaneously

* See “Delivery of the <u>placenta</u>” in “<u>Manually assisted vaginal delivery</u>” for detailed instructions.

* Examine the <u>placenta</u> to confirm completeness (regular surface with complete cotyledons), which should also consist of the <u>umbilical cord</u>, complete <u>amniotic membranes</u>, and three <u>blood vessels</u> (one <u>vein</u>, two <u>arteries</u>).

* Repair any <u>obstetric lacerations</u>.

* Fourth stage of <u>labor</u>: Monitoring to rule out <u>postpartum hemorrhage</u> or <u>preeclampsia</u>

### Normal mechanics of childbirth [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Yuanpm)




Adaptation to the different forms of the <u>pelvic</u> region requires a great deal of rotation.



1. * Engagement, descent, and increased <u>flexion</u> (occur simultaneously)

* The head engages below the plane of the <u>pelvic inlet</u>.

* The presenting part begins to descend into the <u>birth canal</u>.

* The chin of the fetus moves towards its chest.
2. * <u>Internal rotation</u>: The fetal head rotates by 90° (two 45° steps) in the midpelvis, from a transverse to <u>anterior</u>-<u>posterior</u> position.
3. * Extension: The fetal head, lying behind the <u>symphysis pubis</u> bone and the <u>pelvic floor</u>, acts upwards and forwards.
4. * Restitution: The fetal head rotates 45° in the opposite direction as it passes through the <u>pelvic outlet</u>.
5. * <u>External rotation</u>: The <u>anterior</u> shoulder rotates 45° anteriorly as it meets the maternal <u>pelvic floor</u>. This action is transmitted to the head which also rotates 45°, placing the head in its original transverse position.
6. * Expulsion: Delivery of the head, <u>anterior</u> shoulder followed by the <u>posterior</u> shoulder, and the body

## Intrapartum fetal monitoring

### Electronic fetal heart rate monitoring [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IAaYOM)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rAafOM)




* Description: widely used diagnostic tool during <u>3<sup>rd</sup> trimester</u> and <u>labor</u> to detect signs of <u>fetal distress</u>

* Procedure

* Determination of the <u>fetal heart rate</u> (<u>FHR</u>), presence of <u>acceleration</u> or deceleration by <u>Doppler ultrasound</u>, recording beats per minute (bpm) in the upper curve (cardiogram)

* During <u>birth</u>, the <u>FHR</u> may be monitored internally via an electrode that is attached to the fetal head (fetal scalp electrode monitoring).

* Rupture of the membranes must have occurred or an <u>amniotomy</u> performed

* Used when external monitoring is difficult (e.g., maternal <u>obesity</u>, <u>polyhydramnios</u>, <u>multiple gestations</u>)

* Mechanoelectrical measurement of uterine contractions via a pressure transducer, recording in the lower curve in kPa (tocodynagraph)

* Indications 

* During <u>labor</u>

* Admission in the <u>labor</u> ward

* In every case of complication during <u>pregnancy</u> or delivery, such as impending <u>preterm birth</u>, abnormalities of the fetal <u>heart</u>, <u>multiple pregnancy</u>, suspected <u>placental insufficiency</u>, uterine bleeding, <u>tocolysis</u>





### Fetal heart rate [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dua1tm)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wuahtm)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7Aa4OM)




* In <u>CTG</u>, the <u>FHR</u> is designated as the baseline or basal <u>heart rate</u> and is normally 110–160 bpm.

* <u>Tachycardia</u>

* Mild <u>tachycardia</u>: <u>FHR</u> of 160–180 bpm for > 10 minutes

* Severe <u>tachycardia</u>: <u>FHR</u> of ≥180 bpm for > 10 minutes

* Causes:  <u>stress</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, maternal <u>fever</u>; , medication (e.g., betamimetics for the treatment of <u>tocolysis</u>), <u>chorioamnionitis</u>, fetal <u>arrhythmias</u>, fetal <u>anemia</u>, <u>hypoxia</u>

* <u>Bradycardia</u>

* Mild <u>bradycardia</u>: <u>FHR</u> of < 110 bpm for > 3 minutes

* Severe <u>bradycardia</u>: <u>FHR</u> of < 100 bpm for > 3 minutes

* Causes:  <u>supine hypotensive syndrome</u>, fetal <u>heart</u> defects; , <u>central nervous system</u> anomalies, severe <u>hypoxia</u>

* Methods to assess <u>FHR</u>: <u>Nonstress test</u> (<u>NST</u>) and <u>contraction stress test</u> (<u>CST</u>);  are performed during the <u>third trimester</u> of <u>pregnancy</u> to measure <u>FHR</u> reactivity to fetal movements and <u>FHR</u> reactivity in response to uterine contractions  respectively. See <u>nonstress test</u> and <u>contraction stress test</u> in “<u>Prenatal care</u>” for details.

#### Fetal heart rate variability [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GCXBtZ0)




On <u>CTG</u>, variability of <u>FHR</u> is represented by the oscillation of the <u>FHR</u> around the baseline and is determined by measuring the amplitude between the highest and lowest turning point of the <u>FHR</u> curve.




| Overview of <u>fetal heart rate variability</u> |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type | Oscillation amplitude | Causes |
| <br>Moderate variability <br> | <br>* 6–25 bpm<br><br> | <br><br>* Physiological fluctuation of <u>FHR</u><br><br>* Normal finding<br><br><br> |
| Absent variability | <br>* Undetectable amplitude<br><br> | <br>* Severe fetal <u>acidemia</u><br><br> |
| <br>Minimal variability <br> | <br>* < 6 bpm<br><br> | <br><br>* Sleeping fetus<br><br>* Effects of <u>opioids</u> or <u>magnesium</u><br><br>* Fetal <u>hypoxia</u><br><br><br> |
| <br>Marked variability <br> | <br>* > 25 bpm<br><br> | <br><br>* Fetal <u>hypoxia</u><br><br>* <u>Umbilical cord compression</u><br><br>* May precede a decrease in variability<br><br><br> |
| <br>Sinusoidal variability <br> | <br><br>* 5–15 bpm<br><br>* <u>FHR</u> wave resembles a sinus wave<br><br><br> | <br><br>* Severe fetal <u>anemia</u><br><br>* Severe fetal <u>hypoxia</u><br><br><br> |
| Pseudosinosoidal variability | <br><br>* Similar appearance to sinusoidal variability<br><br>* Irregularly shape and amplitude of the <u>FHR</u> curves<br><br><br> | <br>* Maternal <u>meperidine</u> use<br><br> |

#### Acceleration (CTG) [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7Aa4OM)




* Description: a normal temporal increase in the <u>FHR</u> from the baseline by > 15 bpm for more than 15 seconds but less than 10 minutes if the <u>gestational age</u> is > 32 weeks, or by > 10 bpm for more than 10 seconds if the <u>gestational age</u> is < 32 weeks

* Interpretation

* The presence of > 2 <u>accelerations</u> within a span of 20 minutes indicates a reactive <u>fetal heart rate tracing</u>.

* If the <u>acceleration</u> lasts longer than 10 minutes, it should be considered a baseline change in the <u>fetal heart rate</u>.

#### Decelerations (CTG) [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IAaYOM)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GAaBOM)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sAatOM)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tAaXlM)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uAaplM)



* Description: a temporary decline in the <u>FHR</u> of > 15 bpm for a maximum duration of 3 minutes




| Overview of types of fetal deceleration |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type | Etiology | Characteristics | Measures |
| <br>Early deceleration  <br> | <br>* Compression of the head during a contraction triggering a <u>vagal</u> response<br><br> | <br><br>* The beginning and end of <u>decelerations</u> correspond with the progression of a contraction (the deceleration reaches its minimum, referred to as the <u>nadir</u>, when the contraction curve attains its peak).<br><br>* Onset to <u>nadir</u> is gradual (≥ 30 seconds).<br><br>* Typically occurs during active <u>labor</u> when the <u>cervix</u> is dilated ≥ 5 cm and the head is engaged within the <u>pelvic cavity</u><br><br><br> | <br>* <u>FHR tracings</u> are usually normal (no <u>fetal distress</u>)<br><br> |
| <br>Late deceleration  <br> | <br>* <u>Uteroplacental insufficiency</u> (leads to fetal <u>hypoxia</u> and <u>acidosis</u>)<br><br> | <br><br>* Decrease in the <u>FHR</u> following the maximum contraction curve<br><br>* Onset to <u>nadir</u> is gradual (≥ 30 seconds).<br><br><br> | <br><br>* <u>Intrauterine resuscitation</u><br><br>* If <u>FHR</u> pattern does not improve despite <u>intrauterine resuscitation</u>: emergency <u>cesarean delivery</u> [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vuaAGm)<br><br><br> |
| <br>Variable deceleration  <br> | <br>* <u>Umbilical cord compression</u>/prolapse (see “<u>Umbilical cord complications</u>”)<br><br> | <br><br>* Variable presentation and temporal relation to the changes in contractions<br><br>* Onset to <u>nadir</u> is abrupt (< 30 seconds) and lasts ≥ 15 seconds but < 2 minutes.<br><br><br> | <br><br>* Intermittent variable decelerations (< 50% of contractions): usually no interventions are needed<br><br>* Recurrent variable decelerations (≥ 50% of contractions)<br><br>* <u>Intrauterine resuscitation</u><br><br>* If <u>FHR</u> pattern does not improve despite <u>intrauterine resuscitation</u>: emergency <u>cesarean delivery</u><br><br><br> |
| <br>Prolonged deceleration <br> | <br><br>* Same as those for late and <u>variable deceleration</u>, but protracted and more severe<br><br>* Continued uterine contractions, <u>inferior vena cava</u> syndrome, <u>peridural anesthesia</u>, rapid decrease in the mother's blood pressure<br><br><br> | <br>* A decrease in <u>FHR</u> of ≥ 15 bpm from the baseline, lasting ≥ 2 minutes but < 10 minutes<br><br> | <br><br>* <u>Intrauterine resuscitation</u><br><br>* If <u>FHR</u> pattern does not improve despite <u>intrauterine resuscitation</u>: emergency <u>cesarean delivery</u><br><br><br> |

### Interpretation




* Nonreassuring fetal status

* Characteristic changes in the <u>fetal heart rate</u> (<u>FHR</u>) in response to fetal <u>hypoxia</u> and <u>metabolic acidosis</u>

* Fetal <u>tachycardia</u> (<u>FHR</u> > 160–180/min)

* Fetal <u>bradycardia</u> (<u>FHR</u> < 110/min)

* Loss of baseline variability

* <u>Recurrent variable decelerations</u> and/or <u>late decelerations</u>

* A nonreassuring tracing requires <u>intrauterine resuscitation</u> and/or immediate delivery (cesarean or, if imminent, vaginal delivery).

* Reassuring fetal status

* A <u>fetal heart tracing</u> that shows a good beat to beat variability (> 6 bpm), > 2 <u>accelerations</u> within a 20 minute period, and no evidence of <u>fetal distress</u> (e.g., fetal <u>bradycardia</u>, fetal <u>tachycardia</u>, late or <u>variable decelerations</u>, sinusoidal pattern)

* Indicates fetal well-being.





### Management




See “<u>Intrauterine resuscitation</u>” for details.




* Initial management includes repositioning of the mother, <u>supplemental O<sub>2</sub></u>, fluids, and delayed active pushing in the <u>second stage of labor</u>.

* Consider <u>amnioinfusion</u>, <u>tocolytics</u>, and emergency <u>cesarean delivery</u> if initial measures are unsuccessful.

## Complications of delivery

### Life-threatening complications




* <u>Prolonged second stage of labor</u>

* <u>Obstructed labor</u>, e.g., due to <u>shoulder dystocia</u>

* <u>Umbilical cord complications</u>, including <u>nuchal umbilical cord</u>

* <u>Uterine rupture</u>

* <u>Uterine inversion</u>

* <u>Postpartum hemorrhage</u>

* <u>Amniotic fluid embolism</u>

* See also “<u>Abnormal labor and delivery</u>.”





### Perineal lacerations




* Definition: tear of the perineal area due to significant or rapid stretching forces during <u>labor</u> and delivery

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: most common obstetric injury of the <u>pelvic floor</u>

* <u>Risk factors</u> [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CAaqNM)

* <u>Macrosomia</u>

* <u>Forceps delivery</u>

* No previous delivery

* <u>Prolonged second stage of labor</u>

* <u>Occiput posterior</u> delivery

* Rapid delivery of head in <u>breech presentation</u>

* Head extension before <u>crowning</u>

* Lack of perineal elasticity (e.g., perineal <u>edema</u>)

* Classification ;   [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Yuanpm)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cK1a230)

* First degree: cutaneous to <u>subcutaneous tissue</u> tear (<u>skin</u>, fourchette, <u>posterior</u> vaginal wall) with no involvement of the perineal muscles

* Second degree: first-degree <u>lacerations</u> plus <u>laceration</u> of the perineal muscles without involvement of the anal sphincter

* Third degree: second-degree <u>lacerations</u> plus involvement of the <u>external anal sphincter</u> (may lead to <u>fecal incontinence</u> due to sphincter involvement)

* A: < 50% of the <u>external anal sphincter</u> is torn.

* B: > 50% of the <u>external anal sphincter</u> is torn.

* C: external and internal anal sphincters are torn.

* Fourth degree: third-degree <u>lacerations</u> plus <u>lacerations</u> of the <u>anterior</u> wall of the <u>anal canal</u> or <u>rectum</u>

* Clinical features: symptoms of a missed <u>perineal laceration</u> (occult <u>perineal laceration</u>) may manifest immediately or up to years after delivery ;  [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WK1P230)

* Perineal <u>edema</u>/<u>hematoma</u>, <u>dysuria</u>

* Symptoms of <u>pelvic floor dysfunction</u> (e.g., fecal/flatus incontinence, <u>pelvic organ prolapse</u>)

* Signs of infection (e.g., foul-smelling discharge, <u>fever</u>, persisting <u>pain</u>)

* Diagnosis

* <u>Digital rectal examination</u> findings

* A palpable defect

* Decreased <u>anal sphincter tone</u> and/or asymmetric sphincter contractions

* Endoanal <u>ultrasonography</u>: to evaluate the integrity of the internal and <u>external anal sphincter</u>

* Treatment

* First and second degree: Minor tears (e.g., <u>superficial</u>, hemostatic <u>lacerations</u>) are left to the clinician’s discretion to determine if <u>suturing</u> is required. [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VK1G230)

* Conservative: e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u>, <u>sitz baths</u>

* Suture: <u>local anesthesia</u> and <u>laceration</u> closure using surgical glue or continuous sutures

* Third and fourth degree

* Regional or <u>general anesthesia</u> may be used.

* Reconstructive <u>surgery</u> to repair the anal sphincters and <u>mucosa</u>

* Reconstruction of the <u>distal</u> rectovaginal septum and the <u>perineal body</u>

* Complications [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1K12230)

* Primarily associated with third- and fourth-degree <u>lacerations</u>.

* Complications include:

* <u>Pain</u> and <u>dyspareunia</u>

* Rectovaginal <u>fistulae</u>

* Hemorrhage

* Infection

* <u>Wound dehiscence</u>

* Prevention: application of warm compress to <u>perineum</u> during delivery





> [!TIP]
> Complications of fourth-degree <u>lacerations</u> include rectovaginal <u>fistulae</u>.

### Coccydynia




* Etiology: injury to the <u>coccyx</u> during <u>childbirth</u> as a result of internal and external pressure on the <u>coccyx</u> during <u>labor</u> and delivery

* Clinical features

* <u>Pain</u> and tenderness of the <u>coccyx</u>, esp. when sitting or leaning back

* <u>Pain</u> may suddenly increase when the patient is changing from a sitting to a standing position.

* <u>Pain</u> may also occur during defecation or sexual intercourse.

* <u>Physical examination</u>: palpation of the <u>coccyx</u> elicits <u>pain</u>

* Diagnosis: clinical

* Management

* Protection (e.g., sitting on Donut or wedge cushions)

* <u>Analgesics</u> (e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u>)

* Local heat or cooling according to patient preference

* Exacerbating factors (e.g., sitting on hard surfaces, cycling) should be avoided if possible.

* Prognosis: resolves spontaneously in the majority of patients (> 90%) [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YK1nU30)





### Postpartum retroperitoneal hematoma




* <u>Epidemiology</u>: rare (∼ 1:1000) [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zs1rxR0)

* Etiology: injury to branches of the <u>internal iliac artery</u> (most commonly, <u>uterine artery</u>)

* Most commonly due to:

* <u>Laceration</u> of a <u>uterine artery</u> during hysterotomy or <u>uterine rupture</u>

* Extension of a paravaginal <u>hematoma</u> into the <u>retroperitoneal space</u>

* Other: <u>pelvic</u> or abdominal injury, anticoagulation, rupture of an <u>aneurysm</u> of the abdominopelvic vasculature

* Clinical features

* Signs of <u>hemodynamic instability</u> (e.g., <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>)

* Usually painless (unless caused by <u>pelvic</u> or abdominal injury)

* Diagnostics: imaging (e.g., <u>sonography</u>, CT)

* Management

* Prompt <u>laparotomy</u>

* Alternatively: <u>selective arterial embolization</u>

## Planned home birth

* <u>Statistics</u>

* In the US, approx. 1% of births per year are home births.

* 75% of these home births are planned.

* Indications

* There is insufficient evidence to determine what makes a good candidate for a home <u>birth</u>.

* Home births can be considered in individuals with no contraindications.

* Patients who would like to plan a home <u>birth</u> should be advised about the benefits and risks of home <u>birth</u> compared to hospital delivery in order to make an informed decision.

* Advantages compared to hospital delivery

* Lower risk of maternal interventions (e.g., induction or <u>augmentation of labor</u>, <u>episiotomy</u>, <u>cesarean delivery</u>)

* Lower risk of certain maternal complications (e.g., vaginal or <u>perineal lacerations</u>, peripartum or postpartum infections)

* Lower costs

* Familiar environment for the mother

* Disadvantages compared to hospital delivery

* Higher risk of <u>perinatal</u> <u>death</u> for both the mother and fetus

* Higher risk of neurological complications for the <u>newborn</u>

* Contraindications

* <u>Absolute contraindications</u>

* <u>Fetal malpresentation</u> (e.g., <u>breech presentation</u>)

* <u>Multiple gestation pregnancy</u>

* Previous <u>cesarean delivery</u>

* <u>Relative contraindications</u>

* Any other <u>risk factor</u> for a complicated <u>pregnancy</u> (see “<u>High-risk pregnancies</u>”)

* No means for safe and timely transport to a nearby hospital or accredited <u>birth</u> center

* Lack of access to home-<u>birth</u> provider services (e.g., certified professional midwife, certified nurse midwife, <u>obstetrician</u>, <u>family medicine</u> physician)
