# Nipple discharge

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AL0RZS)

## Summary

<u>Nipple</u> discharge can be physiological or pathological. Physiological discharge is typically bilateral, multiductal, with a milky appearance; causes include lactation and <u>galactorrhea</u>. Pathological discharge is typically unilateral, uniductal, nonmilky, and spontaneous. Although most causes of pathological nonmilky <u>nipple</u> discharge are benign (e.g., <u>intraductal papilloma</u>, <u>mammary duct ectasia</u>), <u>malignancy</u> is an important consideration. The <u>diagnostic approach to nipple discharge</u> is based on clinical evaluation findings, including characteristics of the discharge and patient age. Further evaluation with imaging is required for all patients with <u>red flags in nipple discharge</u>. Treatment depends on the underlying cause.




<u>Nipple</u> discharge in male individuals is not addressed in this article.

---

## Classification

* Physiological nipple discharge [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)

* Bilateral discharge from multiple ducts

* Typically milky; may be green or clear

* Usually provoked (i.e., after <u>nipple</u> stimulation or expression)

* Pathological nipple discharge [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)

* Unilateral discharge that typically originates from a single duct

* Nonmilky; may be clear, bloody, or serosanguineous

* Usually spontaneous (i.e., occurs without <u>nipple</u> stimulation or expression)

## Etiology

### <u>Physiological nipple discharge</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z41rNS0)




* Lactation: discharge related to <u>pregnancy</u> or <u>breastfeeding</u>

* Galactorrhea

* Discharge unrelated to <u>pregnancy</u> or <u>breastfeeding</u>

* Caused by physiological, pharmacological, pathological or <u>idiopathic</u> <u>hyperprolactinemia</u>:  ;  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xTdEG60)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FldgAJ0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hodcbI0)

* Endocrine disorders (e.g., <u>hypothyroidism</u>, <u>prolactinomas</u>)

* Medications (e.g., <u>dopamine antagonists</u>, <u>TCAs</u>)

* Chronic <u>nipple</u> stimulation (e.g., piercings, tight clothing)

* See “<u>Etiology of hyperprolactinemia</u>” for details.





### <u>Pathological nipple discharge</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z41rNS0)




* <u>Benign breast conditions</u>

* <u>Intraductal papilloma</u> (most common) [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3h1Sdg0)

* <u>Mammary duct ectasia</u>

* <u>Fibrocystic breast changes</u>

* <u>Breast abscess</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u> (5–15% of cases): most commonly <u>ductal carcinoma in situ</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3h1Sdg0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Intraductal papilloma</u> is the most common cause of <u>pathological nipple discharge</u> accounting for up to 58% of cases. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3h1Sdg0)

## Clinical evaluation

### Focused history [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)




* Nature of discharge (e.g., color, laterality, spontaneous vs. provoked)

* <u>Obstetric history</u> (e.g., time since last <u>pregnancy</u> and <u>breastfeeding</u>)

* <u>Medication review</u> for drugs that can cause <u>galactorrhea</u> (see “<u>Etiology of hyperprolactinemia</u>”)

* <u>Nipple</u> stimulation (e.g., from tight-fitting clothing or during <u>sexual activity</u>)





### Focused examination [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)




Perform a <u>clinical breast examination</u> to assess for:




* <u>Palpable breast mass</u>

* Visible <u>nipple</u> discharge (spontaneous or after expression)

* Characteristics of <u>nipple</u> discharge (e.g., color, involvement of single or multiple ducts)





### Red flags in nipple discharge [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)




The following are <u>red flag features</u> for <u>malignancy</u>.




* Characteristics of <u>pathological nipple discharge</u>

* Unilateral

* Uniductal

* Persistent

* Spontaneous

* Clear, bloody, or serosanguineous

* <u>Breast mass</u>

* <u>Risk factors for breast cancer</u> (e.g., advanced age, positive <u>family history</u>)

## Diagnosis

### Initial diagnostic approach [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z41rNS0)




Perform a focused clinical evaluation in all patients, including for <u>red flags in nipple discharge</u>.




#### Presence of <u>red flags</u>




* <u>Breast mass</u>: Obtain <u>initial diagnostics for a palpable breast mass</u> and manage accordingly.

* Other <u>red flags in nipple discharge</u>

* Patients ≥ 30 years of age ;  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z41rNS0)

* <u>Ultrasonography</u>

* AND <u>diagnostic mammography</u> or <u>digital breast tomosynthesis</u> (DBT)

* Patients < 30 years of age

* <u>Ultrasonography</u>

* Consider <u>diagnostic mammography</u> or DBT.  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)





#### No <u>red flags</u>




Perform evaluation based on the characteristics of the discharge.




* Bilateral milky discharge

* All patients: <u>pregnancy test</u>

* If <u>pregnancy test</u> is negative, perform <u>diagnostics for hyperprolactinemia</u> (e.g., serum <u>prolactin</u> and <u>TSH</u> levels).

* Nonmilky discharge 

* Patients aged ≥ 40 years  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)

* <u>Ultrasonography</u>

* AND <u>diagnostic mammography</u> or DBT

* Patients aged < 40 years: Advise avoidance of <u>nipple</u> stimulation and reassess in 3 months.





### Additional studies [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z41rNS0)




* Indications

* Diagnostic uncertainty

* Follow-up of abnormal findings on initial imaging

* Preprocedural planning

* Modalities

* <u>Breast</u> <u>MRI</u>

* <u>Ductography</u>

* <u>Breast biopsy</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Biopsy</u> is necessary if imaging findings are concerning for <u>malignancy</u> (e.g., <u>BI-RADS 4</u> or 5). [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)

## Treatment

Treatment is based on the underlying cause; see “<u>Common causes of nipple discharge</u>.” [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)




* <u>Physiological nipple discharge</u>

* Lactation: Advise patients that milky discharge may continue for one year after the end of <u>pregnancy</u> or <u>breastfeeding</u>.

* <u>Galactorrhea</u> 

* Physiological <u>hyperprolactinemia</u>: Advise avoidance of <u>nipple</u> stimulation and reassess in 3 months.

* Pathological or pharmacological <u>hyperprolactinemia</u>: See “<u>Treatment of hyperprolactinemia</u>.”

* <u>Pathological nipple discharge</u>

* Concern for <u>malignancy</u>: Management is based on <u>biopsy</u> results; consult appropriate specialists.

* For all other patients (e.g., with <u>BI-RADS 1–3</u> on imaging), consider either:

* Duct excision

* Clinical and imaging surveillance for 24 months [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x21EPT0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qe1uAf0)
