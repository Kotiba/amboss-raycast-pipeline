## Summary

Endocrinology is the study of the endocrine system (i.e., the <u>hypothalamus</u>, <u>pituitary gland</u>, <u>thyroid gland</u>, <u>adrenals</u>, and <u>gonads</u>), metabolic diseases, and certain aspects of nutritional medicine. The endocrine glands are responsible for producing and secreting <u>hormones</u>, which influence the function of cells in certain tissues of the body. <u>Hormone</u> secretion is controlled by highly regulated pathways, the most important of which is the <u>hypothalamic-pituitary axis</u>. The <u>hypothalamus</u> secretes and stores <u>nontropic hormones</u> (e.g., <u>ADH</u>, <u>oxytocin</u>) and <u>releasing hormones</u> (e.g., <u>TRH</u>, <u>CRH</u>, <u>GnRH</u>). The <u>pituitary gland</u> is composed of the <u>anterior pituitary</u>, which secretes <u>tropic hormones</u> (e.g., <u>ACTH</u>, <u>TSH</u>, <u>FSH</u>, <u>LH</u>) and whose function is controlled by <u>hypothalamic</u> <u>releasing hormones</u>, and the <u>posterior pituitary</u>, which stores <u>ADH</u> and <u>oxytocin</u>. The <u>gonads</u> are the <u>ovaries</u> in female individuals and <u>testicles</u> in male individuals. Their function is controlled via the <u>hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis</u>, as well as the secretion of <u>sex hormone-binding globulin</u> (<u>SHBG</u>). Disruption of the <u>hypothalamic</u>-<u>pituitary</u> axis can result in the development of various endocrine disorders, which are classified according to the level of pathway disruption: primary (disorders of the peripheral endocrine gland), secondary (<u>pituitary</u> dysfunctions), and tertiary (<u>hypothalamic</u> disorders). An understanding of these <u>hormone</u> pathways is important for the diagnosis and management of endocrine disorders, particularly when interpreting changes in <u>hormone</u> levels and the results of suppression and/or stimulation tests.




For more information, see the articles "<u>Thyroid gland and parathyroid glands</u>" and “<u>Adrenal gland</u>.”

## Basics of endocrinology

### Hormones




* Definition

* <u>Hormones</u> are <u>endogenous</u> messengers produced by endocrine glands or single cells that are responsible for <u>signal transduction</u>.

* They influence the function and metabolic rate of other organs and cells in the body.

* Complex regulatory circuits (e.g., the <u>hypothalamic-pituitary axis</u>) control their secretion.

* Types of <u>hormones</u>

| --- | --- | --- |
| Type of <u>hormone</u> | Description | Example |

> [!NOTE]
> <u>Hydrophilic hormones</u> (e.g., <u>catecholamines</u>) are stored in secretory granules and released when needed. <u>Lipophilic hormones</u> (e.g., adrenocortical <u>steroid hormones</u>) pass into the bloodstream once synthesized without being stored in cells.



* Degradation of <u>hormones</u>

* <u>Steroid hormones</u> and <u>thyroid hormones</u>: inactivation and conjugation in the <u>liver</u> and excretion in <u>bile</u>

* <u>Catecholamines</u>: enzymatic degradation and excretion in urine (e.g., <u>vanillylmandelic acid</u>)

* <u>Peptide</u>/protein <u>hormones</u>: proteolytic degradation mainly in the <u>liver</u> and <u>kidneys</u>




### Feedback control mechanisms




<u>Hormone</u> secretion is controlled by the following feedback mechanisms: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bqbHCu)




* Negative feedback  

* <u>Hormone</u> secretion by the endocrine gland suppresses the release of <u>hypothalamic</u> and <u>pituitary</u> <u>hormones</u>.

* Negative feedback loop types include:

* Ultrashort feedback loop: <u>Hypothalamic</u> <u>hormones</u> inhibit their own secretion via <u>autocrine</u> effects.

* Short feedback loop: <u>Pituitary</u> <u>hormones</u> inhibit the release of <u>hypothalamic</u> <u>hormones</u>.

* Long feedback loop: <u>Hormones</u> from peripheral endocrine glands inhibit the release of <u>hypothalamic</u> and <u>pituitary</u> <u>hormones</u>.

* Example: <u>Thyroid hormone</u> signaling decreases the production of <u>thyrotropin-releasing hormone</u> (<u>TRH</u>) and <u>thyroid-stimulating hormone</u> (<u>TSH</u>).

* Positive feedback

* <u>Hormone</u> secretion enhances its own production.

* Example: Uterine stretching during <u>labor</u> contractions under the influence of <u>oxytocin</u> triggers the release of more <u>oxytocin</u> from the <u>posterior pituitary</u>.

### Diagnosis of endocrine diseases




* Direct measurement of <u>hormone</u> blood levels (e.g., measuring <u>prolactin</u> blood level upon suspicion of <u>prolactinoma</u>)

* Stimulation of glands to detect underactivity (e.g., <u>ACTH stimulation test</u> for <u>Addison disease</u>)

* Inhibition of glands to detect hyperactivity (e.g., <u>dexamethasone suppression test</u> for <u>Cushing syndrome</u>)

* Imaging of glands to determine:

* Morphological abnormalities (e.g., <u>thyroid ultrasound</u>)

* Functional abnormalities (e.g., <u>thyroid scintigraphy</u>)

* Specific <u>laboratory studies</u> (e.g., determination of <u>thyrotropin</u> <u>receptor</u> <u>antibodies</u>; <u>HbA<sub>1c</sub></u> for <u>diabetes mellitus</u>)

## Hypothalamus and pituitary gland

### <u>Hypothalamus</u>




* Anatomy

* <u>Ventral</u> part of the <u>diencephalon</u> (see “<u>Diencephalon</u>”)

* Composed of multiple <u>nuclei</u> (e.g., <u>lateral nucleus</u>, <u>preoptic nucleus</u>)

* Function

* Regulation of hormonal secretion by the <u>anterior pituitary gland</u> via the <u>hypothalamic-pituitary axis</u>

* Secretion/storage of <u>ADH</u> and <u>oxytocin</u>

* <u>ADH</u> and <u>oxytocin</u> are produced in the <u>supraoptic nucleus</u> and <u>paraventricular nucleus</u> of the <u>hypothalamus</u>.

* Both <u>hormones</u> are transported to the <u>posterior pituitary</u> via neurophysins (a group of carrier <u>proteins</u>) and released into the circulation as needed.

* Reception and integration of sensory inputs (e.g., from <u>area postrema</u>, <u>OVLT</u>)

* Thirst and hunger regulation

* Autonomic function control

* <u>Thermoregulation</u>

* <u>Hormones</u>

* Inhibiting hormones

* Function: decrease hormonal secretion from the <u>pituitary gland</u>

* Examples: <u>somatostatin</u>, <u>dopamine</u>

* Releasing hormones

* Function: increase hormonal secretion from the <u>pituitary gland</u>

* Examples: <u>thyrotropin-releasing hormone</u> (<u>TRH</u>), <u>corticotropin-releasing hormone</u> (<u>CRH</u>), <u>gonadotropin-releasing hormone</u> (<u>GnRH</u>), <u>growth hormone-releasing hormone</u> (<u>GHRH</u>)

### Pituitary gland (<u>hypophysis</u>)




* Anatomy

* Located in the sella turcica (midline <u>depression</u> of the <u>sphenoid bone</u>) of the <u>middle cranial fossa</u>

* Connected to the <u>hypothalamus</u> via the pituitary stalk (a tube-like structure between the median eminence of the <u>hypothalamus</u> and the <u>posterior pituitary</u> that contains the <u>axons</u> of the <u>posterior pituitary</u> <u>neurons</u>)

* Consists of two major parts:

* Anterior pituitary gland (<u>adenohypophysis</u>): develops from oral <u>ectoderm</u> (<u>Rathke pouch</u>)

* Posterior pituitary gland (<u>neurohypophysis</u>): develops from <u>neural ectoderm</u>

* The three major types of <u>pituitary</u> cells are:

* Acidophil cells

* Secrete <u>prolactin</u> and <u>growth hormone</u> (<u>GH</u>)

* Stain well with acidic dyes such as eosin

* Basophilic cells

* Secrete <u>adrenocorticotropic hormone</u> (<u>ACTH</u>), <u>thyroid-stimulating hormone</u> (<u>TSH</u>), <u>luteinizing hormone</u> (<u>LH</u>), and <u>follicle-stimulating hormone</u> (<u>FSH</u>)

* Stain well with basic dyes (e.g., hematoxylin)

* Chromophobe cells

* Do not secrete <u>hormones</u>  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ES08Yf)

* Stain poorly with both acidic and basic dyes

* Function

* <u>Anterior pituitary gland</u>

* Regulation of endocrine gland function via the release of <u>tropic hormones</u>

* Secretion of <u>nontropic hormones</u> with direct peripheral effects

* <u>Posterior pituitary gland</u>: storage/release of <u>ADH</u> and <u>oxytocin</u> in pituicytes (<u>glial cells</u>)

* Intermediate <u>pituitary gland</u>: secretion of <u>melanocyte-stimulating hormone</u> (<u>MSH</u>)

* <u>Hormones</u>

* Tropic hormones: act on endocrine glands to mediate their effects

* All <u>pituitary</u> glycoprotein <u>hormones</u> (e.g., <u>FSH</u>, <u>LH</u>, <u>TSH</u>) have a common alpha subunit.

* Each <u>hormone</u> has a unique beta subunit.

* Nontropic hormones: act directly on target-tissue cells

## Adrenal cortex

* The <u>adrenal cortex</u> consists of three distinct layers:

* <u>Zona glomerulosa</u>: produces <u>mineralocorticoids</u> (i.e., <u>aldosterone</u>)

* <u>Zona fasciculata</u>: produces <u>glucocorticoids</u> (i.e., <u>cortisol</u>)

* <u>Zona reticularis</u>: produces <u>androgens</u> (i.e., <u>dehydroepiandrosterone</u>)

* Feedback control mechanism

* ↑ <u>Hypothalamic</u> secretion of <u>CRH</u> → ↑ <u>ACTH</u> secretion from the <u>pituitary</u> → ↑ synthesis of <u>glucocorticoids</u> and <u>androgens</u> from the <u>adrenal cortex</u>

* <u>Mineralocorticoids</u> are secreted as a result of <u>renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system</u> activation.

* See “<u>Hormones of the adrenal cortex</u>” in “<u>Adrenal gland</u>.”

## Thyroid gland

* The <u>thyroid gland</u> cells produce the following <u>hormones</u>: [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Fp0grS)

* Follicular cells: the <u>thyroid hormones</u> T<sub>3</sub> (<u>triiodothyronine</u>) and T<sub>4</sub> (<u>tetraiodothyronine</u>)

* <u>Parafollicular cells</u>: <u>calcitonin</u>

* The main function of the <u>thyroid hormones</u> is the regulation of basal metabolism and growth, while <u>calcitonin</u> is involved in <u>calcium</u> and <u>phosphate</u> <u>homeostasis</u>.

* Feedback control mechanism

* ↑ <u>Hypothalamic</u> secretion of <u>TRH</u> → ↑ <u>TSH</u> secretion from the <u>pituitary</u> → ↑ <u>thyroid hormone</u> production

* <u>Calcitonin</u> is not controlled by the <u>hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis</u>. It is released in response to increased serum <u>Ca<sup>2+</sup></u> levels.

* See “<u>Thyroid gland and parathyroid glands</u>.”

## Gonads

### Overview




* Definition

* <u>Gonads</u> are reproductive organs that produce <u>gametes</u> and sex <u>hormones</u>.

* The male <u>gonad</u> is the <u>testicle</u> and the female <u>gonad</u> is the <u>ovary</u>.

* Sex <u>hormones</u>

* Male individuals: <u>testosterone</u>, <u>dihydrotestosterone</u>

* Female individuals: <u>estrogen</u>, <u>progesterone</u>

* Regulation: The secretion of sex <u>hormones</u> is controlled by the <u>pituitary</u> and <u>hypothalamic</u> <u>hormones</u>.

* <u>LH</u>

* <u>FSH</u>

* <u>GnRH</u>

#### Feedback control mechanisms




* <u>Hypothalamus</u>

* Central regulation

* Pulsatile release of <u>GnRH</u> during <u>puberty</u> stimulates secretion of <u>FSH</u>, <u>LH</u>, and sex <u>hormones</u>.

* <u>GnRH</u> secretion may be influenced by competitive sports, <u>malnutrition</u>, or <u>stress</u>, any of which can lead to <u>amenorrhea</u> in women.

* Peripheral regulation via feedback inhibition of <u>GnRH</u>, <u>FSH</u>, and <u>LH</u> secretion by the following <u>hormones</u>:

* <u>Androgen</u>

* <u>Estrogen</u>

* <u>Progesterone</u>

* <u>Inhibin</u>

* Sex hormone-binding globulins (SHBGs): a group of transporter <u>proteins</u> required for the transport of <u>lipophilic</u> sex <u>hormones</u>

* <u>Hyperestrogenism</u> (e.g., <u>pregnancy</u>, <u>OCP</u> use) causes increased <u>SHBG</u> levels.

* Female individuals with low levels of <u>SHBG</u> (e.g., <u>PCOS</u>) have higher levels of free <u>testosterone</u>, which is responsible for the development of <u>hirsutism</u> in these patients.

* Male individuals with high <u>estrogen</u> levels (e.g., in <u>liver cirrhosis</u>) are at risk of developing <u>gynecomastia</u> because of increased <u>SHBG</u> levels and decreased free <u>testosterone</u> levels.

* In addition to <u>SHBG</u> <u>gene</u> <u>polymorphisms</u> there are many factors that affect SHBG levels. [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PodWXI0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l0WvTP0)

* ↓ levels: <u>obesity</u>, uncontrolled <u>type 2 DM</u>, <u>nephrotic syndrome</u>, <u>decompensated cirrhosis</u>, untreated <u>hypothyroidism</u>, untreated <u>acromegaly</u>, use of <u>glucocorticoids</u>, use of <u>exogenous</u> <u>androgens</u>

* ↑ levels: advanced age, <u>HIV infection</u>, <u>compensated cirrhosis</u>, <u>hepatitis</u>, untreated <u>hyperthyroidism</u>, use of some <u>anticonvulsants</u> (e.g., <u>carbamazepine</u>, <u>phenytoin</u>, <u>phenobarbital</u>), use of <u>exogenous</u> <u>estrogens</u>

## Regulation of appetite and satiety

### Overview [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qqbCzu)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IqbY-u)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqbf-u)




* Input

* Gastrointestinal system

* <u>Endocrine hormones</u>: released into the circulation in response to feeding/<u>fasting state</u>

* <u>Vagal</u> afferents: relay signals from intestinal <u>mechanoreceptors</u> to the <u>nucleus tractus solitarius</u>

* <u>Fat tissue</u>: <u>leptin</u> <u>hormone</u> released into the circulation

* Integrating center: <u>arcuate nucleus (hypothalamus)</u>

* Projection: The arcuate <u>nucleus</u> relays signals to other <u>hypothalamic nuclei</u> via orexigenic and anorexigenic <u>neurons</u>.

* Orexigenic <u>neurons</u>

* Stimulate appetite

* Release <u>neuropeptide Y</u> and <u>agouti-related peptide</u>

* Anorexigenic <u>neurons</u>

* Suppress appetite

* Release <u>POMC</u>

* Output

* <u>Lateral nucleus</u> of the <u>hypothalamus</u> (hunger center): appetite stimulation

* <u>Ventromedial nucleus</u> of the <u>hypothalamus</u> (satiety center): appetite suppression

* Clinical relevance

* Polyphagia: excessive hunger (e.g., in <u>hyperthyroidism</u>, <u>Prader-Willi syndrome</u>)

* <u>Anorexia</u>: a loss of appetite (e.g., in cancer, <u>HIV</u>)
