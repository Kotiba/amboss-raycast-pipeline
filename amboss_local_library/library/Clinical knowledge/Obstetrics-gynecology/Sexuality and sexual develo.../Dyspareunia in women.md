# Dyspareunia in women

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Obstetrics/gynecology > Sexuality and sexual development disorders > Dyspareunia in women*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v80Ao3)

---

## Summary

Dyspareunia in women is <u>pain</u> that occurs with vaginal intercourse or penetration. The <u>pain</u> may be <u>superficial</u> (with vaginal entry), deep (on deeper penetration or thrusting), or both. Causes include <u>vulvar dermatoses</u>, genitourinary infections, low <u>estrogen</u> states, and structural abnormalities. Clinical evaluation involves a detailed medical and psychosocial history and <u>pelvic examination</u>. The diagnostic approach depends on clinical findings and can include testing for infections, hormonal testing, and advanced studies such as imaging, <u>cystoscopy</u>, or <u>biopsy</u>. Common diagnoses of exclusion are <u>vulvodynia</u> and <u>genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder</u>. Management is specific to the underlying cause.

## Etiology

### Superficial dyspareunia [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hNdcaq0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)




* <u>Vulvar dermatoses</u>

* <u>Lichen simplex chronicus</u>

* <u>Lichen sclerosus</u>

* <u>Lichen planus</u>

* <u>Contact dermatitis</u>

* <u>Bartholin gland cyst and abscess</u>

* <u>Vulvodynia</u>: vulvar <u>pain</u> lasting ≥ 3 months without a clear underlying cause  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eNdxZq0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NdT0q0)

* Generalized <u>vulvodynia</u>: <u>pain</u> that affects the entire <u>vulva</u>

* Localized <u>vulvodynia</u>: <u>pain</u> that affects a specific area of the <u>vulva</u> (e.g., vestibulodynia, which is <u>pain</u> localized to the <u>vaginal vestibule</u>)

* <u>Genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder</u>, including <u>vaginismus</u>  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jNd_aq0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)

* <u>Neuralgias</u> (e.g., <u>postherpetic neuralgia</u>, <u>pudendal neuralgia</u>)





### Deep dyspareunia [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hNdcaq0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)




* <u>Pelvic</u> infections (e.g., <u>pelvic inflammatory disease</u>)

* <u>Interstitial cystitis</u>

* Structural

* <u>Endometriosis</u>

* <u>Adenomyosis</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WOdPrJ0)

* <u>Pelvic</u> adhesions

* Uterine <u>retroversion</u>

* <u>Uterine fibroids</u>

* <u>Ovarian cysts</u> or masses

* Gastrointestinal conditions (e.g., <u>irritable bowel syndrome</u>, <u>inflammatory bowel disease</u>)

* Musculoskeletal conditions (e.g., <u>arthritis</u>. hip labral tear)





### <u>Superficial</u> and/or <u>deep dyspareunia</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hNdcaq0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)




* <u>Vulvovaginal atrophy</u> and/or inadequate lubrication

* Low <u>estrogen</u> states

* <u>Primary ovarian insufficiency</u>

* Bilateral <u>oophorectomy</u>

* <u>Genitourinary syndrome of menopause</u>

* <u>Pituitary</u> dysfunction

* Postpartum and/or <u>breastfeeding</u>

* Medications (e.g., <u>tamoxifen</u>, <u>GnRH agonists</u>, <u>aromatase inhibitors</u>)

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* Cancer treatment (e.g., <u>chemotherapy</u>, radiation)

* Lack of arousal

* Infectious or inflammatory

* <u>Vulvovaginitis</u> (e.g., <u>bacterial vaginosis</u>, <u>vulvovaginal candidiasis</u>, <u>trichomoniasis</u>, <u>noninfectious vulvovaginitis</u>)

* <u>Urinary tract infections</u>

* <u>Genital herpes</u> simplex

* Structural

* <u>Pelvic floor dysfunction</u> (e.g., hypertonic muscles, <u>pelvic floor</u> muscle spasms, <u>pelvic organ prolapse</u>) [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xMdEIq0)

* <u>Hysterectomy</u>

* <u>Vulvar and vaginal injuries</u> (e.g., <u>perineal laceration</u>, <u>episiotomy</u>, <u>female circumcision</u>)

* <u>Vulvar and vaginal cancer</u>

* <u>Anomalies of the female genital tract</u> (e.g., hymenal variants, <u>transverse vaginal septum</u>) or <u>urethral diverticulum</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Postpartum dyspareunia</u> can be related to multiple factors, including vaginal dryness due to <u>breastfeeding</u>, perineal trauma during delivery, <u>postpartum depression</u>, and/or relationship changes. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iodJbI0)

## Clinical evaluation

Use a nonjudgmental, <u>trauma-informed approach</u> with all patients, and provide <u>affirmative care</u>.




### Focused history [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AldRzJ0)




* <u>Pain</u> characteristics, including:

* Duration

* Location (<u>superficial dyspareunia</u> vs. <u>deep dyspareunia</u>)

* Severity

* Specific provoking <u>sexual activities</u>

* Any relationship to <u>menstruation</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UMdbLq0)

* Associated symptoms

* Vaginal or <u>pelvic</u> symptoms (e.g., <u>vaginal discharge</u>, <u>vaginal bleeding</u>)

* Other symptoms (e.g., urinary, GI, and/or musculoskeletal)

* Changes in hormonal status (e.g., <u>pregnancy</u>, <u>breastfeeding</u>, <u>menopause</u>)

* <u>Risk factors for STIs</u>

* Past medical and surgical history

* Psychosocial stressors (e.g., relationship and other life events); consider:

* <u>Screening for depression</u>

* Screening for <u>generalized anxiety disorder</u>

* <u>Screening for IPV</u> and <u>sexual abuse</u> (current or previous)

* Medications (prescription and over-the-counter)





> [!TIP]
> Assess patients for psychosocial factors (e.g., severe relationship <u>stress</u>, <u>intimate partner violence</u>, <u>mood disorders</u>) that can be <u>risk factors</u> for and/or consequences of dyspareunia. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UMdbLq0)




### Focused examination [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hNdcaq0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UMdbLq0)




Perform an abdominal and <u>pelvic examination</u> in all patients.




* External genital examination

* <u>Vulvar cotton swab test</u>  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eNdxZq0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lmdvfq0)

* <u>Vaginal examination of the pelvic floor musculature</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UMdbLq0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nmd-fq0)

* <u>Bimanual examination</u>

* <u>Speculum examination</u>

* Assess for <u>pelvic organ prolapse</u> and <u>cervical motion tenderness</u>

* <u>Rectovaginal examination</u> (in patients with rectal <u>pain</u> and/or <u>deep dyspareunia</u>)





> [!TIP]
> Patients with dyspareunia may have <u>anxiety</u> about and/or difficulty tolerating a complete <u>pelvic examination</u>. Use a <u>trauma-informed approach</u>, clearly explain the examination steps, and emphasize that the examination can be stopped at any time. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GlcByc0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3NdSaq0)

## Treatment

* Treat the underlying cause.

* A combination of treatment modalities are often necessary and may include: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hNdcaq0)

* Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants

* Topical and/or <u>oral analgesics</u>

* <u>Pelvic floor physical therapy</u>

* Procedural interventions (e.g., injections, <u>surgery</u>)

* Refer to specialists (e.g., <u>gynecologist</u>, urogynecologist, <u>pain management</u>, psychologist) as appropriate.
