## Summary

Safe and fulfilling <u>sexual activity</u> can be an important part of a healthy lifestyle. Safer sex requires education and awareness of the risks involved in <u>sexual activity</u> as well as taking precautions to mitigate these. Risks include infection (e.g., <u>HIV</u>, <u>HPV</u>, <u>gonorrhea</u>, <u>syphilis</u>), which is best prevented with barrier protection (e.g., <u>condoms</u> or <u>dental dams</u>); injury (from unsafe practices such as <u>asphyxiation</u> or from accidents resulting from potentially hazardous practices, e.g., bondage play), which is best prevented through prior education about the practices engaged in; and <u>unintended pregnancy</u>, which is best prevented with oral, barrier, or other forms of <u>contraception</u>. As a category of biological classification, “sex” refers to the binary division of most living beings into “male” and “female” based on their <u>chromosomal</u>, <u>phenotypical</u>, and reproductive traits; the category of “<u>intersex</u>” comprises various types of ambiguous <u>chromosomal</u> and <u>phenotypical sex</u>, generally, due to a <u>disorder of sex development</u> (<u>DSD</u>). In contrast to “sex,” the term “<u>gender</u>” refers to one's identity as male, female, or a variety of <u>nonbinary</u> configurations based on social, cultural, behavioral, and environmental factors rather than biological ones. <u>Sexual orientation</u> is the prevalent pattern of an individual’s sexual and emotional attraction to other individuals. People worldwide continue to experience discrimination, persecution, and marginalization based on their sex, <u>gender</u>, and <u>sexual orientation</u>. The ongoing struggle of women's rights, LGBT+, and other movements has raised awareness of the underlying injustices and advanced changes in culture and legislation promoting sexual/<u>gender</u> equality and empowering communities to openly live and advocate for their lifestyle. This has had a great impact on health care not only in terms of clinically addressing the needs of minority groups but also in terms of providing a safe and nonstigmatizing setting for all individuals to seek and receive reaffirming and medically appropriate health care. Accordingly, sexual medicine involves not only the treatment of <u>gender</u>- and sexuality-related disorders and <u>sexually transmitted infections</u>, but, moreover, includes the provision of counsel and support regarding topics of <u>sexual development</u>, <u>sexual activity</u>, <u>gender identity</u>, and <u>sexual orientation</u>.




Physiological aspects of sexuality, such as <u>sexual development</u> and the <u>sexual response cycle</u> are also discussed in this article. For disorders, see the articles on “<u>Sexual dysfunction and paraphilic disorders</u>” and “<u>Differences (disorders) of sex development</u>”.

## Gender

Traditionally, <u>gender</u> is an individual’s self-identification and self-expression according to the binary categories of male or female as informed by social, cultural, behavioral, and environmental factors associated with sex, the term today furthermore recognizes the relationship between masculinity and femininity as a spectrum that includes a wide range of <u>gender-diverse</u> identities.




* Gender: a social construct referring to the norms (e.g., attitudes, behaviors) associated with an individual's sex

* Gender identity

* An individual’s personal and internal sense of being male, female, any combination of the two, or neither

* By age three, children typically begin to categorize themselves by <u>gender</u>.

* Gender expression

* The external representation of <u>gender</u> through culturally informed attitudes and behaviors, e.g., external appearance, mannerisms, <u>gender</u> roles

* Typically reflects <u>gender identity</u>, though this is not necessarily the case










### Cisgender




“<u>Cisgender</u>” describes a binary <u>gender identity</u> in alignment with <u>sex assigned at birth</u>, i.e., male or female.




* Cismale: a male individual who identifies as definitely male

* Cisfemale: a female individual who identifies as definitely female





### Gender diversity




<u>Gender diversity</u> is the fair and equitable representation and inclusion of individuals of all <u>genders</u> in society and recognizes and addresses the fact that <u>gender</u> is a spectrum that goes beyond the binary framework.




* <u>Gender diverse</u> (<u>gender</u> nonconforming, gender nonbinary, <u>gender</u> variant, genderqueer)  

* Not aligned with the traditional understanding of <u>gender</u> as a binary construct

* While often used as an umbrella term for <u>gender identities</u> that do not align with <u>sex assigned at birth</u> (e.g., <u>transgender</u>), the term “<u>gender diverse</u>” should be understood to encompass the entire spectrum of <u>gender</u>

* Transgender

* The identification with a <u>gender</u> that does not correspond with the <u>sex assigned at birth</u>

* Often used synonymously with “<u>gender diverse</u>” or “<u>gender nonbinary</u>,” but this is imprecise and should be avoided as <u>transgender</u> individuals may identify as <u>nonbinary</u> <u>gender diverse</u> or as binary male or female.

* Transmasculine (<u>transgender man</u>; female-to-male or female-to-<u>nonbinary</u> individual, FTM/FTX): male-leaning or diverse <u>gender identity</u> in an individual assigned the female sex at <u>birth</u>

* Transfeminine: (<u>transgender woman</u>; male-to-female or male-to-<u>nonbinary</u> individual, MTF/MTX): female-leaning or diverse <u>gender identity</u> in an individual assigned the male sex at <u>birth</u>

* Bigender (<u>pangender</u>, <u>androgyne</u>): identity that comprises both male and female <u>gender</u>

* Gender neutral (<u>agender</u>): identity that does not align with the notion of having a <u>gender</u>

* Genderfluid: identity in flux between <u>genders</u>

* Third gender: identity of a <u>gender</u> besides male and female as defined by oneself and/or society (e.g., Hijras in the Indian subcontinent, Khanith in Oman, and two-spirit identities in the United States)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Gender identity</u>, <u>gender expression</u>, sex, and <u>sexual orientation</u> may not align with binary <u>cisgender</u> <u>heterosexuality</u>.




> [!TIP]
> <u>Gender diversity</u> is not a disorder, but it may cause clinically significant <u>distress</u> leading up to, e.g., <u>gender dysphoria</u>.




### <u>Gender transition</u> and affirmation




* Gender transition: the process of recognizing, accepting, embracing, and expressing a <u>gender identity</u> that is not in alignment with the <u>sex assigned at birth</u>

* Gender affirmation: the process of undergoing the social, legal, and/or physical changes necessary to complete <u>gender transition</u>; may involve <u>gender-affirming hormone therapy</u> and/or <u>surgery</u>

* <u>Gender-affirming hormone therapy</u>

* Administration of <u>hormones</u> to induce the development of <u>secondary sexual characteristics</u> matching an individual’s <u>transgender identity</u>

* See “<u>Principles of transgender health care</u>.”

* <u>Gender-affirming surgery</u> (formerly “<u>gender-confirming surgery</u>” or “sexual reassignment <u>surgery</u>”)

* <u>Surgery</u> to modify an individual’s physical appearance to match their <u>transgender identity</u>

* See “<u>Principles of transgender health care</u>.”

## Sexual orientation

The prevalent pattern of an individual’s sexual and emotional attraction to other individuals. Like <u>gender</u>, <u>sexual orientation</u> is regarded today as a spectrum rather than a rigid classification of identities. Accordingly, while <u>gender</u>-conforming <u>heterosexuality</u> represents the dominant pattern of <u>sexual orientation</u> and behavior, an individual’s <u>gender</u>, sex, and <u>sexual orientation</u> as well as the <u>sexual practices</u> in which they engage may be in flux and are not necessarily contingently aligned. .




* Heterosexuality (straight)

* Sexual attraction to individuals of the opposite sex

* May also be defined as attraction to the opposite <u>gender</u> independent of <u>sex assigned at birth</u> (e.g., a <u>transgender man</u> who is attracted to women may identify as <u>heterosexual</u>)

* Homosexuality

* Sexual attraction to individuals of the same sex

* Gay: male <u>homosexuality</u>

* Lesbian: female <u>homosexuality</u>

* May also be defined as attraction to the same <u>gender</u> independent of <u>sex assigned at birth</u> (e.g., a <u>transgender man</u> who is attracted to men may identify as gay)

* Bisexuality

* Sexual attraction to individuals of the same as well as the opposite sex, often in varying and/or fluctuating degrees

* For many, <u>bisexuality</u> also encompasses having sexual attraction to <u>gender diverse</u> individuals

* Bi-curious: interest in and/or openness to <u>homosexual</u> experiences despite currently identifying as neither <u>homosexual</u> nor <u>bisexual</u>

* Pansexuality: sexual attraction to people independent of sex, <u>gender</u>, or <u>sexual orientation</u>

* Polysexuality: attraction to individuals of various but not every sex, <u>gender</u>, or <u>sexual orientation</u>.

* Asexuality

* Absence of sexual attraction to other people

* Does not imply an inability to emotionally or romantically connect with other people

## Sexual activities

### Principles of healthy <u>sexual activity</u>




* Safe and fulfilling <u>sexual activity</u> can be an important part of a healthy lifestyle.

* <u>Sexual activity</u> between two or more individuals is legally contingent on mutual freely given consent and all participants having reached the age of consent, with any individual being having the right to revoke consent and stop the activity at any time.

* While the development of a sex-positive attitude is an important aspect of human health, awareness of potential risks involved in <u>sexual activity</u> (e.g., infection, injury, <u>unintended pregnancy</u>) is no less important.

* It is important for physicians to address issues of sexuality and <u>sexual health</u> openly and without prejudice.

* The glossary below serves to aid clinicians in communicating topics of <u>sexual health</u> openly with patients and can by no means cover the entire spectrum of human <u>sexual activity</u>.





### Sexual intercourse (sex)




Sex comprises any form of physical sexual contact between two or more individuals, frequently but not always to the point of orgasm. The term “sex” is sometimes used more restrictively for penetrative intercourse only.




* Nonpenetrative: sexual intercourse not involving penetration

* Mutual oral and manual nongenital stimulation

* Foreplay: the engagement in acts aimed at mutual sexual stimulation in preparation for further <u>sexual activity</u>; may involve kissing, tactile stimulation, or oral sex

* Penetrative: sexual intercourse involving the penetration of the <u>vagina</u>, <u>anus</u>, or mouth with any part of the body or object

* Anal: sexual intercourse involving the erotic stimulation of another individual’s <u>anus</u>; may or may not be penetrative

* Vaginal: sexual intercourse involving the erotic stimulation of another individual’s <u>vagina</u>; may or may not be penetrative

* Oral: erotic stimulation of another individual using the mouth

* Anilingus (analingus, colloquially referred to as “rimjob”): oral stimulation of another individual’s <u>anus</u>

* Cunnilingus: oral stimulation of another individual’s <u>vagina</u>, <u>vulva</u>, and/or <u>clitoris</u>

* Fellatio (colloquially referred to as “blowjob”): oral stimulation of another individual’s <u>penis</u>

* Manual: erotic stimulation of another individual using the hands

* Handjob: manual stimulation of another individual’s <u>penis</u>

* Fingering: manual stimulation of another individual’s <u>vagina</u>, <u>vulva</u>, and/or <u>clitoris</u>





### Masturbation [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eSYxAo)




<u>Masturbation</u> is the practice of sexual self-stimulation, usually to the point of orgasm.




* Normal activity in individuals of all ages, usually beginning during <u>adolescence</u>

* Only considered excessive if associated with significant <u>distress</u> and impaired social or occupational function e.g., skipping school/work to masturbate

* Excessive and/or compulsive <u>masturbation</u> is not classified as a separate mental disorder in <u>DSM-5</u>. However, depending on manifestations, it can be diagnosed as an other <u>sexual dysfunction</u>, an <u>impulse control disorder</u>, or an <u>obsessive-compulsive disorder</u>.





### Abstinence




The term abstinence describes the conscious and deliberate refraining from <u>masturbation</u> and/or sexual intercourse.




* Virginity: an individual’s state before their first penetrative intercourse

* Celibacy: the state of abstinence from marriage and/or sexual intercourse; in the narrow sense, applied to persons who have taken a religious vow

* Incel (<u>involuntary celibate</u>): an online community based on the shared inability to find a sexual partner despite the desire for sexual partnership





### Varieties of <u>sexual practices</u>




* Polyamory: the practice of intimate relationships with multiple mutually consenting partners

* Monoamory: the practice of an intimate relationship with a single partner

* Men who have sex with men (MSM): practice of a male sex individual regardless of <u>sexual orientation</u> having sexual intercourse with another male sex individual

* Women who have sex with women (WSW): Practice of a female sex individual having sexual intercourse with another female sex individual

* Roleplay: the assuming and <u>acting out</u> of roles in an erotic context

* Cross-dressing (<u>transvestism</u>): the practice of wearing clothes traditionally associated with another sex 

* Individuals may engage in <u>transvestism</u> for a variety of reasons and purposes, not all of which are of a sexual nature.

* <u>Transvestism</u> is often, but not necessarily, associated with <u>LGBT+ culture</u> and should not be confused with <u>homosexuality</u> or <u>transgender identity</u>.

* As a performative art (drag), <u>transvestism</u> typically involves the exaggerated representation of another sex for comedic, often satirical, effect.

* <u>Cross-dressing</u> ; for artistic purposes and in settings where it causes no <u>distress</u> and/or functional impairment to the individual is not a paraphillic condition and should not be diagnosed as <u>transvestic disorder</u>.

* The <u>cross-dressing</u> and <u>transvestic disorder</u> must furthermore be distinguished from the normal practice of <u>transgender</u> individuals dressing according to their <u>gender identity</u>.

* Body modification: the modification of one’s body, e.g., through piercings or elective <u>surgery</u>

* BDSM: an umbrella term for a variety of practices and roleplay involving bondage and discipline (BD), dominance and submission (DS), and/or sadism and masochism (SM)

* <u>Fetishism</u>

* Intense sexual arousal focused on an inanimate object or nonsexual part of the human body e.g., feet

* Often more broadly defined as intense sexual arousal from a specific object, activity, (sexual or nonsexual) part of the body

* <u>Fetishism</u> is only considered a disorder if the practice is associated with persistent fetishistic urges that cause significant <u>distress</u> and/or impairment of function, in which case it is considered a <u>paraphilic disorder</u>.

* Sex work (prostitution)

* Engagement in <u>sexual practices</u> for money

* May but does not necessarily involve sexual contact (includes, e.g., erotic dancing, performing sexual acts via webcam)





### Sex-associated risks




* <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u> (<u>STI</u>)

* <u>Unintended pregnancy</u>

* Trauma and injury

* Potentially risky <u>sexual activities</u>

* Multiple sexual partners → ↑ risk of <u>STI</u>

* Rough sex (e.g., bondage, (auto)sadism/masochism, <u>asphyxiation</u>/choking) → ↑ risk of injury or <u>death</u>

* Sex work (involving sexual intercourse)

* ↑ Risk of <u>STI</u>

* Exposure to maltreatment/violence





### Sex-associated practices




* <u>Contraception</u>

* <u>Oral contraceptives</u>

* <u>Barrier contraceptives</u>

* <u>Condom</u>

* <u>Diaphragm (contraceptive)</u>

* <u>Coitus interruptus</u>

* <u>Basal body temperature method</u>

* Safer sex: the practice of engaging in <u>sexual activities</u> in a way that prevents disease and/or injury

* <u>Condom</u>

* <u>Dental dam</u>





### <u>Sexual violence</u>




* Any sexual act involving the use of physical or mental force by an individual upon another individual.

* Violent <u>sexual practices</u> between consenting individuals do not automatically constitute abuse, <u>rape</u>, or <u>sexual assault</u>.

* See “<u>Sexual violence</u>” for more information.

### Sexuality and aging




The reproductive capacity of women ends at the beginning of <u>menopause</u> (45–55 years), while male reproductive capability is maintained throughout life. <u>Aging</u> has an effect on sexuality and sexual function in both men and women.




#### Female sexuality




* ↓ <u>Sensitivity</u> to <u>nipple</u>, vulvar, and clitoral stimulation

* Physical and psychological changes that affect sexuality (e.g., ↓ <u>estrogen</u> → vaginal dryness and <u>atrophy</u> → <u>dyspareunia</u>)

* ↓ Sexual activity

* For more information, see “<u>Menopause</u>.”





#### Male sexuality




* ↓ Amount and quality of <u>sperm</u>

* ↓ Sexual activity

* ↓ Sexual response

* Functional changes become more common in men > 50 years (see “<u>Erection</u> and <u>ejaculation</u> disorders”).

## Sexual health

### Taking a sexual history




* Routinely incorporate <u>sexual history</u> into primary care.

* <u>Adolescents</u> > 12 years of age should be interviewed alone and <u>confidentiality</u> should be ensured.

* Follow the 5 Ps of sexual health assessment. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OHXIqz)

* Partners: Inquire about the number of sexual partners and <u>sexual orientation</u>.

* Pregnancy: Inquire whether the patient wants to avoid <u>pregnancy</u> or is planning a <u>pregnancy</u>.

* Practices: Inquire about <u>sexual practices</u> (e.g., oral, vaginal, anal)

* Protection against <u>STIs</u>: Inquire about the protective measures against <u>STIs</u> currently used by the patient and their partner(s).

* Past history of <u>STIs</u>: Inquire about history of <u>STIs</u>.

* See also “<u>Communicating with transgender and gender-diverse patients</u>” below.





### Counseling on sexual health




#### Counseling on safer sex practices




* Risks of unprotected <u>sexual activity</u>

* <u>STIs</u> (e.g., <u>HIV infection</u>, <u>chlamydia</u>)

* <u>Unintended pregnancy</u> and abortion

* Which groups have additional <u>risk factors for STIs</u>

* Importance of safe, pleasurable sexual experiences that are free from <u>coercion</u> and violence

* The use of <u>condoms</u> and other <u>STI prevention</u> measures

* Obtaining recommended <u>screening for STIs</u>





#### Counseling on contraceptive options




* Discuss the patient's priorities regarding whether and when to have children.

* If the patient desires education on <u>contraceptive</u> options, discuss their <u>effectiveness</u>, benefits, and potential adverse effects.

* <u>Hormonal contraceptive options</u> (reversible)

* Most effective: <u>contraceptive implants</u> and <u>intrauterine devices</u> with <u>progestin</u>

* Less effective: injectable <u>progestin</u>, <u>oral contraceptives</u>, and <u>contraceptive patches</u>

* <u>Nonhormonal contraceptive options</u>

* Most effective: <u>vasectomy</u> (potentially irreversible), <u>female sterilization</u> (irreversible), and <u>intrauterine devices</u> (reversible)

* Less effective, reversible options: <u>barrier contraception</u>

* Educate patients on <u>emergency contraception</u> options (<u>IUD</u>, <u>oral emergency contraception</u>) and how to access them.

* For more information, see “<u>Contraception</u>.”





#### Other sexual conditions




As necessary, consider counseling on other sexual conditions.




* Urogenital malignancies (includes screening and prevention e.g., <u>HPV vaccination</u>, <u>cervical smear</u>)

* <u>Ovarian cancer</u>

* <u>Endometrial cancer</u>

* <u>Vulvar and vaginal cancer</u>

* <u>Cervical cancer</u>

* <u>Penile cancer</u>

* <u>Testicular cancer</u>

* <u>Prostate cancer</u>

* <u>Infertility</u>

* <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u>

* <u>HIV infection</u>

* <u>Syphilis</u>

* <u>Gonorrhea</u>

* <u>Chlamydia</u>

* <u>HPV</u>

* <u>Differences (disorders) of sexual development</u>

* <u>Anomalies of the female genital tract</u>

* <u>Menstrual cycle</u> and <u>menstrual cycle abnormalities</u>

* <u>Puberty</u>

* <u>Precocious puberty</u>

* <u>Delayed onset of puberty</u>

* Sexual disorders

* <u>Sexual dysfunction</u>

* <u>Paraphilic disorders</u>

## Affirmative care

### Principles [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HHXK7z)




<u>Affirmative care</u> is a model of health care that takes a respectful, validating, and supportive approach to topics of <u>gender identity</u>, sexuality, and sexual relationships. The foundation of <u>affirmative care</u> is awareness for the special needs of and respect for patients with identities beyond the <u>cisgender</u>, <u>heterosexual</u> spectrum. This involves creating an inclusive clinical environment in which patients feel welcome and safe to discuss their health. A respectful and identity-affirming doctor-patient relationship is key to ensuring that all patients, but especially individuals belonging to potentially marginalized minorities who have often experienced discrimination, receive the health care they require.




#### Inclusive environments




* Try to ensure that registration systems and intake forms allow for including information on “<u>gender identity</u>,” “<u>sex assigned at birth</u>,” “preferred name,” “preferred pronouns,” and “preferred form of address” (e.g., Mr., Mrs., Ms, first name only, other)

* Provide separate all-<u>gender</u> bathrooms, if possible.

* Familiarize yourself with the preferred terminology used in the LGBT+ community.

* Provide training for staff to ensure behavior and language that promotes inclusivity.





#### Communication [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HHXK7z)




* Use gender-neutral language (a style of language that avoids attribution of individuals to binary <u>gender</u> categories) until a setting can be established to ask the patient what name and pronoun they prefer.

* Consider that individuals may be <u>nonbinary</u>, <u>bigender</u>, not identify with any <u>gender</u>, or be uncertain of their <u>gender identity</u>.

* Avoid making assumptions regarding <u>gender identity</u> and <u>sexual orientation</u> and refer to the information provided in the intake form when addressing questions of <u>gender</u> and sexuality.

* Only address questions of <u>gender identity</u> and <u>sexual orientation</u> if it is relevant to the patient's visit.

* Offer open communication about <u>gender identity</u>; Expressing empathy and acknowledging an individual's <u>nonbinary</u> or <u>transgender identity</u> establishes trust with positive effects for an individual's self-affirmation and quality of life.

* Be honest about mistakes made in gendered language and show a willingness to learn and improve.

* Provide information and resources about <u>transgender</u> care and facilitate <u>access to health care</u> services (e.g., <u>gender</u>-affirming therapy) if the patient expresses interest.

* Offer counseling and referral to mental health services if patients show signs of <u>gender dysphoria</u>, <u>major depressive disorder</u>, <u>anxiety</u>, social isolation, and <u>suicidal ideation</u>.

* Reiterate that a <u>nonbinary</u> or <u>transgender identity</u> is not a disorder and that medical attention is unnecessary unless the identity causes physical or mental health issues.

* Reassure patients of <u>confidentiality</u>.

* Only discuss a patient's <u>gender identity</u> with team members or colleagues involved in the patient's care if it is relevant, i.e., the information is needed to address the patient respectfully.





#### Reporting




* Summarize information provided by the patient to ensure it has been understood correctly.

* Report the patient's <u>gender identity</u>, chosen name, and pronouns used in order to ensure that this information is accessible to all <u>health care professionals</u> involved in the care of the patient.

* If the chosen name does not match the name on medical records or insurance, these documents may be updated to avoid confusion and clerical errors.





### Special considerations in youth [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SRcymX0)




* <u>Adolescents</u> > 12 years of age should be interviewed alone and <u>confidentiality</u> should be ensured.

* Establish a setting that offers the opportunity to openly communicate about <u>gender identity</u> and sexuality.

* Provide the opportunity for children and <u>adolescents</u> to speak openly about <u>gender</u> and sexuality and do not assume <u>gender</u> binarity.

* Provide information and resources to educate parents, family members, school teachers about <u>transgender</u> and <u>nonbinary</u> identities, transition options, and creating a safe, inclusive environment at home and in one's social surroundings.

* Social transition measures (e.g., changes in clothing, hairstyles, names, and pronoun use) are an option for young individuals to reduce identity-related <u>distress</u>.

* Provide information and resources about medical interventions

* See <u>puberty suppression</u>

* See <u>gender-affirming hormone therapy</u>

* See <u>gender-affirming surgery</u>

* For safety reasons, do not disclose a child’s <u>gender identity</u> to their parents without receiving consent/permission.





### <u>Gender-inclusive language</u> in clinical practice [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HHXK7z)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hRcc5X0)




* Use the patient's preferred name.

* Use the patient's preferred pronoun (e.g., “they,” “ze,” “xe,” “hir,” “per,” “ve,” “he,” “she”) or, if preferences are unknown, the <u>gender</u>-neutral singular “they.”

* Use <u>gender-neutral language</u> and avoid language that implies that <u>gender</u> is binary. For example:

* Avoid “a physician should care for his patients” and rather say “physicians should care for their patients.”

* Avoid gendered terms such as “mankind,” “chairman,” and “policeman” and use “humankind”, “chairperson”, “police officer” instead.

* Avoid phrases such as “he or she,” “male or female,” or “opposite sex” and use open or inclusive terms such as the singular “they,” “<u>gender</u>,” and “another sex”/“other sex.”
