## Summary

Electrocardiography is an important diagnostic tool in cardiology. External electrodes are used to measure the electrical conduction signals of the <u>heart</u> and record them as lines on graph paper (i.e., an electrocardiogram; ECG). The interpretation of the amplitude and duration of these lines allows for the assessment of normal <u>cardiac physiology</u> as well as the detection of <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>, <u>conduction system</u> abnormalities, and/or <u>ischemia</u>. This article provides an overview of the most essential components of the ECG and describes its clinical application and the characteristic ECG findings for common diseases.

## Technique/steps

### Overview  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4_b3ow)




* Definition: An ECG represents a recording of the electrical activity of the <u>heart</u> that is captured via external electrodes and transcribed onto graph paper as ECG leads (for more information on the electrical activity of the <u>heart</u>, see “<u>Conducting system of the heart</u>”).

* Electrodes 

* Physical conductive pads attached to the patient's chest and limbs

* Detect the direction of the <u>depolarization</u> <u>vectors</u>

* Leads : graphical representation of the <u>depolarization</u> <u>vectors</u> of the <u>heart</u>

* Six precordial leads (V<sub>1</sub>–V<sub>6</sub>) capture the electrical activity of the <u>heart</u> in a <u>horizontal plane</u>.

* Six limb leads (I, II, III, aVL, <u>aVF</u>, aVR ) capture the electrical activity of the <u>heart</u> in a vertical plane.

* Input from three of the limb electrodes is combined to form the six <u>limb leads</u>.

* The fourth electrode is neutral.

### ECG paper   [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)




* ECG paper speed

* In the US, the ECG paper speed is generally 25 mm/s.

* Alternatively, a paper speed of 50 mm/s can be used.

* Machine calibration: 1 mV = 1 cm (i.e., 1 mV of electrical activity results in a 1 cm vertical deflection on the grid paper)

* <u>Rhythm strip</u>: a prolonged 10-second recording of a lead (usually lead II)

* ECG grid paper

* Small squares of 1 mm<sup>2</sup>

* Horizontally: 1 mm = 0.04 s (0.02 s for a paper speed of 50 mm/s)

* Vertically: 1 mm = 0.1 mV

* Large squares of 5 mm<sup>2</sup> 

* Horizontally: 5 mm = 5 x 0.04 s = 0.2 s (0.1 s for a paper speed of 50 mm/s)

* Vertically: 5 mm = 5 x 0.1 mV = 0.5 mV





> [!TIP]
> It is easy to misinterpret an ECG if the paper speed and calibration are not taken into account.

### ECG lead reversal or incorrect placement [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




Electrode or lead reversal or incorrect placement can alter ECG findings. The following are common findings associated with specific types of reversal or incorrect placement.




#### <u>Limb lead</u> reversal [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




* Left (L) arm/right (R) arm (most common) 

* Negative <u>P wave</u> and negative <u>QRS complex</u> in lead I

* Positive <u>P wave</u> and positive <u>QRS complex</u> in aVR

* Right or extreme axis deviation

* Normal R-wave progression in <u>precordial leads</u>

* R arm/L leg: all leads inverted (except for aVL)

* R leg/other limb: One lead appears almost flat.

* L arm/L leg: Changes may be subtle and are usually only detected when compared to previous ECGs.

* Lead I switched with lead II

* aVL switched with <u>aVF</u>

* Lead III inverted

* L arm/L leg and R arm/R leg

* Lead I appears flat.

* aVL and aVR appear identical.

* Lead II appears as an inverted lead III.





#### <u>Precordial lead</u> reversal or incorrect placement [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QMWuoN0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jMW_oN0)




* Reversal

* Usually manifests as disruption of normal P-, QRS-, and <u>T-wave</u> progression

* Suspect misconnected cables if a sudden change in wave morphology returns to normal in the next lead.

* Incorrect placement 

* Changes are often subtle and difficult to detect.

* Common misplacements: placement of V<sub>1</sub> and V<sub>2</sub> too superiorly and V<sub>5</sub> and V<sub>6</sub> too medially [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QMWuoN0)

* May appear as false reversed or <u>poor R-wave progression</u>, which may be mistaken for an <u>anterior MI</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jMW_oN0)





#### Troubleshooting [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




* Compare with a prior ECG if possible.

* Verify correct <u>ECG lead placement</u> at the bedside and repeat the ECG if in doubt.





### ECG artifact [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/G5WBlN0)




* Definition: ECG distortions not related to cardiac electrical activity

* Physiological artifact

* Often caused by motion

* Repetitive narrow spikes (may have a similar appearance to <u>dysrhythmias</u>): caused by small amplitude movements (e.g., <u>tremors</u>, shivering)

* Wandering baseline: caused by large amplitude movements (e.g., patient movement, inadequate electrode contact)

* Nonphysiological artifact

* Often caused by electrical interference

* Appears as an indistinct or thick baseline; may make rhythm analysis difficult

* Artifact reduction methods [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)

* Avoid placing electrodes over bony prominences and major muscles.

* Shave thick <u>hair</u> and clean and dry the <u>skin</u> prior to placement.

* Connect the ECG to a grounded outlet and turn off nearby electronics to minimize nonphysiological artifact.





> [!TIP]
> Suspect artifact if ECG findings do not correlate with the clinical picture, e.g., apparent <u>ventricular tachycardia</u> in an asymptomatic, hemodynamically stable patient. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)

## Interpretation/findings

### ECG components [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4_b3ow)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)




#### Overview




* Wave: a deflection of the ECG line due to any change in the electrical activity of the <u>heart</u> (e.g., <u>P wave</u>, <u>T wave</u>)

* Positive (upward) deflection: the electrical impulse is moving toward the electrode

* Negative (downward) deflection: the electrical impulse is moving away from the electrode

* Equiphasic (equally upward and downward) deflection: the electrical impulse is moving perpendicular to the electrode

* Some waves form complexes (e.g., <u>QRS complex</u>).

* Segment: the line between two different waves, excluding the waves (e.g., <u>ST segment</u>)

* Interval: includes a segment and one (or more) waves (e.g., <u>PR interval</u>)





#### Key components   [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HMbKJ8)




* <u>P wave</u>: atrial <u>depolarization</u> originating in the <u>sinoatrial node</u> (<u>SA node</u>)

* <u>PR interval</u>: <u>depolarization</u> originating in the <u>SA node</u> and traveling through the <u>atria</u>, the <u>AV node</u>, and the <u>His-Purkinje system</u>

* <u>QRS complex</u>: ventricular <u>depolarization</u>

* <u>ST segment</u>: the duration between ventricular <u>depolarization</u> and <u>repolarization</u>

* <u>T wave</u>: ventricular <u>repolarization</u>

* <u>QT interval</u>: total time of ventricular <u>depolarization</u> and <u>repolarization</u>

* <u>U wave</u>: occurs after the <u>T wave</u>; exact origin unknown [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5aXiO9)

### Approach to ECG interpretation   [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4_b3ow)




* When interpreting an ECG, it is important to keep in mind the patient's clinical picture and, if possible, compare the current ECG with previous ones.

* A thorough ECG interpretation algorithm should assess:
1. * <u>Heart</u> rhythm (best seen in lead II)
2. * <u>Heart rate</u> (any lead)
3. * <u>Cardiac axis</u> (leads I and <u>aVF</u>)
4. * <u>P-wave</u> morphology and size (best seen in lead II)
5. * PR-interval duration (best seen in lead II)
6. * QRS-complex morphology and duration (assessed in all leads individually)
7. * <u>ST-segment</u> morphology (assessed in all leads individually)
8. * <u>T-wave</u> morphology (assessed in all leads individually)
9. * <u>QT-interval</u> duration (lead aVL)
10. * <u>U-wave</u> morphology (leads V<sub>2</sub>–V<sub>4</sub>)

## Determination of heart rate and rhythm

### Determination of the heart rhythm [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)




* The <u>heart</u> rhythm is assessed by evaluating the frequency and regularity of the <u>P waves</u> and the <u>QRS complexes</u>, as well as the relationship between the two.

* A 10-second <u>rhythm strip</u> is required to assess the <u>heart</u> rhythm.

* Any abnormalities of the <u>heart</u> rhythm should prompt further evaluation (see “<u>Cardiac arrhythmias</u>”).





#### Sinus rhythm




* Definition: a physiological <u>heart</u> rhythm and age-appropriate <u>heart rate</u> set by the <u>SA node</u>

* <u>Sinus rhythm</u> features 

* <u>P wave</u>

* Positive in leads I, II, and <u>aVF</u>

* Negative in lead aVR

* Followed by a <u>QRS complex</u>

* Regular <u>PR interval</u>

* <u>QRS complex</u>: preceded by a <u>P wave</u>

* Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: a variation of the <u>heart rate</u> during respiration, which is normal and common in young adults  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZxbZED)

### Determination of the heart rate [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)




* The ventricular rate  can be calculated by using the frequency of the <u>QRS complexes</u>, which correlate with ventricular systoles.

* The atrial rate, which correlates with atrial <u>systole</u>, can be calculated by using the frequency of the <u>P waves</u> (e.g., when assessing <u>supraventricular arrhythmias</u>).

* In clinical settings, the <u>heart rate</u> can be measured with an ECG ruler.





#### <u>Heart rate</u> (HR) estimation methods




* Regular QRS rhythm

* HR = 300/number of large (5 mm<sup>2</sup>) boxes between two successive <u>QRS complexes</u> (e.g., if you count 5 large boxes between one R wave and the next, the HR is approx. 300 ÷ 5 = 60/min)

* HR = 150/<u>RR interval</u> in cm (e.g., if there are 2 cm in between two consecutive R waves, HR = 150/2 = 75/min)

* HR = 60/<u>RR interval</u> in seconds (e.g., if there is a 0.5 s interval between two successive R waves, HR = 60/0.5 = 120/min)

* Irregular QRS rhythm: HR = 6 x total number of <u>QRS complexes</u> on a standard 10-second ECG <u>rhythm strip</u> (e.g., if you count 10 <u>QRS complexes</u> on a standard 10-second ECG <u>rhythm strip</u>, the HR is approx. 6 x 10 = 60/min)

## Determination of the cardiac axis

### Definition [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)




* The <u>electrical axis of the heart</u> represents the mean direction of ventricular <u>depolarization</u> in a <u>frontal plane</u>.

* The normal <u>cardiac axis</u> in an adult is between -30° and +90°.





### Methods for determining the <u>cardiac axis</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EfY8Lo)




There are several methods to determine the <u>cardiac axis</u> using the <u>QRS complex</u> polarity. The axis is calculated according to the <u>hexaxial reference system</u> (Cabrera circle).




* Isoelectric (equiphasic) <u>QRS complex</u> method 
1. * Determine the lead in which the <u>QRS complexes</u> are isoelectric (equally positive and negative).
2. * Assess the two leads perpendicular to this lead on the <u>Cabrera circle</u>.
3. * The <u>cardiac axis</u> corresponds to the perpendicular lead with positive <u>QRS complexes</u>.

* Leads I and <u>aVF</u> method  
1. * Determine the <u>QRS complex</u> polarity in leads I and <u>aVF</u>.

* Positive <u>QRS complex</u>: the area above the isoelectric line and under the curve is larger than the area under the isoelectric line above the curve

* Negative <u>QRS complex</u>: the area under the isoelectric line and above the curve is larger than the area above the isoelectric line and under the curve
2. * The <u>cardiac axis</u> can be approximated by evaluating the combinations of the <u>QRS complex</u> polarities in leads I and <u>aVF</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J5XsOA)

* Positive in both leads I and <u>aVF</u>: normal axis (0° to 90°)

* Positive in lead I and negative in <u>aVF</u>: <u>left axis deviation</u> (-90° to -30°) or normal axis (-30° to 0°)

* Negative in lead I and positive in <u>aVF</u>: <u>right axis deviation</u> (90° to 180°)

* Negative in both leads I and <u>aVF</u>: extreme <u>right axis deviation</u> (-180° to -90°)
3. * Lead II can be used for a more accurate <u>determination of the cardiac axis</u> if the <u>QRS complex</u> is positive in lead I and negative in <u>aVF</u>.

* Negative <u>QRS complex</u> in lead II: <u>left axis deviation</u>

* Positive or isoelectric <u>QRS complex</u> in lead II: normal axis

## P wave

* Physiology [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HMbKJ8)

* The <u>P wave</u> represents atrial <u>depolarization</u>, which originates in the <u>SA node</u>.

* The <u>P wave</u> has a lower amplitude and a more curved shape compared to <u>QRS complex</u> waves because of the slower <u>depolarization</u> magnitude  and a slight delay in left <u>atria</u> (LA) <u>depolarization</u> compared to the <u>right atria</u> (<u>RA</u>)

* Morphology [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HMbKJ8)

* Present in all leads

* Duration: < 0.12 s  (in all leads) [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k_bmow)

* Amplitude: < 0.25 mV  (in all leads) [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vWbAMs)

* Polarity 

* Positive in leads I, II, and <u>aVF</u>

* Negative in lead aVR

* Biphasic in lead V<sub>1</sub>: negative deflection < 1 mm  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k_bmow)

## PR interval

* Physiology [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)

* <u>PR interval</u>

* Measured from the start of the <u>P wave</u> to the start of the <u>QRS complex</u>, which may be a <u>Q wave</u> or an R wave

* Includes the <u>P wave</u> and the <u>PR segment</u>

* Represents the <u>depolarization</u> originating in the <u>SA node</u> and traveling through the <u>atria</u>, the <u>AV node</u>, and the <u>His-Purkinje system</u>

* Mainly assessed in lead II  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DeX1ax)

* PR segment

* Reflects the transmission of the electrical impulse through the <u>AV node</u>

* Considered the reference isoelectric line for the remainder of the ECG components

* Morphology

* <u>PR interval</u> duration: 0.12–0.20 s  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4_b3ow)

* Amplitude and polarity: <u>P wave</u> followed by an isoelectric line (see “<u>P wave</u>”)

* Precedes each <u>QRS complex</u>

## QRS complex

### Overview




#### Physiology [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)




* The <u>QRS complex</u> represents ventricular <u>depolarization</u> and corresponds to ventricular <u>systole</u>.

* Because of a faster <u>depolarization</u> magnitude of the ventricles  , the waves of the <u>QRS complex</u> have a sharp appearance.





#### <u>QRS complex</u> components  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)




* Q wave

* A negative deflection preceding an R wave

* Represents <u>depolarization</u> of the interventricular septum (left-to-right direction) via the <u>bundle of His</u>

* <u>Q waves</u> are a normal finding in leads I, III, aVL, V<sub>5</sub>, and V<sub>6</sub> (see “<u>Pathological Q waves</u>” below).

* R wave

* Any positive deflection in the <u>QRS complex</u>

* Represents <u>depolarization</u> of the <u>left ventricular</u> <u>myocardium</u> (right-to-left direction)

* S wave

* A negative deflection after an R wave

* <u>Depolarization</u> of the upper <u>lateral</u> ventricular wall via <u>Purkinje fibers</u> (left-to-right direction)

* Intrinsicoid deflection [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OmXIfA)

* The interval between the onset of the <u>Q wave</u> and the peak of the R wave

* Delayed <u>intrinsicoid deflection</u> (> 0.05 s) is associated with an increased risk of <u>heart failure</u>. [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kmXmfA)





![QRS terminology](../../../../media/vcvv88gtsl9dwueekq9xpmxc.jpg)

QRS terminology




#### Morphology [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)




* Duration

* <u>QRS complex</u>: < 0.1 s 

* Narrow QRS complex

* Typically a <u>QRS complex</u> duration of < 120 ms (or fewer than three small squares on the ECG)

* Indicates normal ventricular <u>depolarization</u>

* Physiological (i.e., <u>sinus rhythm</u>), but also seen in any supraventricular <u>ectopic</u> or arrhythmogenic focus/foci

* Wide QRS complex

* Typically a <u>QRS complex</u> duration of > 120 ms (or more than three small squares on the ECG)

* Indicates prolonged ventricular <u>depolarization</u>

* May be seen in <u>ventricular rhythms</u> (e.g., <u>ventricular tachycardia</u> or <u>premature ventricular beats</u>) or in supraventricular rhythms with <u>aberrant conduction</u> (e.g., <u>complete bundle branch block</u>)

* <u>Q wave</u>: < 0.04 s

* R wave > S wave

* Amplitude

* <u>Q wave</u>: < 0.2 mV

* R wave: progressively increases from lead V<sub>1</sub> to V<sub>5</sub>

* S wave: progressively decreases from lead V<sub>1</sub> to V<sub>5</sub>





> [!NOTE]
> “From V<sub>1</sub> to V<sub>5</sub>, there's sunSet and sunRise”: From leads V<sub>1</sub> to V<sub>5</sub>, S wave Sets while R wave Rises.

### Brugada syndrome [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R0Xlf9)




* Definition: a rare <u>autosomal dominant</u> genetic mutation that leads to abnormal cardiac conduction and sudden <u>death</u>

* Etiology: The most common identified mutation affects cardiac <u>voltage-gated sodium channels</u>.

* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* Most common in Asian men

* Symptoms mostly manifest in adulthood.

* Clinical features

* Often an incidental finding, as most patients are asymptomatic

* <u>Syncope</u>

* <u>Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia</u>

* <u>Ventricular fibrillation</u>

* Diagnostics  

* ECG findings

* Brugada pattern: <u>pseudo-RBBB</u> with <u>ST elevation</u> in leads V<sub>1</sub>–V<sub>3</sub>

* <u>J waves</u> in leads V<sub>1</sub>–V<sub>3</sub> [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALXRZ_)

* Rule out underlying <u>heart</u> disease (e.g., <u>cardiac stress test</u> and <u>echocardiography</u>).

* Treatment

* General measures 

* Avoid certain medications (e.g., certain <u>antiarrhythmics</u>, psychotropics, <u>anesthetic agents</u>). [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h0Xcf9)

* Avoid excessive <u>alcohol</u> intake, <u>cocaine</u>, and large meals.

* Treat <u>fever</u> with <u>antipyretics</u>.

* <u>Implantable cardiac defibrillator</u> (<u>ICD</u>)

* Screen all first-degree relatives annually with <u>clinical examination</u> and ECG.

* See also “<u>Brugada syndrome</u>” in “<u>Management of tachycardia</u>” for stabilization of <u>wide complex tachycardia</u> due to <u>Brugada syndrome</u>.

* Complications

* <u>Sudden cardiac death</u>

* Increased risk of <u>atrial fibrillation</u> [[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d4XoRy)

## T wave

### Overview [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)




* Physiology: : The <u>T wave</u> represents ventricular <u>repolarization</u>.

* Morphology

* Shape: asymmetrical, with the downward slope steeper than the initial upward slope

* Amplitude: < 10 mm (between 1/8 and 2/3 of the R wave)

* Polarity: physiologically <u>concordant</u> to the <u>QRS complex</u> (positive if the <u>QRS complex</u> is positive or negative if the <u>QRS complex</u> is negative)

## QT interval

### Overview [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/P2XW3x)




* Represents the entire duration of ventricular <u>depolarization</u> and <u>repolarization</u>

* Measured from the beginning of the <u>QRS complex</u> to the end of the <u>T wave</u>

* Measured in the lead with the longest <u>QT interval</u>





#### <u>Corrected QT interval</u> (QTc) [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/P2XW3x)




* General

* Correction for the <u>QT interval</u> is necessary, as it varies with <u>heart rate</u>.

* <u>QTc</u> = <u>QT interval</u>/√RR interval (seconds)

* Duration

* Adult men: <u>QTc</u> = 390–450 ms

* Adult women: <u>QTc</u> = 390–460 ms





> [!NOTE]
> Generally, the <u>QT interval</u> should not be more than half of the <u>RR interval</u>.

## U wave

* General [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)

* Small deflection after the <u>T wave</u>

* Polarity is the same as the <u>T wave</u>

* Best seen in leads V<sub>2</sub> to V<sub>4</sub>, but not always visible

* Normal finding in athletes

* Etiology

* Exact origin is unknown

* Thought to be due to delayed <u>repolarization</u> of the midmyocardial cells (<u>myocardium</u>) and the <u>His-Purkinje system</u>

* Causes of prominent <u>U waves</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCY9qr)

* <u>Hypokalemia</u>

* <u>Hypercalcemia</u>

* <u>Bradycardia</u>

## Clinical applications of ECG

### Ambulatory ECG monitoring [[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PfXWmx)




* Description: ECG devices can be used in the outpatient setting to monitor and record the cardiac rhythm over a prolonged period of time.

* Types

* Continuous: Holter monitor [[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MobMcu)

* A continuous, ambulatory, battery-operated ECG recorder worn for 24–48 hours

* Common metrics

* Average, minimum, and <u>maximum heart rate</u>

* <u>Heart</u>-rate variability

* Episodes and duration of <u>arrhythmias</u>

* QRS late potentials

* <u>ST-segment</u> changes

* Analysis of the P and <u>T waves</u>

* Limitations

* The short duration of monitoring results in a low diagnostic yield.

* Devices are not waterproof.

* The patient needs to document symptoms separately.

* Intermittent 

* Event recorder

* A device used in the evaluation of <u>arrhythmias</u> or <u>syncope</u> to record the patient's <u>heart</u> rhythm during symptomatic episodes

* Devices are triggered to record data either by the patient (when experiencing symptoms) or automatically (when an <u>arrhythmia</u> is detected)

* Loop recorder

* A type of <u>event recorder</u> that can be triggered either automatically or manually by the patient

* Records the patient's <u>heart</u> rhythm up to an hour prior to the arrhythmic event as well as during the event

* External recorders: worn externally for short periods of time (4–6 weeks)

* <u>Implantable loop recorders</u>: can be used for up to 36 months (e.g., for patients with more infrequent episodes)

* Pacemakers or implanted <u>cardioverter defibrillators</u>

* Patient-led monitoring (e.g., via a smartwatch)

* Indications

* Daily or near-daily symptoms of:

* <u>Syncope</u>

* <u>Palpitations</u>

* <u>Chest pain</u>

* Assessment of the effects of new rate-control medication (e.g., <u>metoprolol</u>) or pacemaker function

* Screening for ventricular ectopy in high-risk patients (e.g., patients with <u>cardiomyopathy</u> or <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>)





### Other clinical applications of ECG




* <u>Diagnosis of ACS</u> (see also “<u>ECG changes in STEMI</u>”)

* <u>Cardiac arrhythmias</u>

* <u>Cardiac tamponade</u>

* <u>Pericarditis</u>

* <u>Long QT syndrome</u>

* <u>Cardiac stress test</u>

* <u>Sick sinus syndrome</u>

* <u>Pericardial effusion</u>

* <u>Preoperative assessment</u>
