# Procedural sedation and analgesia

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Treatment approaches and procedures > Procedural sedation and analgesia*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w70hLh)

## Summary

Procedural sedation and <u>analgesia</u> (<u>PSA</u>) can be used to reduce <u>pain</u> and awareness during painful or distressing procedures performed outside of the operating room. The level of awareness is typically adjusted according to patient <u>risk factors</u> and the type of procedure. In contrast to patients under <u>general anesthesia</u>, patients undergoing <u>PSA</u> typically maintain <u>airway</u> reflexes and spontaneous respiration. While the risk to patients undergoing <u>PSA</u> is low, both <u>oversedation</u> and adverse effects from the medication are possible. Therefore, <u>PSA</u> should only be performed by trained providers and in patients who have been screened for conditions that are high risk for complications. The choice of sedatives and <u>analgesics</u> should be adapted to the procedure and the patient's physiological parameters, and patients should be monitored closely both during and after the procedure. Patients may be discharged after <u>PSA</u> once they return to their baseline level of functioning.




Recommendations in this article are consistent with the 2018 American Society of Anesthesiologists (<u>ASA</u>) guidelines, the 2019 American College of Emergency Physicians (<u>ACEP</u>) guideline, and the 2014 <u>ACEP</u> clinical policy statement relating to <u>PSA</u>. ## Recovery and discharge

Recovery and discharge criteria may vary between institutions and local protocols should be consulted. In general, patients should be monitored in a suitable environment until they are alert, with stable <u>vital signs</u> that are within their normal parameters. The duration of monitoring also depends on the drugs used during sedation and the patient's health status.




### Monitoring during recovery [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p9XLoZ0)




* Location: conducted in an area with access to resuscitation equipment where constant observation is possible

* Monitoring parameters

* Continuous saturation monitoring until the patients is alert and has reached their <u>target SpO<sub>2</sub></u>

* Intermittent (every 5–15 minutes) monitoring of ventilation and blood pressure until their cardiorespiratory status and <u>level of consciousness</u> are at or near baseline

* Additional consideration: If reversal agents were administered, sufficient time must have passed since the last administration before observation ends.





> [!TIP]
> Serious complications from <u>PSA</u> most commonly occur at the end of the procedure after the painful stimulus has been removed (e.g., reduction of a <u>dislocated</u> <u>joint</u>).




### Requirements for discharge [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p9XLoZ0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* Patient requirements

* <u>Vital signs</u> should be stable and the <u>airway</u> patent.

* Patients should be at their baseline level of:

* Consciousness

* Mobility

* Communication

* Discharge of outpatients

* Address postprocedural <u>pain</u> and <u>nausea</u> before discharge.

* Ensure that the patient is accompanied by a responsible adult.

* Provide the patient with discharge instructions, including those for the presenting complaint.

* Ask the patient to return to the hospital if they experience confusion or respiratory symptoms.

* Instruct the patient to avoid driving for 24 hours after discharge.





### Inadequate emergence after anesthesia




#### Delayed emergence [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cXWaCP0)




* Definition: inability to regain full consciousness 30–60 minutes after <u>general anesthesia</u>

* Etiology

* Residual effect of one or more perioperative agents (e.g., <u>opioids</u>, <u>benzodiazepines</u>, <u>propofol</u>, <u>muscle relaxants</u>), due to, e.g.:

* Dosing errors

* <u>Drug interactions</u>  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lXWvyP0)

* <u>Hypothermia</u>

* Underlying medical conditions (e.g., <u>liver</u> or <u>kidney</u> disease)

* Metabolic alterations (e.g., <u>hypoglycemia</u>, <u>electrolyte</u> imbalances)

* <u>Hypoventilation</u> (e.g., due to inappropriate ventilation technique)

* Neurological complications (e.g., intraoperative cerebral <u>hypoxia</u>, <u>stroke</u>)

* Clinical features

* Sedation

* Slowed and/or impaired reaction to stimuli

* Impairment of protective <u>airway</u> reflexes

* <u>Bradypnea</u>

* <u>Hypoxia</u>

* Management [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NXW-yP0)

* Immediate assessment, including:

* <u>Primary survey</u>, blood <u>glucose</u> measurement

* <u>Treatment of hypothermia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, and <u>hypoxia</u> if present

* If <u>euglycemia</u> with stable <u>vital signs</u>: <u>secondary assessment</u> (serum <u>electrolytes</u>, <u>arterial blood gas analysis</u>, <u>physical examination</u>, <u>neuromuscular blockade testing</u>)

* If suspicion for a specific causative agent is high, administer appropriate medication.

* <u>Opioid overdose</u>: administer <u>naloxone</u>.

* <u>Benzodiazepine overdose</u>: administer <u>flumazenil</u>.

* Residual neuromuscular blockade: administer <u>sugammadex</u>, consider <u>airway</u> protection.

* Central <u>anticholinergic syndrome</u>: administer <u>physostigmine</u>.

* If symptoms persist, evaluate for potential causes of <u>delayed emergence</u> including <u>drug interactions</u>, conditions predisposing for slow metabolism (e.g., <u>liver</u> or <u>kidney</u> dysfunction), and neurological causes (e.g., hemorrhagic or <u>ischemic stroke</u>)

* Prevention

* Intraoperative <u>neuromuscular monitoring</u>

* Avoidance of <u>hypothermia</u>





> [!TIP]
> Although <u>hypothermia</u>, metabolic alterations, and underlying medical conditions can interfere with drug metabolism, they can also be the primary cause of <u>delayed emergence</u>.




#### Emergence agitation [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nXW7AP0)




* Definition: a typically brief, <u>self-limited</u> state of psychomotor hyperactivity during emergence from <u>general anesthesia</u> or procedural sedation before becoming fully conscious

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: most common in children [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cXWaCP0)

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* Use of <u>volatile anesthetic</u> agents

* Inadequate <u>pain control</u>

* Male sex

* Smoking

* Mental health disorders (e.g., <u>posttraumatic stress disorder</u>)

* Associated with specific types of <u>surgery</u>

* Clinical features

* <u>Agitation</u>

* <u>Disorientation</u>

* Purposeless thrashing movements

* <u>Psychosis</u>

* Combative behavior resulting in injury to self or <u>health care providers</u>

* Management

* Exclude alternative <u>causes of altered mental status</u>.

* For severe <u>agitation</u>: administer <u>dexmedetomidine</u>, <u>benzodiazepines</u>, and/or <u>propofol</u>.

* See also “<u>Approach to the agitated or violent patient</u>.”

* Prevention

* Identification of at-risk patients

* Intraoperative administration of <u>dexmedetomidine</u>

* Optimized <u>pain management</u> during emergence
