## Summary

Critically ill patients are typically cared for in <u>intensive care units</u> (<u>ICUs</u>), which are designated hospital units dedicated to managing patients who require a higher <u>level of care</u> than that available on a general medical ward or <u>step down unit</u>, staffed by highly trained clinicians. After initial resuscitation and stabilization, management of <u>ICU</u> patients typically involves ongoing <u>hemodynamic monitoring</u> (often via invasive devices such as an <u>arterial line</u>) and respiratory monitoring (including <u>interpretation of ventilator alarms</u> for mechanically ventilated patients), <u>supportive care of the critically ill patient</u> (including nutrition and metabolic support, <u>analgesia</u>, and sedation), and the prevention of common complications (e.g., <u>VTEs</u>, <u>pressure ulcers</u>, <u>nosocomial infections</u>, <u>delirium</u>). Predictive scoring systems (e.g., the <u>SOFA score</u>, <u>APACHE II score</u>) may be useful for the prognostication of <u>ICU</u> patients. Given the critical nature of <u>ICU</u> care, <u>teamwork</u> and communication, and <u>end-of-life care</u> are essential. Typical reasons for admission to an <u>ICU</u> include the need for organ support (e.g., for patients with <u>brain injury</u>, <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>, <u>COPD exacerbation</u>, <u>sepsis</u>, <u>GI bleeding</u>) and/or close, continuous monitoring (e.g., postoperatively or while receiving a high-risk medication).
