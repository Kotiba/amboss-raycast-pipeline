# Cannabis-induced disorders

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Psychiatric disorders > Cannabis-induced disorders*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MF0Mi3)

## Summary

<u>Cannabis</u>-induced disorders include intoxication, withdrawal, <u>cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome</u>, and other <u>cannabis</u>-related psychiatric and medical conditions. Causative agents include various preparations made from the <u>cannabis</u> plant, cannabinoid extracts, and <u>synthetic cannabinoids</u> that are consumed by smoking, vapor inhalation, and/or oral ingestion. Clinical features of <u>cannabis intoxication</u> include positive neuropsychiatric effects such as euphoria and relaxation, negative neuropsychiatric effects such as <u>anxiety</u>, <u>perceptual disturbances</u>, and cognitive dysfunction, and physiologic effects including increased appetite and changes in blood pressure. Symptoms of <u>cannabis withdrawal</u> manifest shortly following cessation of prolonged heavy use and include irritability, <u>anxiety</u>, and <u>depression</u>. Treatment of <u>cannabis intoxication</u> and withdrawal is mainly supportive. <u>Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome</u> can occur with heavy daily use, manifests with cyclic episodes of abdominal, <u>pain</u>, <u>nausea</u>, and <u>vomiting</u>, and resolves with abstinence from <u>cannabis</u>. Acute episodes are treated with <u>antiemetics</u>, <u>IV fluids</u>, and other supportive measures.




See “<u>Cannabis use disorder</u>” for information on the related <u>substance use disorder</u>.

## Overview

### Substances [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EgW8Ck0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)




There are a variety of preparations that may be consumed by smoking, vapor inhalation, and/or oral ingestion.




* Whole-plant cannabis (street names: <u>marijuana</u>, <u>weed</u>, grass, <u>pot</u>, ganja): dried leaves and/or buds of the <u>cannabis</u> plant

* Hashish (street names: hash, dab, wax, budder): the resin of the cannabis plant, either in solid or oil form

* Cannabinoid-based products: commercially manufactured products containing <u>tetrahydrocannabinol</u> (<u>THC</u>) and/or <u>cannabidiol</u> (<u>CBD</u>)

* Medical <u>cannabis</u>: whole-plant <u>cannabis</u> (“medical <u>marijuana</u>”) or <u>cannabinoids</u> (e.g., <u>dronabinol</u>) used for medical indications (e.g., cancer <u>pain</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, poor appetite, <u>glaucoma</u>)

* Synthetic cannabinoids: synthesized <u>cannabinoid receptor</u>-binding substances that mimic the effect of <u>THC</u>  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)





> [!NOTE]
> DroNABINOl is an example of a medical canNABINOid.




### Mechanisms of action [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cSWaAk0)




Despite extensive research, the mechanisms of action of <u>cannabinoids</u> are still not fully understood.




* Tetrahydrocannabinol (<u>THC</u>): main psychoactive component

* Interacts with cannabinoid receptors <u>CB1</u> and <u>CB2</u> → inhibition of <u>adenylate cyclase</u>

* Induces euphoria and is associated with dose-dependent adverse effects (e.g., <u>anxiety</u>, <u>agitation</u>, <u>psychosis</u>, cognitive dysfunction)

* Cannabidiol (<u>CBD</u>): main nonpsychoactive component

* Does not interact with <u>CB1</u> and <u>CB2</u> <u>receptors</u>

* Reduces the psychoactivity and enhances the tolerability of <u>THC</u>

## Cannabis intoxication

### Clinical features [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EgW8Ck0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JkWsKl0)




The clinical features of <u>cannabis</u> use vary depending on the type of substance and preparation, <u>CBD</u>-to-<u>THC</u> <u>ratio</u>, <u>THC</u> dose, route of ingestion, and user comorbidities. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cSWaAk0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jfW_Nk0)




#### Neuropsychiatric effects of cannabis




* Positive neuropsychiatric effects

* Euphoric mood, joviality

* Calming and relaxation

* Increased awareness of the senses

* Negative neuropsychiatric effects

* Decreased attention, <u>disorganized thought</u>

* Distorted sense of time

* Impairments in concentration, reaction time, coordination, judgment, and/or <u>memory</u>

* <u>Depression</u>, <u>anxiety</u>; , <u>agitation</u>, panic

* <u>Perceptual disturbances</u>;  and other <u>psychotic symptoms</u>

* Social detachment





> [!TIP]
> <u>Cannabis</u> preparations with a high <u>CBD</u>-to-<u>THC</u> <u>ratio</u> are less likely to induce adverse psychiatric effects than those with a low <u>CBD</u>-to-<u>THC</u> <u>ratio</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cSWaAk0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jfW_Nk0)




#### Physiologic effects of <u>cannabis</u>




* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>tachypnea</u>, <u>tremor</u>, <u>arrhythmia</u>

* Increased or decreased blood pressure and/or <u>orthostatic hypotension</u>

* <u>Conjunctival injection</u>, <u>mydriasis</u>, <u>nystagmus</u>

* Increased appetite

* <u>Urinary retention</u>





> [!TIP]
> The effects of oral ingestion of <u>THC</u> may be delayed and unpredictable. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Massive oral ingestion of <u>cannabis</u> products and use of <u>synthetic cannabinoids</u> have been associated with severe <u>agitation</u>, <u>CNS depression</u>, and <u>seizures</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




### Diagnosis




#### Approach




* Rule out <u>life-threatening causes of altered mental status</u>.

* If relevant, evaluate for and initiate <u>management of acute psychosis</u>.

* Use DSM criteria to establish the diagnosis.

* Consider <u>urine drug screening</u> if history of <u>cannabis</u> exposure is in doubt.





#### <u>DSM-V</u> diagnostic criteria [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)




Both criteria must be fulfilled during or shortly after <u>cannabis</u> consumption and not be attributable to another cause.



1. * Significant <u>neuropsychiatric effects of cannabis</u>: behavioral and/or psychological changes such as euphoria, <u>anxiety</u>, or social withdrawal
2. * At least two of the following within two hours of <u>cannabis</u> consumption:

* <u>Tachycardia</u>

* <u>Conjunctival injection</u> (red eyes)

* Increased appetite

* Dry mouth




#### <u>Urine drug screening</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Rarely helpful, as results may be falsely positive for acute intoxication  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* May be considered for the evaluation of suspected inadvertent <u>cannabis</u> exposure or <u>altered mental status</u> of unknown cause

* Does not detect most <u>synthetic cannabinoids</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Exposure to <u>efavirenz</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>, <u>pantoprazole</u>, <u>promethazine</u>, or hemp can cause <u>false positive</u> results on <u>urine drug screening</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)




### Treatment [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EgW8Ck0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Treatment of <u>cannabis intoxication</u> is primarily supportive. Ensure <u>patient safety</u>, provide reassurance, and enable patients to rest in a quiet environment.




* Severe <u>CNS</u> intoxication

* Follow the <u>ABCDE approach in poisoning</u>.

* <u>Manage acute seizures</u>, if necessary.

* Significant <u>agitation</u>: See also “<u>Management of the agitated or violent patient</u>.”

* <u>Identify potential for violence</u> and use <u>deescalation techniques</u>.

* Consider <u>benzodiazepines for agitation</u> or <u>antipsychotics for agitation</u>.

* <u>Manage acute panic attacks</u>, if necessary.

* <u>Treat acute psychosis</u>, if necessary.





> [!WARNING]
> Use of <u>activated charcoal</u> following oral ingestion of <u>cannabis</u> products is not recommended, as the risks are considered to outweigh potential benefits. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)




### Disposition [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Disposition is determined on an individual basis, depending on symptoms and severity:




* Severe <u>CNS</u> intoxication: Admit to the <u>ICU</u>.

* Acute <u>psychosis</u>

* Consider admission for further care and <u>psychiatry</u> consult (if available)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EgW8Ck0)

* See “Disposition” in “<u>Management of acute psychosis</u>.”

* Children with significant symptoms: Consider admission for 24 hours of observation.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* All other patients: Observe in the emergency department until signs of intoxication resolve.





> [!TIP]
> Consider using the <u>SBIRT approach</u> to guide further counseling and treatment.

## Cannabis withdrawal

<u>Cannabis withdrawal</u> develops within hours to days of cessation of regular use and may persist for weeks. Manifestations and symptom severity are highly variable. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EgW8Ck0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vgWACk0)




### <u>DSM-V</u> diagnostic criteria [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)




Both criteria must be fulfilled and not be attributable to another cause.



1. * ≥ 3 of the following features occurring within ∼ 1 week following cessation of prolonged heavy <u>cannabis</u> use:  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)

* Irritability, aggression, or anger

* <u>Anxiety</u>

* <u>Depression</u>

* <u>Anorexia</u> or weight loss

* Restlessness

* <u>Sleep</u> disturbances

* Significant discomfort from at least one of the following: <u>headaches</u>, <u>tremors</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>, <u>fever</u>, chills, sweating
2. * Clinically significant <u>distress</u> or functional impairment resulting from the above features.




> [!WARNING]
> Rule out potentially life-threatening withdrawal syndromes (e.g., <u>alcohol withdrawal</u>, <u>benzodiazepine withdrawal</u>), as symptoms of <u>cannabis withdrawal</u> are not specific. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MfWM5k0)




### Management [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MfWM5k0)




* Provide outpatient psychosocial support and <u>counseling on substance use disorders</u>.

* Symptomatic management as needed (e.g., <u>management of acute panic attacks</u>, <u>benzodiazepines for agitation</u>)

* Consider referring patients with polysubstance use and/or significant comorbidities to a supervised or inpatient <u>withdrawal management setting</u>.

## Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome

### Background [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KfWUMk0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pSWL0O0)




* Definition: a functional <u>nausea and vomiting</u> syndrome associated with long-term <u>cannabis</u> use

* Pathophysiology: not entirely understood  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KfWUMk0)

* <u>Risk factors</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KfWUMk0)

* Male sex

* Age < 50 years

* Long-term daily <u>cannabis</u> use





### Clinical features [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KfWUMk0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KSWU0O0)




* Episodes of abdominal <u>pain</u>, <u>nausea</u>, and <u>vomiting</u>   [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KSWU0O0)

* Cyclic occurrence: acute onset of episodes lasting ≤ 1 week, with asymptomatic periods between

* Symptoms are relieved by hot showers or baths.

* Occurrence resolves with prolonged sustained cessation of <u>cannabis</u> use.





### Diagnosis [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pSWL0O0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KSWU0O0)




* <u>Clinical diagnosis</u> based on typical clinical features in chronic heavy <u>cannabis</u> users

* Consider using the <u>Rome IV diagnostic criteria for functional nausea and vomiting disorders</u>.

* Evaluate for alternative diagnoses if any <u>red flags for abdominal pain</u> or <u>red flags for nausea and vomiting</u> are present.

* See also “<u>Diagnostic workup of nausea and vomiting</u>.”





### Management [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KSWU0O0)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6SWj0O0)




#### <u>Supportive care</u> for acute episodes




Acute management focuses on symptomatic relief and treatment of <u>complications of intractable vomiting</u>.




* Administer <u>antiemetics</u>; see “<u>Overview of antiemetics</u>” for agents and dosages.

* Consider <u>capsaicin</u> cream 0.075% applied to the abdomen or back.  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KfWUMk0)

* Hot showers or baths may offer further symptom relief.

* Provide <u>IV fluid resuscitation</u>, <u>electrolyte repletion</u>, and/or <u>management of acute kidney injury</u> as indicated.

* See also “<u>Management of nausea and vomiting</u>.”





#### Long-term treatment




<u>Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome</u> resolves with complete cessation of <u>cannabis</u> use.




* Educate the patient about the etiology of their symptoms to encourage cessation.

* Consider referral to counseling for <u>cannabis use disorder</u> and abstinence support.

* Consider referring patients with polysubstance use and/or significant comorbidities to a supervised or inpatient <u>withdrawal management setting</u>.
