# Opioid overdose

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Psychiatric disorders > Opioid overdose*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uG0pa3)

---

## Summary

<u>Opioid</u> overdose results from the toxic effects of <u>exogenous</u> <u>opioids</u>. Deaths related to <u>opioid</u> overdose have been steadily increasing in the United States over the past two decades because of a sharp increase in the prescription of <u>opioids</u> for <u>chronic pain</u> and increasing amounts of illegally manufactured <u>fentanyl</u>. Common clinical features of <u>opioid</u> overdose include respiratory <u>depression</u>, <u>CNS depression</u>, and <u>miosis</u>. <u>Treatment of suspected opioid overdose</u> requires <u>airway management</u> and prompt assessment of the need for <u>naloxone</u> to counter <u>opioid-induced respiratory depression</u>, which can be fatal. Inpatient admission is indicated for patients with ongoing respiratory <u>depression</u>, overdose from <u>long-acting opioids</u>, or medical complications from an <u>opioid</u> overdose. All patients with a noniatrogenic <u>opioid</u> overdose should undergo an <u>assessment for substance use disorder</u> (<u>SUD</u>) and be discharged with take-home intranasal <u>naloxone</u>.




For children, see also "<u>Respiratory distress and failure in children</u>."

## Epidemiology

* <u>Opioid</u> overdose is the most common cause of drug overdose <u>death</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-q1DaR0)

* From 2015 to 2022, annual <u>opioid</u> overdose deaths nearly tripled.  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-q1DaR0)

* Deaths typically involve high-<u>potency</u> synthetic <u>opioids</u> (e.g., <u>fentanyl</u>).



Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

---

## Etiology

<u>Iatrogenic</u> overdose occurs when a prescribed dose exceeds an individual's tolerance for <u>opioids</u>. Noniatrogenic overdose (i.e., in the setting of <u>unhealthy drug use</u>) may be intentional or unintentional (e.g., use of a higher dose than intended).




### Risk factors for opioid overdose [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0I1eYR0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)




* <u>Opioid</u>-specific factors

* High dose

* High <u>potency</u> (e.g., <u>fentanyl</u>)

* Long <u>half-life</u> (e.g., <u>methadone</u>)

* Patients with <u>risk factors for opioid-related harm</u>

* Concurrent use of <u>sedative-hypnotics</u>

* Mental health conditions (e.g., <u>depression</u>, <u>SUD</u>)

* Prescriptions from multiple providers and/or pharmacies [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)

* Release from incarceration in the last few weeks [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZI1ZYR0)

* Recent cessation of <u>medications for opioid use disorder</u> (<u>MOUD</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Opioid</u>-induced <u>CNS depression</u> is intensified when combined with other <u>sedative-hypnotics</u> (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, <u>benzodiazepines</u>).

## Clinical features

### Opioid toxidrome [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




The classic triad consists of:




* <u>Altered mental status</u> (e.g., <u>CNS depression</u>, euphoria)

* Bilateral <u>miosis</u> (pinpoint <u>pupils</u>)  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)

* <u>Opioid-induced respiratory depression</u>





> [!WARNING]
> The absence of <u>miosis</u> does not rule out <u>opioid</u> intoxication.




### Opioid-induced respiratory depression (<u>OIRD</u>)  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/11W2f40)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/W1WPf40)




* ↓ <u>Respiratory rate</u> and/or <u>apnea</u>

* ↓ <u>Tidal volume</u>

* Disordered control of breathing

* <u>Signs of respiratory distress</u>

* Signs of <u>hypercapnic respiratory failure</u>, e.g., ↑ <u>EtCO<sub>2</sub></u>

* Can progress to <u>respiratory arrest</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>OIRD</u> is the most common <u>cause of death</u> from <u>opioid</u> overdose and is treated with <u>naloxone for opioid overdose</u>.




### Other clinical features [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Respiratory: <u>Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema</u>

* Neurological

* <u>Myoclonic</u> jerks; , <u>seizures</u>  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l01vT20)

* Diminished or absent <u>gag reflex</u>

* Gastrointestinal

* <u>Constipation</u> and ↓ <u>bowel sounds</u>

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* Cardiovascular

* <u>Bradycardia</u>

* <u>Hypotension</u>

* Other

* <u>Hypothermia</u>

* <u>Pruritus</u>, flushing

## Management

### Acute management [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)




See “<u>Approach to the poisoned patient</u>” for a stepwise approach to patients with known or suspected <u>poisoning</u>.




* Follow an <u>ABCDE approach</u> (see “<u>ABCDE approach in poisoning</u>” for details).

* Start <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> monitoring and <u>establish IV access</u>.

* Initiate <u>oxygen therapy</u> and <u>airway management</u> as needed.

* Administer <u>naloxone for opioid overdose</u> in patients with <u>opioid-induced respiratory depression</u>.

* Consider diagnostic tests to support the diagnosis, identify complications, and evaluate for comorbidities (see “Diagnostics”).

* Determine if the overdose was intentional, e.g., a <u>suicide attempt</u>.

* Assess for comorbid conditions.





> [!TIP]
> If possible, perform <u>basic airway maneuvers</u> prior to administering <u>naloxone</u> to reduce the risk of <u>pulmonary edema</u> or <u>acute lung injury</u> after the reversal of <u>apnea</u>. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)




> [!WARNING]
> Avoid <u>naloxone</u> in intoxicated patients without <u>OIRD</u>, i.e., with spontaneous <u>respiratory rate</u> > 12 breaths/minute. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4I131R0)




### Naloxone for opioid overdose [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4I131R0)




* Goal: restore <u>respiratory drive</u> while avoiding <u>precipitated withdrawal</u>

* Indication: <u>opioid-induced respiratory depression</u> (<u>OIRD</u>) [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4I131R0)

* There is no validated definition for the severity of <u>opioid-induced respiratory depression</u>.

* Use clinical judgment in patients with <u>stupor</u> and a spontaneous <u>respiratory rate</u> ≤ 12 breaths/minute.

* Pharmacology

* Mechanism of action: competitive <u>μ-opioid receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u> neutralizing <u>opioid</u> <u>agonist</u> effects

* Onset (IV): < 2 minutes

* Duration: 20–90 minutes (shorter than most <u>opioids</u>)

* Dosage: There is no consensus on the optimal regimen for in-hospital settings; follow local protocols when available. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Mr1M3R0)

* Choose the lowest possible starting dose to avoid <u>precipitated withdrawal</u> then titrate as needed to reverse <u>OIRD</u>.

* Consider empiric dosage adjustment for:

* The type and amount of <u>opioids</u> taken

* Presence of <u>opioid dependence</u>

* Patient weight

* The risk of <u>respiratory arrest</u>

* There is no direct <u>correlation</u> between <u>OIRD</u> severity and the <u>naloxone</u> dose required to reverse it. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Naloxone</u> has a dose-dependent duration of action that is shorter than most <u>opioids</u>. It does not shorten the duration of <u>opioid</u> toxicity. Repeat dosing and monitoring are often required. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)




#### Starting dose [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Mr1M3R0)




The following dosages are suggested based on expert opinion and <u>FDA</u> guidance.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HnXKuA)




* Preferred route: intravenous

* <u>Imminent respiratory arrest</u>: Administer high-dose IV <u>naloxone</u> DOSAGE and begin emergency <u>airway management</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cYcaLa0)

* All other patients with <u>OIRD</u>: Begin with low-dose IV <u>naloxone</u> DOSAGE (<u>off label</u>).  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Mr1M3R0)

* No <u>IV access</u>

* IM <u>naloxone</u> DOSAGE

* Intranasal <u>naloxone</u> DOSAGE





> [!TIP]
> Lower doses are typically sufficient for <u>opioid</u>-dependent patients. Higher doses are appropriate for <u>opioid</u>-naive patients and are typically required for any patients with synthetic <u>opioid</u> (e.g., <u>fentanyl</u>) overdose. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Mr1M3R0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cYcaLa0)




> [!WARNING]
> In <u>cardiac arrest</u>, do not delay <u>ACLS</u> in order to administer <u>naloxone</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HnXKuA)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cYcaLa0)




#### Subsequent dosage




Titrate further dosing of <u>naloxone</u> to clinical response: e.g., <u>respiratory rate</u>, <u>tidal volume</u>, <u>EtCO<sub>2</sub></u>, and other <u>signs of respiratory distress</u> or <u>respiratory failure</u>.




* No improvement

* Repeat the dose every 2–3 minutes as needed.

* Consider increasing the dose on each repeat administration if there is no response.

* Repeat doses can range widely; follow local protocols and tailor to the individual clinical response.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)

* After ≥ 10 mg of <u>naloxone</u>, reconsider the diagnosis and evaluate for other causes of respiratory <u>depression</u> e.g., <u>xylazine intoxication</u>.

* Initial improvement with recurrent <u>OIRD</u>: Consider continuous <u>naloxone</u> infusion.

* Start infusion at ⅔ the <u>naloxone</u> dose that initially reversed <u>OIRD</u>.  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Titrate the infusion based on clinical response.

* <u>Precipitated opioid withdrawal</u>: Do not administer additional <u>naloxone</u>.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Naloxone</u> is typically unnecessary in intubated and mechanically ventilated patients. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4I131R0)




### Mental health disorder management [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)




* Patients with a confirmed <u>suicide attempt</u> or multiple <u>risk factors for suicidal behavior</u>: Consult <u>psychiatry</u> and consider <u>involuntary hospitalization</u>.

* Stabilized patients with suspected or confirmed <u>opioid use disorder</u>

* Consult addiction medicine and perform <u>SUD assessment</u>.

* Offer <u>medication-assisted treatment</u>.





### Disposition [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)




* Observation period: 4–6 hours after the last <u>naloxone</u> dose [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eI1xbR0)

* Admit patients with:

* Ongoing respiratory <u>depression</u>, e.g., mechanically ventilated patients requiring critical care admission.

* Toxicity from long-acting or extended-release <u>opioids</u> (e.g., <u>methadone</u>, <u>fentanyl</u> patch)

* Complications requiring inpatient management (e.g., <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>, <u>suicidal ideation</u>)

* Discharge criteria

* All patients: alert with <u>normal vital signs</u>

* Known or suspected intentional overdose: after safety assessment (e.g., by <u>psychiatry</u>)

* Consider prescribing or providing home <u>naloxone</u> kits.





> [!TIP]
> Monitor patients for 4–6 hours after administering <u>naloxone</u> for a resumption of <u>opioid</u> effects. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UI1bXR0)
