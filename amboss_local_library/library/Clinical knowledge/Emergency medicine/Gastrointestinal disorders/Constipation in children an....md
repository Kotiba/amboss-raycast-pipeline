# Constipation in children and adolescents

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Gastrointestinal disorders > Constipation in children and adolescents*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qI0Cdh)

## Summary

<u>Constipation</u> is characterized by the infrequent and sometimes painful passage of hard stools. Pediatric <u>constipation</u> is common, with a worldwide <u>prevalence</u> of approximately 10%. <u>Functional constipation</u> accounts for the majority of cases in children and <u>adolescents</u>. <u>Secondary constipation</u>, which is the result of an underlying pathological condition (e.g., <u>Hirschsprung disease</u>, spinal cord abnormalities, metabolic disorder) accounts for fewer than 5% of pediatric <u>constipation</u>. Diagnostics are not routinely recommended to confirm <u>functional constipation</u>; a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> can be established if the <u>Rome IV diagnostic criteria for pediatric functional constipation</u> are met. Diagnostic studies should be performed if <u>secondary constipation</u> is suspected (e.g., <u>constipation</u> in <u>infants</u> aged < 6 months, presence of <u>red flags in pediatric constipation</u>) or if symptoms persist despite treatment. <u>Functional constipation</u> in <u>infants</u> aged 1–5 months typically resolves with <u>sorbitol</u> juice supplementation (e.g., prune juice). Management in children and <u>infants</u> aged ≥ 6 months involves clearing <u>fecal impaction</u> and initiating <u>maintenance therapy</u> with behavior modification and oral <u>laxatives</u>. Oral <u>polyethylene glycol</u> is the preferred <u>laxative</u> for <u>fecal disimpaction</u> and <u>maintenance therapy</u> in <u>infants</u>, children, and <u>adolescents</u>. <u>Secondary constipation</u> is managed by addressing the underlying pathological cause.

## Etiology

* Etiology of primary or functional constipation in children (most common; 95%) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)

* Typically due to stool-withholding behaviors, which may occur during:

* New school attendance (avoidance of school toilets)   [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)

* Toilet training (pressure to control bowels)

* <u>Breastfeeding</u> <u>weaning</u>

* <u>Sexual abuse</u> and/or trauma

* Other causes include:

* Lifestyle: poor diet, insufficient physical activity (in older children and <u>adolescents</u>) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RrYlSq)

* Psychological and behavioral disorders

* <u>Eating disorders</u> (especially in <u>adolescents</u>)  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5E0iw3)

* Genetic disposition

* Alterations in <u>normal gut flora</u>, <u>colonic</u> dysmotility [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jY1_K20)

* Etiology of secondary constipation in children (∼ 5%): causes include [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)

* <u>Cow's milk protein allergy</u>

* <u>Hirschsprung disease</u>

* Congenital <u>anorectal malformation</u>

* <u>Cystic fibrosis</u>

* <u>Spina bifida</u>

* <u>Celiac disease</u>

* <u>Hypothyroidism</u>

* Neuromuscular disorders

## Clinical evaluation

A comprehensive history, <u>physical examination</u>, and assessment of <u>red flag features</u> should be conducted in all patients. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)




> [!TIP]
> Assess for <u>risk factors</u> for <u>functional constipation</u> (e.g., recent family stressors, moving home, starting school, change in diet) and features suggestive of <u>secondary constipation</u>.




### Clinical features [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)




* Infrequent bowel movements

* Painful bowel movements, which may lead to stool withholding behaviors

* Associated features include:

* Abdominal distention, <u>pain</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>anorexia</u>

* <u>Hematochezia</u> (due to <u>anal fissures</u>)

* <u>Urinary tract infection</u> (due to urinary stasis)

* Evidence of <u>fecal impaction</u>  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2IYTXq)

* <u>Fecal incontinence</u> (<u>overflow soiling</u>)

* Palpable mass in the left lower abdominal quadrant

* Dilated <u>rectum</u> containing a large amount of hard stool on rectal examination

* In <u>secondary constipation</u>: features of the underlying disorder





### Red flags in pediatric constipation [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)




<u>Red flag features</u> should prompt referral to a specialist (e.g., pediatric gastroenterologist) for further evaluation. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




* Delayed passage of <u>meconium</u> > 48 hours

* Symptom onset prior to 1 month of age

* <u>Constitutional symptoms</u>, <u>fever</u>

* <u>Failure to thrive</u>

* <u>Bilious</u> <u>vomiting</u>, severe abdominal distention

* Stool changes: bloody stools in the absence of <u>anal fissures</u> or tears , change in stool caliber

* Features suggestive of <u>Hirschsprung disease</u>

* <u>Family history</u> of <u>Hirschsprung disease</u> or other GI conditions

* Features suggestive of <u>spina bifida</u>

* Anal abnormalities

* Fecal occult blood





### <u>Physical examination</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)




* Review growth parameters.

* <u>Abdominal examination</u>

* Lumbosacral examination and <u>neurological examination</u> of lower extremities

* <u>Thyroid examination</u>

* Examination of <u>perineum</u> and <u>anus</u>

* <u>Digital rectal examination</u>;  for the following indications (not routinely recommended) : [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)

* <u>Infants</u> < 6 months of age  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)

* <u>Red flags in pediatric constipation</u>

* Uncertain diagnosis of <u>functional constipation</u>  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)

* Persistent <u>constipation</u> despite 3 months of adequate treatment [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)





> [!TIP]
> Significant expulsion of stool upon removal of the finger following <u>digital rectal examination</u> suggests <u>Hirschsprung disease</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)




> [!WARNING]
> Assess for signs of possible <u>sexual abuse</u> (e.g., anal scars, <u>hematomas</u>, <u>bruising</u>; an intense fear of rectal examination).

## Treatment

<u>Management of pediatric constipation</u> involves clearing <u>fecal impaction</u> and initiating <u>maintenance therapy</u>.




### <u>Fecal disimpaction</u> therapy [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




* Preferred: oral <u>laxative therapy</u> with <u>polyethylene glycol</u> DOSAGE  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)

* Second-line options

* Children: Consider once-daily enemas (e.g., saline enema DOSAGE, mineral oil enema DOSAGE) for 3–6 days. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)

* <u>Infants</u>: Consider glycerin suppositories. DOSAGE [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)

* Unsuccessful disimpaction at home: Consider hospitalization to administer <u>polyethylene glycol</u> via a <u>nasogastric tube</u>.

* Initiate <u>maintenance therapy</u> once disimpaction has been achieved.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Manual disimpaction</u> is not recommended in <u>infants</u> and children because of the risk of <u>colon</u> perforation. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




### <u>Maintenance therapy</u> for <u>functional constipation</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A6YRnJ)




#### <u>Infants</u> aged 1–5 months [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y6YdnJ)




* Exclusively breastfed <u>infants</u> with no <u>red flag features</u>: may only require reassurance

* Dietary intervention : Consider 2 oz of apple, pear, or prune juice.

* Oral <u>laxative therapy</u>

* Consider if <u>constipation</u> persists despite dietary interventions.

* Preferred agent: <u>polyethylene glycol</u> DOSAGE
[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Functional constipation</u> in <u>infants</u> aged 1–5 months typically resolves with <u>sorbitol</u> juice supplementation (e.g., prune juice). [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




#### <u>Infants</u> aged ≥ 6 months, children, and <u>adolescents</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y6YdnJ)




* Educate caregivers on

* The causes of <u>functional constipation</u>

* How to recognize and prevent stool withholding behaviors

* Consider referral to a child psychologist.

* Age-appropriate fiber, fluid, physical activity, and scheduled toileting

* Encourage minimum daily fluid intake.  (See the consensus statement on “Healthy Beverage Consumption in Early Childhood” in “Tips and Links” for details.) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cg1qxT0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xT1EGT0)

* Minimum daily fiber requirements (g/day) = (age in years + 5)  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)

* Healthy bowel habits

* Children who are toilet-trained: Recommend scheduled toileting.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sb1tE20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)

* <u>Adolescents</u>: Reinforce the importance of responding to urges to defecate.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)

* <u>Maintenance pharmacotherapy</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7JW4vm0)

* First-line: <u>polyethylene glycol</u> DOSAGE [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)

* Second-line: <u>lactulose</u> DOSAGE [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)

* Newer alternative for children ≥ 6 years of age: <u>linaclotide</u>   [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)





> [!TIP]
> Supplementing fiber or fluid intake above daily requirements does not improve <u>constipation</u> in children. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




#### Follow-up [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)




* Reassess patients in 2–4 weeks.

* Improvement in symptoms

* Continue <u>maintenance therapy</u> for 2 months.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cD0aWR)

* Gradually taper <u>laxatives</u> based on clinical response.

* Persistent symptoms

* Confirm <u>treatment adherence</u>.

* Offer reeducation of caregivers.

* Consider possible untreated <u>fecal impaction</u> and/or a change in medication or dosage.

* If refractory to the above measures, consider additional testing (see “Diagnosis”) and/or refer to pediatric gastroenterology.

* <u>Biofeedback training</u> may be beneficial in <u>adolescents</u> with <u>defecatory disorders</u>.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)

* Manage relapses with <u>maintenance therapy</u> and, if needed, disimpaction therapy.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Defecatory disorders</u> are more common in <u>adolescents</u> than young children and should be evaluated for in individuals with <u>constipation</u> refractory to initial therapy. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tY1XI20)




### Management of <u>secondary constipation</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQ1-wg0)




* Evaluate and treat the underlying cause.

* <u>Laxative therapy</u>: the same as that for <u>functional constipation</u>
