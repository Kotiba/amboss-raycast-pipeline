## Summary

<u>Urinary tract</u> infections (UTIs) are infections of the <u>bladder</u>, <u>urethra</u>, <u>ureters</u>, or <u>kidneys</u> that are most commonly caused by bacteria, especially <u>E. coli</u>. Infections of the <u>bladder</u> or <u>urethra</u> are called <u>lower UTIs</u>, whereas infections involving the <u>kidneys</u> or <u>ureters</u> are called upper UTIs. Because women have a shorter <u>urethra</u> and anal and genital regions that are closer in proximity, they are at higher risk of contracting UTIs than men. Other <u>risk factors</u> include sexual intercourse, indwelling urinary catheters, <u>pregnancy</u>, and abnormalities of the <u>urinary tract</u>. UTIs in otherwise healthy, nonpregnant, <u>premenopausal</u> women are considered uncomplicated. UTIs in men or individuals with other <u>risk factors</u> for treatment failure or serious outcomes, such as functional or anatomical abnormalities of the <u>urinary tract</u>, are considered complicated. Clinical findings depend on which part of the <u>urinary tract</u> is affected. <u>Lower UTIs</u> manifest with <u>dysuria</u>, suprapubic <u>pain</u>, <u>urinary urgency</u>, and increased <u>urinary frequency</u>, whereas upper UTIs additionally cause <u>fever</u> and flank <u>pain</u>. Diagnosis is usually clinical and can be supported with findings of <u>pyuria</u> and <u>bacteriuria</u> on <u>urinalysis</u>. A <u>urine culture</u> may be required for patients with a <u>complicated lower UTI</u> or equivocal <u>urinalysis</u> findings. Imaging is rarely required but may be indicated to rule out underlying factors (e.g., <u>urinary tract obstruction</u>, anatomical abnormalities). First-line <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> options for <u>uncomplicated lower UTIs</u> include oral <u>nitrofurantoin</u>, <u>trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole</u>, or <u>fosfomycin</u> for up to 7 days. For <u>complicated lower UTIs</u>, <u>broad-spectrum antibiotic</u> therapy should be given for 7–14 days and treatable underlying factors should be addressed. <u>Recurrent UTIs</u> are common in women and <u>antibiotic prophylaxis</u> may be indicated. <u>Catheter-associated urinary tract infections</u> (CAUTIs) are among the most common <u>healthcare-associated infections</u>; they are frequently caused by resistant bacteria and are treated with catheter removal or replacement in addition to <u>antibiotic therapy</u>. Pregnant women should be screened and treated both for UTIs and for <u>asymptomatic bacteriuria</u>. For the management of upper UTIs, see “<u>Pyelonephritis</u>.”




See also “<u>UTI in children and adolescents</u>.”

## Etiology

### Pathogens




#### Bacteria




* Infection ascends from the <u>urethra</u> to the <u>bladder</u>.

* Can ascend further to the <u>ureters</u> and the <u>renal pelvises</u> (see “<u>Pyelonephritis</u>”)

* Bacteria that cause UTI

* <u>Escherichia coli</u>: leading cause of UTI (approx. 80%) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QtYuWI)

* <u>Staphylococcus saprophyticus</u>: 2<sup>nd</sup> leading cause of UTI in sexually active women

* <u>Klebsiella pneumoniae</u>: 3<sup>rd</sup> leading cause of UTI

* <u>Proteus mirabilis</u>

* Produces <u>ammonia</u>, giving the urine a pungent or irritating <u>smell</u>

* Associated with <u>struvite stone</u> formation

* <u>Nosocomial</u> bacteria: <u>Serratia marcescens</u>, <u>Enterococci</u> spp., and <u>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</u> are associated with increased drug resistance.

* <u>Enterobacter</u> species

* <u>Ureaplasma urealyticum</u>





#### <u>Viruses</u>




* <u>Immunocompromised</u> patients and children are particularly susceptible to viral UTIs. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l8Yv5I)

* <u>Adenovirus</u>, <u>cytomegalovirus</u>, and <u>BK virus</u> are commonly involved in <u>hemorrhagic cystitis</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h9YcMr)





#### Fungi  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fc1kbf0)




* <u>Yeast</u>: rare (usually <u>Candida</u> species)  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tc16bf0)

* Disseminated <u>fungal infections</u> (e.g., <u>Blastomyces dermatitidis</u>, <u>Cryptococcus neoformans</u>)





### Predisposing factors




#### Host-dependent factors




* Structural or functional abnormalities of the <u>urinary tract</u> ;  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2s0T8h)

* Prevent <u>bladder</u> emptying and/or result in urinary stasis

* Examples include:

* <u>Benign prostatic hyperplasia</u>

* Congenital <u>malformations</u> causing <u>vesicoureteral reflux</u>

* <u>Urinary bladder</u> diverticulum

* <u>Neurogenic bladder</u>

* <u>Urinary tract calculi</u>

* Sex

* Female individuals: anatomically predisposed because the <u>urethra</u> is shorter and anal and genital regions are in close proximity → bacteria spreading from the anal region → <u>colonization</u> of <u>vagina</u> → ascending UTIs [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k8Ym5I)

* Male individuals: higher risk in uncircumcised male <u>infants</u>  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i8YJmI)

* <u>Pregnancy</u>: hormonal changes during <u>pregnancy</u> → urinary stasis and vesicoureteral reflux → increased risk of UTIs

* <u>Postmenopause</u>: ↓ <u>estrogen</u> → ↓ vaginal lactobacilli → ↑ <u>vaginal pH</u> → ↑ <u>colonization</u> by <u>E. coli</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k8Ym5I)

* Chronic <u>constipation</u>: common cause of <u>UTIs in children</u>

* Prior conditions

* Previous UTI  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k8Ym5I)

* History of <u>kidney</u> <u>surgery</u>

* <u>Immunosuppression</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* Medication: recent use of <u>antibiotics</u>





#### Other factors




* Sexual intercourse

* Postcoital <u>cystitis</u> (honeymoon cystitis): a <u>lower urinary tract infection</u> that occurs in women after recent <u>sexual activity</u>, which can cause irritation of the urethral meatus and facilitate bacterial entry into the <u>urethra</u> (e.g., from the genital and/or anal region).

* <u>Diaphragm</u> and <u>spermicide</u> use

* <u>Catheter-associated urinary tract infection</u> (<u>CAUTI</u>)

* Caused by indwelling urinary catheters

* Most common cause of <u>nosocomial urinary tract infection</u>

## Classification

## Clinical features

* Clinical features of lower UTI [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qMbCp8)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cUYaXo)

* Irritative lower urinary tract symptoms (<u>LUTS</u>)

* Increased <u>urinary frequency</u>

* <u>Urinary urgency</u>

* <u>Dysuria</u>

* <u>Hematuria</u>

* Suprapubic tenderness

* Clinical features of upper UTI (<u>pyelonephritis</u>) [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ks0mEh)

* <u>Symptoms of lower UTI</u>

* <u>Fever</u>

* Flank <u>pain</u>

* <u>Costovertebral angle tenderness</u>

* Fatigue/<u>malaise</u>

* <u>Nausea and vomiting</u>

* See “Clinical features” in “<u>Pyelonephritis</u>.”

* Additional features (special patient groups) [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qMbCp8)

* Male individuals: <u>pain</u> in the <u>prostatic</u>/perineal area

* Children: See “<u>Clinical features of pediatric UTI</u>.”

* Older adults: <u>delirium</u>/acute confusion  [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/P7bWNE)





> [!WARNING]
> In patients with <u>fever</u> and/or flank <u>pain</u>, which are usually absent in <u>lower UTIs</u>, consider a more serious infection (e.g., <u>pyelonephritis</u>).




> [!TIP]
> <u>Dysuria</u> without urgency or frequency may suggest <u>vaginitis</u> or sexually transmitted <u>urethritis</u> rather than <u>cystitis</u>, especially if accompanied by abnormal vaginal or urethral discharge. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)

## Diagnosis

In the following section, “UTI” refers to both upper and <u>lower UTIs</u>, while “<u>lower UTI</u>” typically refers to <u>cystitis</u> with or without <u>urethritis</u> (see “Classification” for details).




### Approach [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YiXnJB)




Symptomatic, <u>uncomplicated lower UTIs</u> can be <u>diagnosed clinically</u>. In all other patients, <u>urinalysis</u> is the most important initial diagnostic test.




* <u>Uncomplicated lower UTI</u> in women

* Typical symptoms  : Treatment may be initiated without further diagnostics.

* Atypical or unclear symptoms: Perform <u>urinalysis</u> using a <u>urine dipstick test</u> and/or microscopy.  

* Positive <u>urinalysis</u> (proof of <u>pyuria</u> and <u>bacteriuria</u>): Initiate treatment.

* Negative <u>urinalysis</u> but persisting suspicion: Obtain <u>urine culture</u>.

* <u>Complicated lower UTI</u> in women

* Obtain <u>urinalysis</u> and <u>urine culture</u>.

* Consider the need for further diagnostics, depending on history and clinical presentation.

* <u>Lower UTI</u> in men

* Obtain <u>urinalysis</u> and <u>urine culture</u>.

* First <u>febrile UTI</u>: Perform CT or <u>ultrasound</u> of the <u>urinary tract</u>.

* Consider referral to urology (e.g., in case of unclear diagnosis, <u>hematuria</u>, voiding difficulties, or <u>recurrent UTI</u>)

* Concomitant <u>prostatitis</u>: See “Diagnostics” in “<u>Prostatitis</u>.”

* <u>Upper UTI</u>: See “Diagnostics” in “<u>Pyelonephritis</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> UTI is primarily a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> that is supported by typical findings on <u>urinalysis</u>. <u>Urine culture</u> is indicated in select cases to determine the causative <u>pathogen</u> and adapt <u>antibiotic</u> treatment.




> [!WARNING]
> Investigate isolated <u>urethritis</u> (i.e., without concomitant <u>cystitis</u>) for causes other than <u>lower UTI</u> (e.g., <u>STI</u>, <u>reactive arthritis</u>); see “<u>Diagnostics for urethritis</u>.”




### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4s03Eh)




#### <u>Urinalysis</u> [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4s03Eh)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xbbEDH)




* Indications: best initial test for all patients

* Procedure: visual, chemical (<u>dipstick</u>), and microscopic examination of urine

* Specimen collection method

* Clean-catch midstream sample: thought to reduce contamination with vaginal or <u>skin</u> flora

* Straight catheterization of the <u>bladder</u>: may be considered if the risk of contamination is high

* <u>Suprapubic aspiration</u>: no contamination if performed correctly but rarely used due to its invasive nature

* Typical urinalysis findings of UTI  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)

* <u>Pyuria</u>: presence of <u>white blood cells</u> (<u>WBCs</u>) in the urine

* Positive leukocyte esterase: an enzyme produced by <u>WBC</u>

* ≥ 5 <u>WBC</u>/<u>HPF</u> or ≥ 8–10 <u>WBC</u>/mm<sup>3</sup>  [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4s03Eh)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xbbEDH)

* <u>Bacteriuria</u>: presence of <u>bacteria in the urine</u> [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GSXBaB)

* Positive urinary nitrites: indicate bacteria that convert <u>nitrates</u> to nitrites (most commonly <u>gram-negative bacteria</u>; e.g., <u>E.coli</u>)

* Direct visualization by <u>Gram stain</u> (seldom performed)

* Other findings

* <u>Leukocyte</u> casts may indicate <u>pyelonephritis</u>.

* Micro- or <u>macroscopic hematuria</u> may be present.

* Alkaline urine (<u>pH</u> > 8);  and struvite crystals in sediment: indicate <u>urease-producing organisms</u> (e.g., <u>Proteus</u>, <u>Klebsiella</u>, <u>Staphylococcus saprophyticus</u>)  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qMbCp8)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dWco4Y0)

* The presence of squamous <u>epithelial</u> cells can be a sign of contamination.





#### Urine culture [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4s03Eh)




* Indications

* Suspicion for <u>complicated UTI</u> or healthcare-associated UTI

* Suspicion for <u>pyelonephritis</u> or <u>urosepsis</u>

* Suspicion for <u>uncomplicated cystitis</u> with either of the following:

* History of <u>recurrent UTIs</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EPX8gy)

* Equivocal <u>urinalysis</u>

* Atypical symptoms

* Concern for multiresistant pathogens, e.g., due to recent <u>antibiotic</u> use [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* Age ≥ 65 years [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F3Xg4B)

* Follow-up cultures for test of cure in the following cases:

* Nonresolving symptoms despite <u>antibiotic</u> treatment

* Anatomic or functional abnormalities of the <u>urinary tract</u>

* Continued pathological findings on <u>urinalysis</u>

* Interpretation
* Cultures are considered positive if either of the following is present: 

* Significant bacteriuria: defined as ≥ 10<sup>5</sup><u>CFU</u>/mL in a clean-catch specimen  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bpbHLu)

* Any organisms in a specimen obtained by <u>suprapubic aspiration</u>  [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4s03Eh)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s3XtPB)

* Typical colony findings

* <u>E. coli</u>: intensely pink on <u>MacConkey agar</u>

* <u>Klebsiella pneumoniae</u>: viscous colonies

* <u>Serratia marcescens</u>: often red in appearance

* <u>Proteus mirabilis</u>: swarming motility pattern

* <u>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</u>: blue-green pigment





> [!TIP]
> In patients with complicated or <u>recurrent urinary tract infections</u>, a <u>urine culture</u> should be obtained prior to initiating <u>antibiotic</u> treatment. <u>False negative</u> results are possible if a culture is obtained after the patient has received <u>antibiotics</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> In patients with lower abdominal <u>pain</u> and <u>sterile pyuria</u>, consider <u>bladder</u> or ureteral irritation from an intraabdominal or <u>pelvic</u> infection unrelated to the <u>urinary tract</u> (e.g., <u>appendicitis</u>, <u>diverticulitis</u>). [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Sc1ybf0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hc1cXf0)




#### Additional diagnostics [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YiXnJB)




* <u>Pregnancy test</u>: indicated in women of childbearing age

* Testing for <u>sexually transmitted infections</u> (<u>STIs</u>)

* Indicated in patients with <u>STI risk factors</u> and/or symptoms of an <u>STI</u>

* At-risk patients should be tested for <u>Chlamydia trachomatis</u> and <u>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</u>.

* Blood tests

* Not routinely performed in patients with <u>lower UTI</u>

* May be indicated to assess concomitant conditions (e.g., <u>diabetes mellitus</u>) and exclude differential diagnoses





### Imaging [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RcclXY0)




Imaging is generally not indicated or helpful for the diagnosis of <u>lower UTI</u>, but it may be performed in select patients to rule out complicating factors (e.g., <u>urinary tract obstruction</u>) or if <u>complicated pyelonephritis</u> or <u>urosepsis</u> are suspected. For imaging indications and findings in <u>upper UTI</u>, see “Diagnostics” in “<u>Pyelonephritis</u>.”




* Indications may include:

* Suspected <u>urinary tract obstruction</u>

* Severe illness (e.g., <u>septic shock</u>)

* Early recurrence of UTI (within two weeks of appropriate treatment)

* Persistent <u>bacteriuria</u> despite treatment

* Recurrent <u>complicated UTI</u>

* Men with <u>febrile UTI</u>

* Modalities

* CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u> with or without IV contrast

* Most sensitive for initial imaging

* Noncontrast CT is useful to diagnose <u>urolithiasis</u>.

* IV contrast is indicated if complications (e.g., <u>abscess</u>) or other causes of obstruction are suspected.

* <u>Ultrasound of the kidneys</u> and <u>bladder</u>

* Perform if there are contraindications to contrast or radiation.

* Useful for detecting <u>hydronephrosis</u> and measuring <u>postvoid residual volume</u> if an obstruction is suspected

* Additional modalities include <u>MRI</u> abdomen and <u>pelvis</u>, <u>voiding cystourethrography</u>, and <u>retrograde cystography</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Imaging is not routinely necessary for patients with <u>uncomplicated lower UTI</u>.

## Differential diagnoses

### Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GSXBaB)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cecqab0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ipbJ6u)




* Description

* Presence of ≥ 100,000 <u>CFU</u>/mL in at least two voided urine samples in patients with no <u>symptoms of UTI</u> (e.g., <u>dysuria</u>, frequency, urgency, suprapubic <u>pain</u>)

* <u>Bacteriuria</u> typically resolves spontaneously in healthy, nonpregnant women without any side effects.

* Women with <u>asymptomatic bacteriuria</u> are more likely to develop future UTIs than women with no <u>bacteriuria</u>.

* <u>Epidemiology</u>
* <u>Prevalence</u>

* Occurs in 1–5% of healthy, <u>premenopausal</u> women and in 2–10% of pregnant women

* Occurs in 9–25% of older adults and individuals with <u>diabetes</u>, indwelling catheters, or <u>spinal cord injuries</u>

* Etiology: <u>E. coli</u> is the most common causative organism.

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* Age ≥ 65 years

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Spinal cord injury</u>

* Indwelling urethral catheter

* <u>Pregnancy</u>

* Diagnosis: <u>urinalysis with microscopy</u>

* Mid-stream urine sample: bacterial growth ≥ 100,000 <u>CFU</u>/mL in two consecutive samples in women or in one sample in men

* Catheterized urine sample: bacterial growth ≥ 100 <u>CFU</u>/mL in one sample in women or men

* Management

* Treatment is recommended in:

* Individuals undergoing endourological procedures with possible <u>mucosal</u> trauma [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dqboxu)

* Pregnant women: See “Treatment of ASB and <u>lower UTIs</u> in pregnant women” below.

* Treatment is not recommended in:

* Healthy, nonpregnant women

* Older adults and individuals with <u>diabetes</u>, indwelling catheters, or <u>spinal cord injuries</u>





### Other differential diagnoses




* <u>Interstitial cystitis</u>

* <u>Vaginitis</u>

* <u>Pelvic inflammatory disease</u> (<u>PID</u>)

* <u>Prostatitis</u>  [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h30cRf)

* <u>Urethritis</u> with <u>sexually transmitted infections</u> (e.g., <u>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</u>, <u>Chlamydia trachomatis</u>), <u>Candida</u>, or irritants

* Tuberculous <u>cystitis</u> (see “<u>Urogenital tuberculosis</u>”)

* Drug-induced <u>cystitis</u> (e.g., <u>cyclophosphamide</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>) or radiation-induced <u>cystitis</u>

* <u>Hemorrhagic cystitis</u>

* Trauma

* Structural abnormalities of the <u>urethra</u> (e.g., <u>diverticula</u>, strictures)

* Other diseases of the <u>bladder</u> (e.g., <u>urolithiasis</u>, <u>bladder cancer</u>, foreign objects)





The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Treatment

In the following section, “UTI” refers to both upper and <u>lower UTIs</u>, while “<u>lower UTI</u>” typically refers to <u>cystitis</u> with or without <u>urethritis</u> (see “Classification” for details).




### General principles [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YiXnJB)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/u9Yp6r)




* <u>Antibiotic</u> treatment is recommended for all patients with symptomatic UTI. 

* The optimal therapy depends on disease severity, local resistance patterns, and patient characteristics (e.g., <u>allergies</u>).

* Initial treatment is with an empiric regimen, which is maintained for <u>uncomplicated cystitis</u>.

* In unclear or complicated cases, the regimen may subsequently have to be adjusted based on <u>urine culture</u> data.

* Consider the need for supportive treatment.

* Phenazopyridine, DOSAGE a urinary <u>analgesic</u>, can be used for symptomatic relief for a maximum of 2 days.

* <u>Oral analgesia</u>, e.g., with <u>NSAIDs</u>, can provide additional relief.

* For the treatment of <u>upper UTI</u>, see “<u>Treatment of pyelonephritis</u>.”





> [!WARNING]
> Consider empiric treatment of <u>STIs</u> in patients with isolated <u>urethritis</u>, <u>prostatitis</u>, or suspected <u>pelvic inflammatory disease</u> (see “<u>Treatment of PID</u>”).




### <u>Uncomplicated lower UTI</u>  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/u9Yp6r)[[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3ccSXY0)




* Management can typically be done in the outpatient setting with oral therapy.

* Treatment duration depends on the chosen <u>antibiotic</u> agent.

* Symptom relief can be expected to occur after an average of 36 hours. [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bpbHLu)

* Persistent symptoms despite <u>antibiotic therapy</u> suggest <u>complicated UTI</u> and/or indicate the need to change the empiric therapy.





#### Empiric antibiotic treatment of uncomplicated lower UTIs




* First-line treatment 

* <u>Nitrofurantoin</u> DOSAGE for 5 days

* <u>Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole</u> (<u>TMP/SMX</u>) DOSAGE for 3 days

* <u>Fosfomycin</u> (single dose) DOSAGE

* Second-line treatment: <u>beta-lactam antibiotics</u> for 5–7 days 

* <u>Aminopenicillins</u> plus <u>beta-lactamase inhibitors</u>, e.g., <u>amoxicillin/clavulanic acid</u> DOSAGE

* Oral <u>cephalosporins</u>, e.g., <u>cefpodoxime</u> DOSAGE, cefdinir DOSAGE, or cefaclor DOSAGE

* Alternatives: Consider <u>fluoroquinolones</u>, e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u> DOSAGE for 3 days for patients with previous infections with bacteria resistant to other drug classes.  [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3ccSXY0)





### <u>Complicated lower UTI</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C9Yqpr)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HecKZb0)




* There are few recommendations for the <u>empiric antibiotic treatment</u> of <u>complicated lower UTI</u>. 

* <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> should have broad-spectrum activity against the expected uropathogens.

* Because UTIs in men can involve the <u>prostate</u>, <u>antibiotics</u> should be able to penetrate <u>prostate</u> tissue (e.g., <u>fluoroquinolones</u>, <u>TMP/SMX</u>).

* In addition to <u>antibiotic therapy</u>, complicating factors (e.g., obstruction) should be treated, if possible.

* For UTI in men, referral to urology may be warranted especially in the following cases:

* Treatment failure or <u>recurrent UTIs</u>

* <u>Hematuria</u>

* Voiding difficulties or acute <u>urine retention</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YiXnJB)

* Suspected <u>acute bacterial prostatitis</u>

* Hospitalization and initial intravenous treatment may be necessary in the following cases: [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* Severe systemic symptoms, e.g., <u>signs of shock</u>

* Inability to tolerate oral <u>antibiotics</u>

* Severe comorbidities, e.g., <u>immunocompromise</u> or <u>heart failure</u>





#### Antibiotic treatment of complicated lower UTIs [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C9Yqpr)




* <u>Antibiotic therapy</u> must be adapted to culture results and is commonly given for 7–14 days.

* Options for the initial empiric treatment of <u>complicated lower UTIs</u> include:

* <u>Fluoroquinolones</u> PO or IV: e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u> DOSAGE or <u>levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Beta lactams</u>

* Second-generation or <u>third-generation cephalosporins</u>: e.g., <u>ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Extended-spectrum penicillins</u>: e.g., <u>ampicillin/sulbactam</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Aminoglycosides</u> (e.g., <u>gentamicin</u> DOSAGE): treatment option if <u>fluoroquinolones</u> or <u>beta lactams</u> are contraindicated or as an addition to <u>beta lactams</u>

* Reasonable options if the <u>pathogen</u> is susceptible include: [[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A1XRjC)

* <u>Nitrofurantoin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>TMP/SMX</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Fosfomycin</u> (multiple doses) DOSAGE  [[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A1XRjC)[[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YScnyb0)





> [!WARNING]
> Treatment regimens for UTI in men should include <u>antibiotics</u> that are able to penetrate <u>prostate</u> tissue (e.g., <u>fluoroquinolones</u> or <u>TMP/SMX</u>). <u>Fosfomycin</u> or <u>nitrofurantoin</u> are generally not adequate.




#### Management of complicating factors




* <u>Nephrolithiasis</u>: In UTI with <u>renal obstruction</u>, urgent urology consultation is required for drainage.  [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/q70C5h)

* Indwelling medical devices (e.g., <u>ureteral stents</u>, <u>percutaneous nephrostomy</u> tubes) [[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EjX8Xy)[[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Eec80b0)

* Management of infections may require exchange or removal of the device, especially when it is obstructed.

* Urology should be consulted urgently for further management.

* Other treatable factors should be addressed: e.g., optimal blood sugar control for diabetics.





### Recurrent UTI [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EPX8gy)[[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vPXAgy)




<u>Recurrent UTIs</u> are common in women and are defined as ≥ 3 episodes of symptomatic, culture-proven UTI in one year or ≥ 2 episodes in 6 months. Management involves the implementation of preventive measures and antibacterial prophylaxis in addition to the <u>antibiotic</u> treatment of acute episodes.




#### Acute management




Whenever possible, obtain a <u>urine culture</u> for every episode prior to initiating <u>antibiotic therapy</u>.




* Choice of <u>antibiotic</u>

* First recurrence: See “<u>Antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated lower UTIs</u>” and “<u>Antibiotic treatment for complicated lower UTIs</u>” for initial empiric regimens.

* Frequent recurrences

* Regimens should be tailored to the patient and prior culture results.

* Consider increased <u>antibiotic</u> resistance to standard agents.

* <u>Antibiotics</u> must be adapted to the current culture results once available.

* Self-initiated treatment

* May be considered for motivated women who have documented <u>recurrent UTIs</u>.

* Patients are given a prescription to fill when symptoms start.

* Reevaluation is required if no improvement occurs within 48 hours.

* Follow-up: Consider test of cure with repeat <u>urine culture</u> only if symptoms persist beyond 7 days.





#### <u>Antibiotic prophylaxis</u>  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EPX8gy)




* Indication: may be considered in all women with recurrent <u>uncomplicated UTIs</u>

* Continuous prophylaxis 

* Typically taken for 3–12 months with periodic reassessment

* Regimens

* <u>Trimethoprim</u> (TMP) daily DOSAGE

* <u>TMP/SMX</u> daily DOSAGE

* <u>Cephalexin</u> daily DOSAGE

* <u>Nitrofurantoin</u> daily DOSAGE

* <u>Fosfomycin</u> every 10 days DOSAGE

* Intermittent or postcoital prophylaxis

* Recommended for women who have <u>recurrent UTIs</u> associated with <u>sexual activity</u>

* Substances

* <u>TMP/SMX</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Cephalexin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Nitrofurantoin</u> DOSAGE





#### Nonantibiotic prophylaxis [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RG1l_R0)




* There is insufficient high-quality data to support the use of cranberry products for preventing UTIs. [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Uc1bbf0)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jc1_Xf0)

* Topical <u>estrogen</u> therapy may be considered in peri- and <u>postmenopausal</u> women.

* Behavioral modifications (e.g., increased fluid intake, postcoital voiding) may be helpful.





### Candiduria [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nfa75P)




* <u>Candida</u> isolated from the urine rarely indicates systemic infection, but it may be a marker for greater mortality in severely ill patients.

* Predisposing factors should be treated in all cases of <u>candiduria</u>, e.g., by removing indwelling catheters whenever possible.

* Indications for <u>antifungal</u> treatment include symptomatic <u>cystitis</u> or <u>pyelonephritis</u>, <u>neutropenia</u>, or a planned urologic procedure.

* Commonly used <u>antifungals</u> include <u>fluconazole</u> and <u>amphotericin B</u>.

* Consult the infectious disease service for assistance with appropriate <u>antifungal</u> treatment.

## Prevention

* Behavioral modifications [[54]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dCYoIr)

* Increased fluid intake

* Timely <u>bladder</u> voiding

* Postcoital voiding

* Adequate genital hygiene

* <u>Clean intermittent catheterization</u>

* Indicated for individuals with <u>neurogenic bladder</u>

* Reduces <u>incidence</u> of <u>catheter-associated UTIs</u>

* Prophylaxis: indicated for <u>recurrent urinary tract infections</u> (see ”<u>Chemoprophylaxis</u>” in “<u>Recurrent UTI</u>” for details)

## Special patient groups

See also “<u>Urinary tract infections in children and adolescents</u>.”




### UTI in older adults [[56]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4Uc3Wb0)




* UTI is very common in advanced age, both in individuals who live in long-term care facilities and those who do not.

* <u>Symptoms of UTI</u> are more commonly atypical (e.g., <u>back pain</u>, <u>pelvic pain</u>, <u>constipation</u>, <u>urinary incontinence</u>, and <u>altered mental status</u>).

* Management principles are generally the same as outlined above.

* Preventative strategies include increased fluid intake and increasing mobility (see also “Prevention” above).





### UTI in pregnancy [[57]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PUcWWb0)




#### Pathophysiology




* <u>Pregnancy</u> may increase the risk of recurrent <u>bacteriuria</u> and UTIs.

* Factors involved

* Increased <u>progesterone</u> levels → urinary stasis and ureteral <u>smooth muscle</u> relaxation and dilation → <u>vesicoureteral reflux</u>

* Gravid <u>uterus</u> → ↑ pressure on the <u>bladder</u> and compression of <u>ureters</u> → <u>vesicoureteral reflux</u>

* Relative <u>immunosuppression</u>





#### <u>Asymptomatic bacteriuria</u> in <u>pregnancy</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cecqab0)




* See “<u>Asymptomatic bacteriuria</u>” for diagnostic criteria.

* Screening is recommended for all pregnant women in the <u>first trimester</u>.

* Treatment is always required, as evidence suggests that it reduces the risk of <u>pyelonephritis</u> and fetal complications.





#### Treatment of ASB and lower UTIs in pregnancy [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[58]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RUcl1b0)




Although <u>UTI in pregnancy</u> is generally considered complicated, treatment regimens for ASB and lower <u>UTI in pregnancy</u> may include those typically used for <u>uncomplicated UTI</u> and should be adapted to the patient's individual risk and <u>urine culture</u> results.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mdcVqY0)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cecqab0)




* Upper UTIs generally require hospitalization and intravenous treatment; see “<u>Pyelonephritis in pregnancy</u>.”

* <u>Empiric antibiotics</u> for ASB and <u>lower UTI</u> considered appropriate during <u>pregnancy</u> include:

* <u>Fosfomycin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Beta-lactam antibiotics</u> for 5–7 days

* Oral <u>cephalosporins</u> (e.g., <u>cefpodoxime</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Aminopenicillins</u> plus <u>beta-lactamase inhibitors</u> (e.g., <u>amoxicillin/clavulanic acid</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Antibiotics</u> that are potentially appropriate during specific stages of <u>pregnancy</u> include:

* <u>Nitrofurantoin</u> DOSAGE for 5 days: Avoid close to the delivery date.

* <u>TMP/SMX</u> DOSAGE for 3 days: Avoid during the <u>first trimester</u> and past 32 weeks' <u>gestation</u>.

* <u>Antibiotics</u> that should be avoided include <u>fluoroquinolones</u> and <u>aminoglycosides</u>.

* Follow-up culture should be considered a week after treatment for ASB and UTI.

* Negative culture: no further treatment required unless new <u>UTI symptoms</u> develop

* Positive culture: indicates persistent <u>bacteriuria</u>; <u>antibiotic therapy</u> based on the culture results





> [!TIP]
> <u>Asymptomatic bacteriuria</u> in <u>pregnancy</u> is a <u>risk factor</u> for <u>pyelonephritis</u> and should be treated.
