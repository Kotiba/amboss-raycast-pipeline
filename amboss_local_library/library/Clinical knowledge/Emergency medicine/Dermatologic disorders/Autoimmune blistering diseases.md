# Autoimmune blistering diseases

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Dermatologic disorders > Autoimmune blistering diseases*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jk0_nT)

---

## Summary

Autoimmune <u>blistering diseases</u> are <u>skin</u> conditions characterized by the formation of <u>blisters</u> due to autoimmune destruction of cellular or extracellular adhesion molecules. The most common <u>autoimmune blistering disease</u> is <u>bullous pemphigoid</u>, which is a chronic, <u>relapsing-remitting disease</u> that primarily affects older adults. It typically manifests with large, tense, pruritic <u>vesicles</u> and <u>bullae</u> and usually responds well to treatment with potent topical <u>glucocorticoids</u>. <u>Pemphigus vulgaris</u> manifests with painful <u>mucosal</u> and/or cutaneous lesions that rupture easily, forming crusted <u>erosions</u>. First-line therapy for most patients consists of <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u> and adjunctive <u>immunosuppressants</u>. Extensive <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u> typically requires hospitalization. Complications may be life-threatening. <u>Dermatitis herpetiformis</u> is characterized by intensely pruritic <u>papules</u> and <u>vesicles</u> and primarily affects the extensor surfaces of the extremities. It is considered a cutaneous manifestation of <u>celiac disease</u>. The prognosis is good for patients who adhere to a lifelong <u>gluten</u>-free diet. Oral <u>dapsone</u> rapidly improves cutaneous lesions. <u>Direct immunofluorescence</u> microscopy of perilesional <u>skin</u> is the <u>gold standard</u> for the diagnosis of all three diseases. <u>Serology</u> using indirect <u>immunofluorescence</u> or <u>ELISA</u> to detect <u>autoantibodies</u> confirms the diagnosis, especially if other findings are equivocal or atypical.

## Bullous pemphigoid

### Definition




<u>Bullous pemphigoid</u> is a chronic relapsing-remitting <u>autoimmune blistering disease</u> that primarily affects older adults.




### Clinical features [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)




* <u>Prodromal</u> (nonbullous) stage

* <u>Pruritus</u> with or without <u>urticarial</u> and/or <u>papular</u> lesions

* May last several weeks to months

* Approx. 20% of patients do not progress to the <u>bullous</u> stage. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)

* <u>Bullous</u> stage 

* Large (1–4 cm), tense, pruritic <u>vesicles</u> and <u>bullae</u> on <u>erythematous</u> base   

* Distribution: often symmetric

* Common sites: trunk and flexural surfaces of the limbs

* Typically heal without scarring, but may result in pigmentation changes

* Oral involvement in 10–30% of patients (other <u>mucosal</u> involvement is rare) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)

* No <u>Nikolsky sign</u>

* Features of associated diseases, e.g.:  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)

* <u>Clinical features of multiple sclerosis</u>

* <u>Clinical features of Parkinson disease</u>

* <u>Clinical features of major neurocognitive disorder</u>





> [!TIP]
> Disease extent can be assessed using the <u>Bullous</u> Pemphigoid Disease Activity Index or daily <u>blister</u> count. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)

### Diagnosis [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)




#### Approach




* Obtain perilesional and lesional punch <u>biopsies</u> in patients with characteristic clinical features..

* Atypical clinical features: Obtain <u>serologies</u>.

* Refer to dermatology if there is diagnostic uncertainty.





#### Clinical criteria [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)




<u>Bullous pemphigoid</u> is likely in patients with at least three of the following:




* Patient age > 70 years

* No <u>mucosal</u> involvement

* No <u>atrophic</u> scars

* No head and neck involvement





#### <u>Biopsy</u>




* <u>Direct immunofluorescence</u> microscopy (<u>gold standard</u>)

* Sample: perilesional <u>punch biopsy</u>

* Findings: linear deposits of <u>IgG</u> and/or <u>complement C3</u> along the <u>basement membrane zone</u>

* <u>Histopathology</u>

* Sample: lesional <u>punch biopsy</u>

* Findings

* Subepidermal <u>blistering</u>

* Eosinophilic infiltration and, occasionally, <u>neutrophils</u> and <u>mononuclear cells</u>





#### <u>Serology</u>




* Methods: <u>ELISA</u> and indirect <u>immunofluorescence</u>

* Findings: <u>IgG</u> <u>autoantibodies</u> against BPAg1 (BP230) and/or BPAg2 (BP180)





> [!TIP]
> Diagnosing nonbullous disease can be challenging and may require additional immunopathological studies.

### Management [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)




All patients should be managed by a dermatologist.




#### Pharmacotherapy [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)




* First-line: potent topical <u>glucocorticoids</u>, e.g., <u>clobetasol propionate</u> (<u>off-label</u>)  DOSAGE [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)

* Rapidly progressive or extensive disease : <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u>, e.g., <u>prednisone</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)

* Options for relapsing or refractory disease or contraindications to <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u> include:

* <u>Steroid</u>-sparing <u>immunosuppressants</u>, e.g., <u>azathioprine</u>, <u>mycophenolate mofetil</u>, <u>methotrexate</u>

* <u>Tetracyclines</u>, e.g., <u>doxycycline</u>

* <u>Biologics</u>, e.g., <u>rituximab</u>, <u>omalizumab</u>, <u>dupilumab</u>

* <u>Intravenous immunoglobulin</u>





#### Disposition



* Admit the following individuals for initial inpatient management:

* Patients with extensive disease

* Older adults

* Patients with <u>multimorbidity</u>




### Prognosis [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/skdt6J0)




* Long-term remission is possible.

* Relapse occurs in ∼ 30% of patients within 12 months of treatment and in ∼ 50% of patients after stopping treatment. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkdK6J0)





### Special patient groups




See “<u>Gestational pemphigoid</u>.”

## Pemphigus vulgaris

### Definition




<u>Pemphigus vulgaris</u> is an <u>autoimmune blistering disease</u> that causes painful <u>mucosal</u> and/or cutaneous lesions.




### Clinical features [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMdonq0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QLduxq0)




<u>Pemphigus vulgaris</u> may manifest as <u>mucosal</u>-dominant, mucocutaneous, or, less commonly, cutaneous-only disease.




* <u>Mucosal</u> involvement: flaccid <u>blisters</u> that rupture easily, causing painful <u>erosions</u>   

* Typically precedes cutaneous involvement

* Often starts in the oral <u>mucosa</u>

* Common site: buccal <u>mucosa</u>

* Possible <u>malnutrition</u> and weight loss due to painful oral <u>erosions</u>

* Cutaneous involvement: <u>erythematous</u> <u>macules</u> that evolve into flaccid <u>blisters</u> with clear fluid and rupture to form painful, often crusted <u>erosions</u>   

* Often occurs weeks to months after <u>mucosal</u> lesions

* Localized or generalized

* Common sites: areas of mechanical <u>stress</u>, seborrheic regions (e.g., head, trunk, groin)

* Typically heals without scarring, but may cause pigmentation changes

* Possible <u>Nikolsky sign</u> during active disease [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMdonq0)





> [!TIP]
> Disease extent can be assessed using the <u>Pemphigus</u> Disease Area Index or the Autoimmune <u>Bullous</u> <u>Skin</u> Disorder Intensity Score. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMdonq0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)

### Diagnosis [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMdonq0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RMdloq0)




#### Approach




* Consider <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u> in patients with characteristic clinical features.

* Obtain perilesional and lesional punch <u>biopsies</u>.

* Obtain <u>serologies</u>.

* Refer to dermatology if there is diagnostic uncertainty.





#### <u>Biopsy</u>




* <u>Direct immunofluorescence</u> (<u>gold standard</u>) [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMdonq0)

* Sample: perilesional <u>punch biopsy</u>

* Finding: reticular <u>IgG</u> and <u>complement C3</u> deposits on <u>keratinocytes</u>

* <u>Histopathology</u>

* Sample: lesional <u>punch biopsy</u>

* Findings 

* <u>Intraepidermal</u> <u>acantholysis</u>

* <u>Basal keratinocytes</u> adherent to the <u>basement membrane</u> (referred to as the “tombstone effect”)

* Occasional eosinophilic or neutrophilic infiltrates





#### <u>Serology</u>




* Indirect <u>immunofluorescence</u>: <u>IgG</u> <u>autoantibodies</u> bind to <u>epithelial</u> cells in a reticular pattern.

* <u>ELISA</u>: <u>IgG antibodies</u> against <u>desmoglein</u> 1 and/or <u>desmoglein</u> 3

### Management [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)




#### Pharmacotherapy [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)




Prescribe the following in consultation with a dermatologist.




* First-line

* <u>Systemic glucocorticoids</u>, e.g., <u>prednisone</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE with or without <u>mycophenolate mofetil</u> OR <u>azathioprine</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)

* OR <u>rituximab</u> with or without <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u>

* Additional therapies

* Severe or refractory disease: <u>IVIg</u>, <u>plasmapheresis</u>

* Symptomatic relief (especially for <u>mucosal</u> lesions): topical <u>glucocorticoids</u>, <u>anesthetic</u> gels

* <u>Pain management</u>

* <u>Antibiotic therapy for skin and soft tissue infections</u> as needed





#### <u>Supportive care</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Hemodynamic support</u>, e.g., <u>IV fluid therapy</u> as needed

* <u>Electrolyte repletion</u>

* Local <u>wound care</u>





### Complications [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Dehydration</u>

* Secondary infection

* <u>Venous thromboembolism</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Complications from the disease and its treatment may be life-threatening without adequate treatment. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)




### Disposition [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VMdGnq0)




* Consult a dermatologist for all patients.

* Patients with extensive <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u> typically require hospital admission.

## Pemphigus foliaceus

* Etiology: : autoimmune disease (only <u>antibodies</u> against <u>desmoglein</u> 1 detectable)

* Clinical features 

* <u>Bullae</u> in the <u>epidermis</u>; , which burst spontaneously because of their <u>superficial</u> location

* Localized mainly on the face, head, <u>stomach</u>, and back

* Almost never with <u>mucosal</u> involvement (unlike <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u>)

* Treatment

* First-line: <u>systemic glucocorticoids</u>

* <u>Dapsone</u>

* Topical <u>glucocorticoids</u>

## Epidermolysis bullosa

### Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (<u>EBA</u>) [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vubAGv)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dub1tv)




* Definition: acquired autoimmune disease with subepidermal <u>blistering</u>; associated with other autoimmune diseases

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: onset in adulthood (usually 40–60 years of age)

* Etiology: <u>autoantibodies</u> targeted against type VII <u>collagen</u>

* Clinical features

* Non-inflammatory form (most common): tense <u>vesicles</u> and <u>blisters</u> on extensor surfaces that are <u>prone</u> to trauma (e.g., hands, knees, knuckles)

* Inflammatory form: tense <u>bullae</u> and <u>vesicles</u> similar to <u>bullous pemphigoid</u>

* Diagnostics

* Direct/indirect <u>immunofluorescence</u>

* <u>ELISA</u>: detection of <u>IgG antibodies</u> against type VII <u>collagen</u>

* Management

* <u>Systemic glucocorticoids</u>, <u>immunosuppressants</u>

* Avoid physical trauma to <u>skin</u>

### Epidermolysis bullosa simplex (<u>EBS</u>)




* Definition: a genetic condition that causes the <u>skin</u> to become very fragile and <u>blister</u> easily in response to minor injury or friction

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: <u>EBS</u> is the most common type of EB.

* Etiology

* <u>Autosomal dominant inheritance</u>

* Mutations in <u>keratin</u> <u>genes</u> (e.g., KRT5, KRT14)

* Pathophysiology: mutations in <u>keratin</u> <u>proteins</u> → defective assembly of <u>keratin</u> filaments → disruption of the basal layer of <u>keratinocytes</u> → ↑ fragility of <u>epithelial tissue</u>

## Mucous membrane pemphigoid

* Definition: : chronic <u>autoimmune blistering disease</u> that affects the <u>mucous membranes</u> and heals with scarring

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: : most frequently affects elderly individuals

* Etiology

* Deposition of <u>autoantibodies</u> against components of the <u>epithelial</u> <u>basement membrane</u>

* Associated with <u>HLA</u>-DQB1*0301 <u>allele</u>

* Clinical presentation

* <u>Blistering</u> lesions primarily in the oral <u>mucosa</u> and <u>conjunctiva</u>, but the nasal and genital <u>mucosa</u> may also be affected

* In ⅓ of patients, <u>skin</u> involvement with scarring is also observed.

* If only the <u>conjunctiva</u> are affected: <u>ocular cicatricial pemphigoid</u>

* Treatment: <u>glucocorticoids</u> (additional/other <u>immunosuppressants</u> according to severity)
