## Summary

Cervicitis is the inflammation of the <u>cervix</u>. Cervicitis is most commonly caused by infection (e.g., with <u>Chlamydia trachomatis</u> or <u>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</u>); noninfectious causes include <u>cervical dysplasia</u> and mechanical or chemical irritation. Patients are often asymptomatic but may present with <u>vaginal discharge</u>, abnormal <u>vaginal bleeding</u>, <u>dyspareunia</u>, or <u>pelvic pain</u>. Cervicitis is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>, based on <u>gynecologic examination</u> findings of mucopurulent or <u>purulent</u> <u>endocervical</u> discharge and a <u>friable cervix</u>. In isolated cervicitis, <u>clinical features of pelvic inflammatory disease</u> (<u>PID</u>) and <u>diagnostic criteria for PID</u> are absent. Initial diagnostic studies include evaluation for <u>STIs</u>. Treatment consists of <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> for individuals with <u>risk factors for STIs</u>; individuals without <u>risk factors for STIs</u> are treated with <u>antibiotics</u> if diagnostic testing confirms an infectious cause. Patients with no evidence of infection should be referred to gynecology for further evaluation. Untreated cervicitis can lead to <u>PID</u>, in which the infection spreads beyond the <u>cervix</u> to the upper <u>female reproductive tract</u> (i.e., the <u>uterus</u>, <u>fallopian tubes</u>, and <u>ovaries</u>) and/or <u>peritoneal cavity</u>.

---

## Etiology

* Infectious:  (most common): <u>C. trachomatis</u>, <u>N. gonorrhoeae</u>, <u>HSV-2</u>  , <u>Trichomonas vaginalis</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

* Noninfectious  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A1XRjC)

* Mechanical irritation (e.g., <u>cervical caps</u>, <u>contraceptive diaphragms</u>, tampons)

* Chemical irritation (e.g., <u>contraceptive</u> creams, latex exposure)

* <u>Cervical dysplasia</u> or polyps





### <u>Risk factors</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* Young age (< 25 years)

* Multiple sexual partners

* New sexual partner

* Unprotected intercourse

## Clinical features

Cervicitis is often asymptomatic. Clinical features may include: [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Scaybj)




* <u>Vaginal discharge</u>: may be <u>purulent</u>, blood-tinged, and/or malodorous

* Postcoital or <u>intermenstrual bleeding</u>

* <u>Dyspareunia</u>

* Lower abdominal or <u>pelvic pain</u>

* Symptoms of the underlying condition (e.g., <u>genital lesions</u> in <u>HSV infections</u>)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Fever</u> is not a typical symptom of cervicitis; presence of <u>fever</u> should raise suspicion for <u>PID</u>. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

## Diagnosis

### Clinical evaluation [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




Isolated cervicitis is <u>diagnosed clinically</u>, based on both of the following.




* Presence of ≥ 1 of the following findings on <u>speculum examination</u>:

* Mucopurulent or <u>purulent</u> discharge in the <u>endocervical</u> canal or on an <u>endocervical swab</u>

* <u>Erythematous</u>, <u>edematous</u>, <u>friable cervix</u>

* Absence of both:

* <u>Clinical features of PID</u>

* <u>Diagnostic criteria for PID</u> (e.g., <u>cervical motion tenderness</u>) on <u>bimanual pelvic exam</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Abdominal tenderness and <u>cervical motion tenderness</u> are not characteristic of isolated cervicitis and suggest that infection has ascended, causing concomitant <u>PID</u>.




### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* Vaginal or cervical swab testing

* <u>NAAT</u> for <u>N. gonorrhoeae</u> and <u>C. trachomatis</u>

* <u>Wet mount preparation</u> to evaluate for:

* <u>WBCs</u> (<u>leukorrhea</u>)

* <u>Trichomoniasis</u>

* <u>Bacterial vaginosis</u>

* If <u>wet mount</u> is negative for <u>Trichomoniasis</u>, obtain <u>NAAT</u> or culture for <u>Trichomoniasis</u>.  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

* Consider <u>diagnostics for Mycoplasma genitalium infection</u>.

* Test all patients with cervicitis for <u>HIV</u> and <u>syphilis</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Cervicitis is unlikely if < 10 <u>WBCs</u>/<u>HPF</u> are seen on microscopic evaluation of vaginal fluid (<u>wet mount</u>). [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

## Treatment

### Approach [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




If presentation is concerning for <u>PID</u>, provide <u>treatment for PID</u>.




* Treat with <u>empiric antibiotic therapy for cervicitis</u> if:

* Patient has <u>risk factors for STIs</u>

* There is concern for poor follow up

* <u>NAAT</u> testing could not be done

* All other patients: Consider waiting for diagnostic test results.

* If positive, treat with appropriate therapy based on the causative agent. See:

* “<u>Treatment of genital chlamydia</u>”

* “<u>Treatment of gonorrhea</u>”

* “Treatment” in “<u>Trichomoniasis</u>”

* If negative, refer to gynecology for evaluation for non-infectious causes.

* For patient's sexual partners from the preceding 60 days, recommend:

* <u>STI testing</u>

* Presumptive treatment for <u>chlamydia</u>, <u>gonorrhea</u>, and/or <u>trichomoniasis</u> based on patient's test results

* Advise abstinence from sexual intercourse until patient and partner have completed treatment and are asymptomatic.

* After completion of treatment, follow up to evaluate for symptom resolution.





> [!TIP]
> Cervicitis is not an indication for <u>IUD</u> removal, but a new <u>IUD</u> should not be placed in patients with active cervicitis. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




### Empiric antibiotic therapy for cervicitis [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* <u>Doxycycline</u> (preferred)  DOSAGE OR <u>azithromycin</u> (alternative) DOSAGE

* Consider empiric coverage for <u>N. gonorrhoeae</u> with <u>ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE based on community <u>prevalence</u> and patient <u>risk factors</u>.





### Further management [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)




* If the patient tested positive for <u>chlamydia</u>, <u>gonorrhea</u>, and/or <u>trichomoniasis</u>, repeat testing in 3 months.

* For persistent symptoms or recurrent cervicitis:

* Evaluate for:

* New exposures (reinfection)

* Treatment nonadherence

* Treatment failure (check cultures for <u>antimicrobial resistance</u>).

* Consider <u>diagnostics for Mgen infection</u> and treat appropriately (see “<u>Treatment of Mgen infection</u>”).

* If no infectious cause is identified, refer to gynecology to evaluate for noninfectious causes (e.g., <u>cervical dysplasia</u>, polyps).
