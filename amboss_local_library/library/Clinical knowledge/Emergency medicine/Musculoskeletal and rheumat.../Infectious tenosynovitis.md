# Infectious tenosynovitis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Musculoskeletal and rheumatic disorders > Infectious tenosynovitis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LF0wi3)

---

## Summary

Infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u> is a closed infection within a <u>tendon sheath</u> that most commonly affects the <u>joints</u> of the hands. Causes include contiguous spread from adjacent structures, hematogenous spread, and direct infection from a <u>puncture wound</u> (e.g., human or <u>animal bite</u>, thorn prick injury). Gram-positive <u>skin</u> flora, specifically <u>Streptococcus</u> species and <u>Staphylococcus</u> species, are the most commonly involved pathogens, followed by gram-negative and anaerobic organisms. <u>Clinical features of infectious tenosynovitis</u> include swelling, <u>pain</u>, and <u>erythema</u> of the area around the involved <u>tendon</u>. In patients with hand involvement, presence of <u>Kanavel signs</u> on examination suggests infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u>. Diagnosis is based on clinical features and <u>patient history</u>. Laboratory tests and imaging can help confirm the diagnosis and assess severity. Infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u> is a <u>medical emergency</u>; therefore, management involves rapid identification and treatment with <u>broad-spectrum IV antibiotics</u>, hospital admission, and possible surgical intervention.




See also “<u>Noninfectious tenosynovitis</u>.”

## Etiology

### Microbial invasion of the <u>tendon sheath</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)




* Contiguous spread of infection from adjacent structures

* Direct infection from <u>puncture wounds</u>, e.g.,

* Human or <u>animal bites</u>

* Thorn prick injuries  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JQ1sCg0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MbdM8o0)

* IV drug use

* Hematogenous spread of infection





### Pathogens [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)




* <u>Gram-positive organisms</u> (most common)

* <u>Streptococcus</u> species

* <u>Staphylococcus</u> species including <u>MRSA</u>

* Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms

* <u>Eikenella corrodens</u>

* <u>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</u>

* <u>Pasteurella multocida</u> (specific to <u>cat bites</u>)

* <u>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</u>: in disseminated <u>gonococcal infections</u> (<u>DGI</u>)

* Other

* <u>Mycobacterium</u> species

* Fungi (e.g., <u>Sporothrix schenckii</u>)

## Clinical features

Infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u> most commonly presents in the <u>joints</u> of the hand. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ifdJN60)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QfduN60)




### Symptoms [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)




* Swelling

* <u>Pain</u>

* <u>Erythema</u>

* Central punctum or scratch

* <u>Fever</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lXdvyo0)





### Examination findings




* Kanavel signs: In patients with finger involvement, the presence of one or more signs suggests infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u> (i.e., <u>pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis</u>).  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0) 

* Holding the affected finger in a flexed position at rest

* Tenderness to palpation along the <u>tendon sheath</u>

* <u>Pain</u> with passive extension of the affected finger

* Fusiform swelling (> 95% of affected individuals) [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)

* Other findings: May be present based on underlying etiology

* <u>Clinical features of disseminated gonococcal infection</u> (<u>DGI</u>)

* <u>Signs of wound infection</u>

* Signs of extensor <u>tendon</u> damage in individuals with <u>dorsal</u> hand injuries (e.g., positive Elson test)  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)





> [!WARNING]
> The absence of one or more <u>Kanavel signs</u> does not exclude the diagnosis of <u>pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis</u>. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)




> [!TIP]
> <u>Kanavel signs</u> are sometimes less apparent in children or when the thumb or pinky are affected. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)

## Diagnosis

Infectious <u>tenosynovitis</u> is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>. Diagnostic studies may be obtained to confirm the diagnosis, identify the causative organism, and determine the extent of infection. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RXdlBo0)




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>: may show signs of infection, e.g., [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* <u>Leukocytosis</u>

* ↑ <u>Inflammatory markers</u>

* Microbiological studies

* <u>Blood culture</u>: for suspected hematogenous spread

* <u>Diagnostics for DGI</u>, if suspected

* Culture of <u>open wounds</u>

* Synovial culture or <u>biopsy</u>  may be necessary for definitive diagnosis. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QfduN60)

* Imaging [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ifdJN60)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QfduN60)

* <u>MRI</u> (preferred): findings include <u>tendon</u> inflammation, effusion, and/or synovial thickening [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ifdJN60)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QfduN60)

* <u>X-ray</u>: can identify bony involvement or foreign bodies

* Other imaging (e.g. <u>CT scan</u> or <u>ultrasound</u>) may be also obtained depending on suspected underlying etiology.

## Treatment

### Approach [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WZdP0o0)




* Initiate <u>broad-spectrum IV antibiotics</u> and admit to the hospital.

* Provide <u>supportive care</u> as needed.

* <u>Wound management</u>

* <u>Tetanus prophylaxis</u>, if indicated

* Splinting and elevation of the affected region

* Consult <u>surgery</u> immediately for [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)

* Advanced infection, e.g., ≥ 3 <u>Kanavel signs</u>, abnormal <u>neurovascular exam</u>, <u>clinical features of acute compartment syndrome</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/obd0uo0)

* No improvement 12–24 hours after initiation of IV <u>antibiotics</u>  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)





### <u>Antibiotic therapy</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)




* Initiate empiric <u>broad-spectrum IV antibiotics</u> based on clinical presentation and suspected organisms.  

* All patients: Include coverage for <u>gram-positive organisms</u> (including <u>MRSA</u>), e.g.,<u>vancomycin</u>. DOSAGE [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)

* <u>IVDU</u> or <u>immunocompromised</u>: Add coverage for gram-negative and anaerobic organisms (e.g., <u>piperacillin/tazobactam</u> DOSAGE). [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)

* Human or <u>animal bites</u>: See “<u>Antibiotics for bite wounds</u>.”

* <u>Risk factors</u> for <u>Pseudomonas</u> infection:  Add an <u>antipseudomonal antibiotic</u> (e.g., <u>meropenem</u> DOSAGE) [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZzcZrU0)

* Suspected <u>DGI</u>:  Add <u>ceftriaxone</u> DOSAGE (see also “<u>Treatment of DGI</u>”). [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2NcT010)

* Tailor <u>antibiotic therapy</u> to culture results, if available.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)

* For patients with <u>mycobacterial</u> or <u>fungal infections</u>: Consult infectious disease.





> [!TIP]
> Initiate <u>antibiotic</u> treatment as soon as possible to decrease the likelihood of serious complications. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)




### <u>Surgery</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XXd99o0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WXdPCo0)




* <u>Incision</u> with closed sheath catheter <u>irrigation</u>

* Open <u>irrigation</u> and <u>debridement</u> of <u>necrotic</u> and/or infected tissue

* <u>Amputation</u> may be required in severe cases. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/obd0uo0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KbdUuo0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Occupational therapy</u> is recommended after <u>surgery</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-qcDad0)
