# Occupational skin diseases

## Summary

Occupational <u>skin</u> diseases are contracted from exposure in the workplace and are the second most common employer-reported <u>occupational disorder</u>. The most common manifestations include <u>irritant contact dermatitis</u>, <u>occupational acne</u>, and <u>latex allergy</u>. Other <u>occupational diseases</u> covered elsewhere include work-related musculoskeletal disorders (e.g., <u>carpal tunnel syndrome</u>, <u>low back pain</u>), <u>asbestosis</u>, <u>mesothelioma</u>, and <u>silicosis</u>.

## Irritant contact dermatitis

* Occupational contact dermatitis is a localized inflammatory <u>skin</u> reaction resulting from exposure to substances at work [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5wWijn0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OpcIpW0)

* Manifestations include: [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5wWijn0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OpcIpW0)

* <u>Irritant contact dermatitis</u> (most common)

* <u>Allergic contact dermatitis</u>

* See "<u>Contact dermatitis</u>" for details.

## Latex allergy

A <u>type I hypersensitivity</u> or <u>type IV hypersensitivity</u> reaction to latex-based products (e.g., rubber gloves, <u>condoms</u>, balloons) [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)




### <u>Epidemiology</u>




* Affects 1–2% of the general population [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)

* Increased risk of <u>allergy</u> in individuals who have:

* <u>Atopy</u>, <u>eczema</u>, and hand dermatitis

* <u>Occupational exposure</u>, e.g., healthcare workers, hairdressers

* Multiple surgeries (e.g., patients with <u>spina bifida</u>)  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)





### Pathophysiology




* Immediate hypersensitivity (<u>type I HSR</u>): preformed <u>IgE antibodies</u> coating <u>mast cells</u> and <u>basophils</u> are crosslinked by contact with <u>antigen</u> (usually latex <u>allergens</u>) → cell <u>degranulation</u> → release of <u>histamine</u> and other inflammatory mediators.(e.g., <u>leukotrienes</u>, <u>prostaglandins</u>) → vasodilatation, increased <u>capillary</u> permeability, <u>smooth muscle</u> contraction, and inflammatory cell <u>chemotaxis</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qqXCz_)

* Delayed-type hypersensitivity (<u>type IV HSR</u>): contact of <u>antigens</u> (usually chemicals used during glove manufacturing such as benzothiazoles or amines) with presensitized <u>T lymphocytes</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IqXY-_)

* Presensitized <u>CD4+ T cells</u> recognize <u>antigens</u> on <u>antigen-presenting cells</u> → release of inflammatory <u>cytokines</u> → activation of <u>macrophages</u>

* Presensitized <u>CD8+ T cells</u> recognize <u>antigens</u> on somatic cells → cell-mediated cytotoxicity





### Clinical features




* <u>Urticaria</u> in the areas of contact

* <u>Rhinitis</u>, <u>asthma</u>  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Fddg7K0)

* <u>Anaphylactic shock</u> (in sensitized individuals)





### Diagnostics [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GddBrK0)




There is no standardized diagnostic algorithm to confirm <u>latex allergy</u>.




* Take a comprehensive history; consider screening questionnaires. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)

* Confirm the diagnosis with <u>laboratory studies</u> or <u>skin</u> testing.  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GddBrK0)

* Suspected <u>immediate hypersensitivity reaction</u> 

* Latex-specific serum Ig E testing

* <u>Skin prick testing</u>

* Suspected <u>delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction</u> : <u>patch testing</u>

* For patients with a suggestive history but negative <u>skin</u> and <u>antibody</u> tests, consider a provocation test.  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GddBrK0)





### Treatment [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GddBrK0)




* Treat the <u>allergic reaction</u> (e.g., see “<u>Anaphylaxis</u>,” “<u>Allergic contact dermatitis</u>,” and “<u>Allergic rhinitis</u>” as needed).

* Educate patients on <u>allergen</u> avoidance in the community.

* For hospitalized patients: Ensure latex-<u>allergy</u> status is documented on admission and the patient's environment remains latex-free.





### Prevention [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vxWAyn0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GddBrK0)




For individuals at the highest risk (e.g., patients with <u>spina bifida</u>), maintain a latex-free environment from <u>birth</u>.
