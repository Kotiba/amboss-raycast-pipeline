[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wS0hbf)

## Summary

<u>Myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u> (MI) refers to <u>ischemic necrosis</u> of <u>myocardial</u> tissue. The most common underlying cause is <u>coronary artery disease</u>. <u>Type 1 myocardial infarction</u> occurs when an unstable <u>plaque</u> ruptures, leading to occlusion of a <u>coronary artery</u>. <u>Type 2 myocardial infarction</u> occurs when there is a mismatch between oxygen supply and demand (due to, e.g., systemic <u>hypotension</u>, vasospasm). MI manifests clinically with <u>acute coronary syndrome</u> (<u>ACS</u>), a potentially lethal condition. Diagnosis is based on typical clinical features, <u>ECG</u> findings, and elevation of <u>cardiac biomarkers</u>. Definitive diagnosis requires <u>cardiac catheterization</u>, which serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. All patients suspected of having <u>ACS</u> should be considered for emergency <u>revascularization</u>; additional aspects of treatment include anticoagulation, <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>, <u>statin</u> therapy, and other adjunctive measures. Prevention of MI recurrence and complications consists of <u>dual antiplatelet therapy</u>, the initiation of <u>beta blockers</u> and/or <u>ACE inhibitors</u>, <u>statin</u> therapy, and addressing any <u>modifiable risk factors</u>.




The acute <u>management of ACS</u>, including diagnosis and treatment, is described in “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>.”

## Definitions

### <u>Myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u> (MI) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)




Defined as acute <u>myocardial injury</u> with clinical and diagnostic evidence of acute <u>ischemia</u>. MI is classified into 5 subtypes.




* Type 1 myocardial infarction: MI caused by <u>atherosclerotic plaque</u> disruption or acute coronary <u>thrombosis</u>

* Most common form

* Caused by acute <u>thrombosis</u> due to <u>erosion</u>, <u>ulceration</u>, fissuring, dissection, or rupture of an <u>atherosclerotic plaque</u>

* ↓ <u>Myocardial</u> blood flow → sudden <u>death</u> of <u>myocardial cells</u>

* Usually manifests as <u>STEMI</u>

* Type 2 myocardial infarction: MI secondary to an oxygen supply/demand mismatch [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xub9pv)

* Less common form (14%)

* Occurs predominantly in women and in individuals with comorbidities (e.g., <u>diabetes</u>, previous <u>NSTEMI</u>)

* Not due to <u>plaque</u> rupture; caused by a condition other than <u>coronary artery disease</u>

* <u>Ischemia</u> is caused by increased oxygen demand (e.g., <u>anemia</u>) or decreased coronary blood supply (e.g., <u>coronary artery spasm</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ALYRZJ)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_LY5ZJ)

* Type 3 <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>: MI resulting in <u>death</u> when biomarker values are unavailable

* Type 4 <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>: MI related to <u>percutaneous coronary intervention</u>

* Type 4a: MI ≤ 48 hours after <u>PCI</u>

* Type 4b: MI related to <u>stent thrombosis</u>

* Type 4c: MI associated with restenosis after <u>PCI</u>

* Type 5 <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>: MI related to <u>coronary artery bypass grafting</u>





### <u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>




* Suspicion or confirmed presence of acute <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u> and/or <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>

* Further classified as <u>unstable angina</u>, <u>NSTEMI</u>, and <u>STEMI</u>

* See “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>.”





### Myocardial injury




* <u>Cardiac troponin</u> (cTn) level elevated above the 99<sup>th</sup><u>percentile</u> of the upper reference limit (URL) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)

* Acute <u>myocardial injury</u>: rise and/or fall of cTn levels in sequential measurements

* Damage may be <u>ischemic</u> or nonischemic.

## Etiology

Any condition that causes occlusion of the <u>coronary arteries</u>, reduces <u>myocardial</u> oxygen supply, or increases oxygen demand can potentially lead to <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u> and <u>infarction</u>.




* <u>Coronary artery disease</u>: most common cause (see “<u>Risk factors for atherosclerosis</u>”)

* <u>Coronary artery</u> vasospasm (e.g., <u>Prinzmetal angina</u>, <u>cocaine</u> use)

* <u>Coronary artery dissection</u> (e.g., <u>SCAD</u>)

* <u>Coronary artery</u> embolism (e.g., due to <u>prosthetic heart valve</u>, <u>atrial fibrillation</u>)

* <u>Takotsubo cardiomyopathy</u>

* <u>Myocarditis</u>

* <u>Thrombophilia</u> (e.g., <u>polycythemia vera</u>)

* <u>Vasculitis</u> (e.g., <u>polyarteritis nodosa</u>, <u>Kawasaki syndrome</u>)

* <u>Myocardial</u> oxygen supply-demand mismatch, e.g., due to the following:

* <u>Hypotension</u>

* <u>Severe anemia</u>

* <u>Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy</u>

* <u>Severe aortic stenosis</u>

## Pathophysiology

### <u>Coronary artery</u> occlusion [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p30Ljf)




* Partial <u>coronary artery</u> occlusion

* Decreased <u>myocardial</u> blood flow → supply-demand mismatch → <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u>

* Usually affects the inner layer of the <u>myocardium</u> (subendocardial <u>infarction</u>)

* Typically manifests clinically as <u>unstable angina</u> and/or <u>NSTEMI</u> (see “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>”)

* Complete <u>coronary artery</u> occlusion

* Impaired <u>myocardial</u> blood flow → sudden <u>death</u> of <u>myocardial cells</u> (if no reperfusion occurs)

* Usually affects the full thickness of the <u>myocardium</u> (<u>transmural</u> <u>infarction</u>)

* Typically manifests clinically as <u>STEMI</u>





![Subendocardial and transmural myocardial infarction](../../../../media/h5yrq3yvkrdsm8fyzpyue74q.jpg)

Subendocardial and transmural myocardial infarction




### <u>Atherosclerotic plaque</u> disruption (<u>type 1 MI</u>) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/p30Ljf)




* For <u>plaque</u> formation, see “<u>Coronary artery disease</u>” and “<u>Atherosclerosis</u>.”

* Stable <u>atherosclerotic plaque</u>: manifests as <u>stable angina</u> (symptomatic during exertion)

* Unstable <u>plaques</u> are lipid-rich and covered by thin fibrous caps : high risk of rupture and <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>

* Inflammatory cells in the <u>plaque</u> (e.g., <u>macrophages</u>) secrete matrix <u>metalloproteinases</u> → breakdown of extracellular matrix → weakening of the fibrous cap → minor <u>stress</u> → rupture of the fibrous cap → exposure of highly thrombogenic lipid core → <u>thrombus formation</u> → <u>coronary artery</u> occlusion

### Oxygen supply and demand mismatch (<u>type 2 MI</u>) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xub9pv)




* Can occur in patients with or without underlying <u>coronary artery disease</u>

* Decreased oxygen supply

* Occlusion of <u>coronary arteries</u> (e.g., coronary dissection, vasospasm)

* Reduced <u>perfusion</u> (e.g., <u>hypotension</u>, <u>bradycardia</u>, <u>anemia</u>)

* Increased oxygen demand (e.g., sustained <u>tachyarrhythmia</u>)





### Nonischemic <u>myocardial injury</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xub9pv)




* <u>Necrosis</u> of <u>myocardial</u> tissue without <u>ischemia</u> (e.g., in <u>sepsis</u>)

* The pathophysiology of <u>myocardial</u> damage is not completely understood, but potential explanations include:

* Inflammatory <u>cytokines</u>

* Toxicity of high <u>catecholamine</u> levels

## Clinical features

* Classic presentation  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* Acute retrosternal <u>chest pain</u> 

* Typical: dull, squeezing pressure and/or tightness

* Commonly radiates to left chest, arm, shoulder, neck, <u>jaw</u>, and/or epigastrium

* Precipitated by exertion or <u>stress</u>

* Symptom relief after administration of <u>nitrates</u> is not a diagnostic criterion for cardiac <u>ischemia</u>.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)

* The peak time of occurrence is usually in the morning.

* See “<u>Angina</u>.”

* <u>Dyspnea</u> (especially with exertion)

* <u>Pallor</u>

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Diaphoresis</u>, <u>anxiety</u>

* <u>Dizziness</u>, <u>lightheadedness</u>, <u>syncope</u>




* Other findings

* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>

* Symptoms of <u>CHF</u> (e.g., <u>orthopnea</u>, <u>pulmonary edema</u>) or <u>cardiogenic shock</u> (e.g., <u>hypotension</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, cold extremities)

* New <u>heart murmur</u> on <u>auscultation</u> (e.g., new <u>S<sub>4</sub></u>)

* Atypical presentations: more likely in elderly, diabetic individuals, and women ;  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T0b62H)

* Stabbing, sharp <u>chest pain</u>

* No or minimal <u>chest pain</u>
* ”Silent MI” without <u>chest pain</u> is more common in patients with <u>diabetes</u>, as a result of <u>polyneuropathy</u>.

* Autonomic symptoms (e.g., <u>nausea</u>, <u>diaphoresis</u>)

* More common in inferior wall <u>infarction</u>

* Epigastric <u>pain</u>

* <u>Bradycardia</u>

* Clinical triad in <u>right ventricular</u> <u>infarction</u>: <u>hypotension</u>, elevated <u>jugular venous pressure</u>, clear <u>lung</u> fields [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tbYXvn)





> [!NOTE]
> Classically, it has been taught that <u>STEMI</u> manifests with more severe symptoms than <u>NSTEMI</u>, but this is not always the case.

## Diagnosis

Follow <u>ACS</u> protocols if acute <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u> is suspected (see “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>”). Diagnosis is based on typical clinical features, <u>ECG</u> findings, and elevation of <u>cardiac biomarkers</u>.




### <u>ECG</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)




* A 12-lead is the initial test in every patient with suspected <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u>.

* Findings include pathological <u>Q waves</u>, <u>ST-segment</u> shifts, and <u>T-wave inversions</u> (see “<u>ECG findings in ACS</u>”).

* Dynamic changes require serial <u>ECG</u> evaluation.

* Compare to prior <u>ECGs</u> (if available).

> [!NOTE]
> <u>Infarction</u> of the <u>anterior</u> wall is caused by obstruction of the <u>LAD</u> or its branches. #### Modified Sgarbossa criteria  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T0b62H)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/S0by2H)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c0baUH)




* A set of <u>ECG</u> criteria that can help identify <u>STEMI</u> in patients with <u>LBBB</u> and high clinical suspicion of <u>ACS</u>.

* The criteria can also be used in right-ventricular pacing with <u>LBBB</u> configuration but are less specific in this scenario.

* Acute <u>STEMI</u> is likely if any of the following are present: 

* <u>Concordant</u> <u>ST elevation</u> of ≥ 1 mm in any lead with a positive QRS

* <u>Concordant</u> <u>ST depression</u> of ≥ 1 mm in any of leads V<sub>1</sub>–V<sub>3</sub>

* Proportionally excessive discordant <u>ST elevation</u> (<u>ST elevation</u> ≥ 25% of the depth of the S wave, i.e., ST/S <u>ratio</u> ≤ - 0.25) in at least one lead with <u>ST elevation</u> ≥ 1 mm [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c0baUH)





> [!TIP]
> Positive <u>modified Sgarbossa criteria</u> can help identify <u>STEMI</u> in symptomatic patients with <u>LBBB</u> for whom <u>ST-segment</u> assessment is difficult.




#### Classical timeline of <u>ECG</u> changes after <u>STEMI</u>




* Acute stage: <u>myocardial</u> damage ongoing

* Hyperacute <u>T waves</u> (<u>peaked T wave</u>)

* <u>ST elevations</u> in two contiguous leads with reciprocal <u>ST depressions</u>

* Intermediate stage: <u>myocardial</u> <u>necrosis</u> present

* Absence of R wave

* <u>T-wave inversions</u>

* Pathological <u>Q waves</u>  [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J_ds6s0)

* Duration ≥ 0.04 seconds

* Amplitude ≥ ¼ of the R wave or ≥ 0.1 mV

* Any <u>Q wave</u> in leads V1–3

* Chronic stage: permanent scarring

* Persistent, broad, and deep <u>Q waves</u>

* Often incomplete recovery of R waves

* Permanent <u>T-wave inversion</u> is possible.





> [!TIP]
> The sequence of <u>ECG</u> changes over several hours to days: hyperacute <u>T wave</u> → <u>ST elevation</u> → pathological <u>Q wave</u> → <u>T-wave inversion</u> → ST normalization → <u>T-wave</u> normalization




### Cardiac biomarkers [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nab7OH)




* <u>Cardiac troponin</u> (cTn) is the most important biological marker of <u>myocardial</u> <u>necrosis</u>.

* Acute <u>myocardial injury</u>: change in <u>cardiac troponin</u> levels in sequential measurements

* <u>Myocardial injury</u>: <u>cardiac troponin</u> elevation above the 99<sup>th</sup><u>percentile</u> of the upper reference limit (URL) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DLY1-p)

* All <u>cardiac biomarkers</u> require clinical context for interpretation (e.g., <u>troponin</u> can also be elevated in other cardiac and noncardiac conditions; see “<u>Differential diagnosis of increased troponin</u>”).

* Values and time references may vary based on the precise <u>laboratory methods</u> employed.

### Additional <u>laboratory studies</u> [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RoYlbJ)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ioYJbJ)




* <u>CBC</u> and <u>markers of inflammation</u>

* <u>Hemoglobin</u>: to evaluate for <u>anemia</u>

* <u>Platelets</u>: to evaluate for <u>thrombocytopenia</u>

* Elevated <u>inflammatory markers</u>: ↑ <u>WBC</u>, <u>CRP</u>

* Serum chemistries

* <u>Basic metabolic panel</u>: to evaluate for renal dysfunction and <u>electrolyte</u> abnormalities

* <u>LDH</u> and <u>AST</u>: may be elevated due to cell <u>necrosis</u>

* <u>BNP</u> or <u>NT-proBNP</u>: may be elevated, especially in concurrent <u>heart failure</u>  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)

* <u>Coagulation panel</u>: to evaluate for baseline coagulopathies

* <u>Urine toxicology screening</u>: Consider in suspected use of <u>cocaine</u> or <u>methamphetamines</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)

* Acute <u>STEMI</u>

* <u>NSTE-ACS</u> with timing depending on <u>risk factors</u> (see also "<u>Invasive strategy for NSTE-ACS</u>")

* See also “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>” and “<u>Cardiac catheterization</u>.”

### Cellular changes




* See “<u>Ischemia</u>” in “<u>Cellular changes and adaptive responses</u>.”

* <u>Reperfusion injury</u>

* Timing

* Can occur spontaneously or after <u>revascularization</u> (e.g., <u>fibrinolysis</u> or <u>PCI</u>)

* Typically occurs when reperfusion occurs > 3 hours after the acute <u>coronary artery</u> occlusion

* Mechanism: : blood flow restored → damaged <u>myocytes</u> release <u>reactive oxygen species</u> (<u>ROS</u>);  → <u>mitochondrial</u> permeability transition pores are formed → cell swelling → <u>cell death</u> → <u>Ca<sup>2+</sup></u> entry into the <u>cytosol</u> → hypercontraction of <u>myocytes</u> → contraction band <u>necrosis</u> and increase in <u>infarct</u> size [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gk0FMT)

* Microscopic findings: neutrophilic infiltration, <u>capillary</u> obstruction, and contraction band <u>necrosis</u> of the <u>myocardium</u>

## Treatment

This section provides an overview of the most important treatment aspects of <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>. See “<u>Acute coronary syndrome</u>” for more detailed management.




> [!WARNING]
> Any patient with <u>ST elevations</u> on <u>ECG</u> requires immediate evaluation for urgent <u>revascularization</u>. The administration of other therapies should not delay care.




### Critical management [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)




See "<u>Management of ACS</u>" for further details.




* <u>Revascularization</u>: all patients with suspected <u>acute coronary syndrome</u> should be considered for emergency <u>percutaneous coronary intervention</u> (<u>PCI</u>)

* Monitoring

* Serial 12-lead <u>ECG</u>

* <u>Continuous cardiac monitoring</u>

* Serial serum <u>troponin</u> measurement

* <u>Antiplatelet therapy</u> and anticoagulation: See "<u>Antiplatelet agents</u> for <u>ACS</u>" and "<u>Anticoagulation for ACS</u>" for more details.

* <u>Dual antiplatelet therapy</u> (<u>DAPT</u>)

* <u>Aspirin</u>

* PLUS;  <u>ADP receptor inhibitor</u>: <u>prasugrel</u>, <u>ticagrelor</u>, or <u>clopidogrel</u>

* Anticoagulation: <u>unfractionated heparin</u> or <u>LMWH</u>

* Management of acute <u>complications of myocardial infarction</u>

* <u>Management of bradycardia</u>

* <u>Management of tachycardia</u>

* <u>Management of acute heart failure</u>

* <u>Management of cardiogenic shock</u>





### Adjunctive therapy [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)




* Oxygen: only if <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> is < 90% [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)

* Sublingual or intravenous <u>nitrate</u> (<u>nitroglycerin</u>)

* For symptomatic relief of <u>chest pain</u>

* Does not improve prognosis

* Contraindications: inferior wall <u>infarct</u> (due to risk for <u>hypotension</u>), <u>hypotension</u>, and/or <u>PDE5 inhibitor</u> (e.g., <u>sildenafil</u>) taken within last 24 hours

* <u>Morphine</u> IV or SQ

* Indicated for severe, persistent <u>chest pain</u> unresponsive to <u>antianginal medication</u>

* Administer with caution due to the increased risk of complications (e.g., <u>hypotension</u>, respiratory <u>depression</u>) and <u>adverse events</u>.

* <u>Beta blockers</u> (avoid in patients with <u>hypotension</u>, features of <u>acute heart failure</u>, and/or risk of <u>cardiogenic shock</u>): Start within 24 hours.

* <u>Statins</u>: early initiation of high-intensity <u>statin</u> (e.g., <u>atorvastatin</u>) regardless of baseline <u>cholesterol</u>, <u>LDL</u>, and <u>HDL</u> levels

* <u>ACE inhibitors</u>/<u>ARBs</u>: Initiate in stable patients with any of the following 

* <u>Anterior</u> <u>STEMI</u>

* <u>LVEF</u> ≤ 40%

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Aldosterone antagonists</u> (e.g., <u>eplerenone</u>): Initiate in patients with <u>LVEF</u> ≤ 40% and any of the following

* <u>Heart failure</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>





> [!TIP]
> Options for initial MI treatment include “MONA-BASH”: Morphine, Oxygen, Nitroglycerin, Antiplatelet drugs (<u>aspirin</u> + <u>ADP receptor inhibitor</u>), Beta blockers, ACE inhibitors, Statins, and Heparin. The scope of interventions depends on the patient's risk profile.




### Prevention of recurrent myocardial infarction [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/b1dH2K0)




Further management includes managing <u>risk factors</u> to prevent recurrence; see also "<u>Management of ASCVD</u>."




#### General principles [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mycVfU0)




* Ensure adherence to <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> to prevent of <u>thrombosis</u> and restenosis.

* Manage <u>ASCVD</u> and any comorbidities (e.g., <u>treatment of hypertension</u>, <u>management of diabetes mellitus</u>).

* Refer for <u>cardiac rehabilitation</u>.

* <u>Screen for tobacco product use</u>; assist with <u>smoking cessation</u> if indicated.

* See "Lifestyle modifications for <u>ASCVD prevention</u>" for more information on nonpharmacological interventions.

* See "<u>Management of CAD</u>" for more details on the management of patients with <u>chronic coronary disease</u>.





#### Pharmacological treatment after <u>ACS</u>




* Lipid management in the first 12 months post-ACS

* All patients should be started on a high-intensity <u>statin</u> (e.g., <u>atorvastatin</u>).[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)

* <u>LDL</u>-C â¥ 70 mg/dL: Add a <u>nonstatin lipid-lowering agent</u>.

* <u>LDL</u>-C 55–69 mg/dL: Consider adding a <u>nonstatin lipid-lowering agent</u>.

* <u>LDL</u>-C < 55 mg/dL: Continue high-intensity <u>statin</u>.

* Reassess <u>lipid profile</u> 4–8 weeks after discharge for therapy adjustment.

* See “<u>Lipid-lowering therapy for ASCVD</u>” for further details, including agents and dosages.

* See "<u>Management of CAD</u>" for more details on <u>LDL</u>-C goals beyond the first 12 months post-<u>ACS</u>.

* <u>Antiplatelet therapy</u> in the 12 months post-<u>ACS</u> [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mycVfU0)

* See “<u>Antiplatelet therapy for ACS</u>” for dosages.

* Choose a regimen and duration based on bleeding risk in consultation with cardiology.

* Default for patients who do not have a high bleeding risk: <u>DAPT</u> is for at least 12 months

* Patients with high bleeding risk (e.g., individuals with <u>thrombocytopenia</u> or <u>cirrhosis</u>): <u>DAPT</u> for 1 month, then either <u>aspirin</u> or a <u>P2Y12 inhibitor</u>

* Patients who require <u>oral anticoagulant therapy</u>: <u>DAPT</u> plus an <u>oral anticoagulant</u> for 1–4 weeks, then <u>clopidogrel</u> and an <u>oral anticoagulant</u>

* Bleeding risk reduction

* Preferred: <u>aspirin</u> plus <u>ticagrelor</u> for 1–3 months, then <u>ticagrelor</u> monotherapy

* Alternative: <u>aspirin</u> plus either <u>ticagrelor</u> or <u>prasugrel</u> for 1 month; then <u>aspirin</u> plus <u>clopidogrel</u>

* Adjunctive: <u>Proton pump inhibitor</u> for patients at high risk of <u>gastrointestinal bleeding</u>

* See "<u>Management of CAD</u>" for more details on <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> beyond 12 months (e.g., lifelong <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>) post-<u>ACS</u>.

* <u>Beta blockers</u>

* Unless contraindicated, all patients should be started on a <u>beta blocker</u>, which has been shown to confer a mortality benefit.

* After one year, consider discontinuation of <u>beta blockers</u> if there are no other indications (e.g., current or former <u>LVEF</u> ≤ 50%, <u>arrhythmias</u>, <u>angina</u>, or <u>hypertension</u>). [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/b1dH2K0)

* Other cardioprotective drugs as indicated: e.g., <u>ACE inhibitors</u>/<u>ARBs</u>, <u>aldosterone antagonists</u>, <u>SGLT-2 inhibitors</u> (see "<u>Pharmacotherapy for heart failure</u>" for more details and dosages)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Ticagrelor</u> or <u>prasugrel</u> are preferred over <u>clopidogrel</u> during the first 12 months in patients who have undergone <u>PCI</u>.




#### Advanced therapies [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)




* <u>Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator</u> indications

* <u>LVEF</u> ≤ 40%

* Clinically relevant <u>ventricular arrhythmias</u> > 48 hours after reperfusion

* <u>Permanent pacemaker</u> indications

* <u>Mobitz type II AV block</u>

* <u>High-grade AV block</u>

* Alternating bundle-branch block

* <u>Third-degree AV block</u>

### 0–24 hours post-<u>infarction</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9A0NNi)




* Sudden cardiac death (SCD)

* Definition: A sudden <u>death</u> presumably caused by <u>cardiac arrhythmia</u> or hemodynamic catastrophe, which occurs either within an hour of symptom onset in patients with cardiovascular symptoms, or within 24 hours of being asymptomatic in patients with no cardiovascular symptoms. [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QDYuVr)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xBYE17)

* Pathophysiology: Fatal <u>ventricular arrhythmia</u> is considered to be the underlying mechanism of <u>SCD</u>. [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PWbWls)

* Underlying conditions

* <u>Coronary artery disease</u>: present in ∼ 70% of cases in adults over 35 years [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4Wb3ls)

* Dilated/<u>hypertrophic cardiomyopathy</u>

* Hereditary ion channelopathies (e.g., <u>long QT syndrome</u>, <u>Brugada syndrome</u>)

* Prevention: installation of the implantable <u>cardioverter-defibrillator</u> device [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PWbWls)

* <u>Arrhythmias</u>: a common <u>cause of death</u> in MI patients in the first 24 hours  

* <u>Ventricular tachyarrhythmias</u>

* <u>Sinus bradycardia</u>

* <u>Atrioventricular block</u> (e.g., <u>complete heart block</u>)

* Conduction blocks

* <u>Asystole</u>

* <u>Atrial fibrillation</u>

* Acute <u>left heart failure</u>: <u>death</u> of affected <u>myocardium</u> → absence of <u>myocardial</u> contraction → <u>pulmonary edema</u>

* <u>Cardiogenic shock</u>

### 1–3 days post-<u>infarction</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9A0NNi)



* Early infarct-associated pericarditis

* Typically occurs within the first week of a large <u>infarct</u> close to the <u>pericardium</u>

* Clinical features of <u>acute pericarditis</u>, including:

* Friction rub

* <u>Pleuritic chest pain</u> , dry <u>cough</u>

* Diffuse <u>ST elevations</u> on <u>ECG</u>

* <u>Pericardial effusion</u>

* Treatment: <u>supportive care</u> (e.g., <u>acetaminophen</u>)  [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)

* Complications (rare): <u>hemopericardium</u>, <u>pericardial tamponade</u>

* Prognosis: usually <u>self-limiting</u>

* Prevention: early coronary reperfusion therapy




### 3–14 days post-<u>infarction</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9A0NNi)




* Papillary muscle rupture  

* Usually occurs 2–7 days after <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>

* Can lead to acute <u>mitral regurgitation</u>

* Location

* More often: posteromedial <u>papillary muscle rupture</u> due to occlusion of the <u>posterior descending artery</u> (single supply)

* Less often: anterolateral <u>papillary muscle rupture</u> due to occlusion of <u>LAD</u> and/or LCx (double supply)

* Clinical features

* New <u>holosystolic</u>, blowing <u>murmur</u> over the 5<sup>th</sup><u>ICS</u> on the <u>midclavicular line</u>

* Signs of acute <u>mitral regurgitation</u>: <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>cough</u>, bilateral <u>crackles</u>, <u>hypotension</u>

* Complications: <u>Mitral regurgitation</u> can lead to severe <u>pulmonary edema</u> and/or <u>cardiogenic shock</u>.

* Ventricular septal rupture

* Usually occurs 3–5 days after <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>

* Pathophysiology: macrophagic degradation of the septum → ventricular septal defect → blood flow from LV to RV following the pressure gradient (<u>left-to-right shunt</u>) → ↑ pressure in RV and ↑ O<sub>2</sub> content in the venous blood

* Most commonly due to <u>LAD</u> <u>infarction</u> (septal <u>arteries</u> arise from <u>LAD</u>)

* Clinical features

* New <u>holosystolic murmur</u> over the left sternal border

* Acute-onset right <u>heart</u> failure (<u>jugular venous distention</u>, <u>peripheral edema</u>)

* Complications: can progress to <u>cardiogenic shock</u> and severe <u>pulmonary edema</u>

* Left ventricular free wall rupture   

* Usually occurs 5–14 days after <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>

* Greatest risk during <u>macrophage</u>-mediated removal of <u>necrotic</u> tissue

* <u>LV hypertrophy</u> and tissue <u>fibrosis</u> from previous MI decrease the risk of <u>free wall rupture</u>.

* Clinical features

* <u>Chest pain</u>

* <u>Dyspnea</u>

* Signs of <u>cardiac tamponade</u> (e.g., <u>Beck triad</u>)

* Complications: <u>cardiac tamponade</u>;  , <u>sudden cardiac death</u> (if the rupture occurs acutely)

* Contained ventricular rupture (<u>pseudoaneurysm</u>): refers to the outpouching of the ventricular wall rupture that is contained by either the <u>pericardium</u>, a <u>thrombus</u>, or <u>scar</u> tissue 

* Usually occurs 3–14 days after <u>myocardial</u> <u>infarction</u>

* Clinical features

* Can be asymptomatic

* If symptomatic

* New <u>heart murmur</u>

* <u>Chest pain</u>

* <u>Heart failure</u>

* <u>Syncope</u>

* Complications

* Rupture → <u>cardiac tamponade</u> (risk of rupture is higher than in a <u>true aneurysm</u>)

* Associated with mural <u>thromboembolism</u>, decreased <u>cardiac output</u>, and increased risk of <u>arrhythmia</u>

### 2 weeks to months post-<u>infarction</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U30b3f)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9A0NNi)




* Atrial and <u>ventricular aneurysms</u>

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: affects 10–20% of patients

* Clinical features

* Persistent (> 3 weeks post-MI) <u>ST elevation</u> and <u>T-wave</u> inversions

* Systolic <u>murmur</u>, <u>S<sub>3</sub></u> and/or <u>S<sub>4</sub></u>

* New <u>heart murmur</u>, <u>chest pain</u>, <u>heart failure</u>, and/or <u>syncope</u>

* Diagnosis: <u>echocardiography</u> 

* Visualization of the pathological <u>myocardial</u> wall protrusion

* Detection of dyskinetic movements of the thinned <u>aneurysmal</u> wall (uncoordinated contraction occurs due to <u>fibrotic</u> changes of the <u>myocardium</u>)

* Complications

* <u>Cardiac arrhythmias</u> (risk of <u>ventricular fibrillation</u>)

* Rupture → <u>cardiac tamponade</u>

* <u>Left ventricular thrombus</u>

* Treatment

* Anticoagulation if <u>LV thrombus</u> is present

* Possibly <u>surgery</u>

* See “<u>Ventricular aneurysm</u>” for details.

* Postmyocardial infarction syndrome (<u>Dressler syndrome</u>): <u>pericarditis</u> occurring 2–10 weeks post-MI without an infective cause 

* Pathophysiology: thought to be due to circulating <u>antibodies</u> against <u>cardiac muscle cells</u> (autoimmune etiology) → <u>immune complex</u> deposition → inflammation

* Clinical features

* Signs of <u>acute pericarditis</u>: <u>pleuritic chest pain</u>;  , dry <u>cough</u> ;  , friction rub

* <u>Fever</u>

* Laboratory findings: <u>leukocytosis</u>, ↑ serum <u>troponin</u> levels

* <u>ECG</u>: diffuse <u>ST elevations</u>

* High-dose <u>aspirin</u>;  (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGEuntil symptoms improve and <u>colchicine</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE for 3 months [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)

* Avoid <u>NSAIDs</u> (other than <u>aspirin</u>) and <u>glucocorticoids</u> post-MI because of the increased risk of reinfarction. [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qGdCZ70)

* Complications (rare): <u>hemopericardium</u>, <u>pericardial tamponade</u>

* <u>Mural thrombus</u> formation → <u>thromboembolism</u> (<u>stroke</u>, <u>mesenteric</u> <u>ischemia</u>, <u>renal infarction</u>, acute obstruction of peripheral <u>arteries</u>)   [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bpWHLm0)

* <u>Arrhythmias</u>

* <u>Congestive heart failure</u> (e.g., due to <u>ischemic cardiomyopathy</u>)

* Can occur at any time after an <u>ischemic</u> event

* Treatment: for patients with <u>LVEF</u> < 40% or <u>signs of heart failure</u>, <u>ACE inhibitor</u>/<u>ARB</u> and <u>aldosterone antagonists</u> have been shown to confer a mortality benefit.

* Reinfarction
