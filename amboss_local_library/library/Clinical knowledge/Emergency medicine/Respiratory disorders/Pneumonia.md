## Summary

Pneumonia is a respiratory infection characterized by inflammation of the alveolar space and/or the <u>interstitial</u> tissue of the <u>lungs</u>. In industrialized nations, it is the leading infectious <u>cause of death</u>. Pneumonia is most commonly transmitted via <u>aspiration</u> of airborne pathogens (primarily bacteria, but also <u>viruses</u> and fungi) but may also result from the <u>aspiration</u> of <u>stomach</u> contents. The most likely causal pathogens can be narrowed down based on patient age, immune status, and where the infection was acquired (community-acquired or hospital-acquired). Pneumonia is classified based on clinical features as either typical or atypical; each type has its own spectrum of commonly associated pathogens. <u>Typical pneumonia</u> manifests with sudden onset of <u>malaise</u>, <u>fever</u>, and a productive <u>cough</u>. On <u>auscultation</u>, <u>crackles</u> and bronchial breath sounds are audible. <u>Atypical pneumonia</u> manifests with gradual onset of unproductive <u>cough</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>, and extrapulmonary manifestations. <u>Auscultation</u> is usually unremarkable. Some patients may present with elements of both types. Diagnostics include blood tests for <u>inflammatory parameters</u> and <u>pathogen</u> detection in blood, urine, or <u>sputum</u> samples. <u>Chest x-ray</u> in cases of <u>typical pneumonia</u> shows opacity restricted to one lobe, while <u>x-ray</u> in <u>atypical pneumonia</u> may show diffuse, often subtle infiltrates. Together with the characteristic clinical features, newly developed pulmonary infiltrate on <u>chest x-ray</u> confirms the diagnosis. Management consists of <u>empiric antibiotic treatment</u> and supportive measures (e.g., oxygen administration, <u>antipyretics</u>).




For specific information on the diagnosis and <u>management of pneumonia</u> in pediatric patients, see “<u>Pneumonia in children</u>.”

### <u>Risk factors</u> [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)




* Old age and immobility of any cause

* Chronic diseases

* Preexisting cardiopulmonary conditions (e.g., <u>bronchial asthma</u>, <u>COPD</u>, <u>heart failure</u>)

* Acquired or congenital abnormalities of the <u>airways</u> (e.g., <u>bronchiectasis</u>, space-occupying lesions, <u>cystic fibrosis</u>)

* <u>Immunosuppression</u>

* <u>HIV infection</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* Cytostatic and <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u>

* <u>Alcoholism</u>

* <u>Malnutrition</u>

* Impaired <u>airway</u> protection 

* Alteration in consciousness (e.g., due to <u>stroke</u>, <u>seizure</u>, anesthesia, drugs, <u>alcohol</u>)

* <u>Dysphagia</u>

* Smoking

* Environmental factors

* Crowded living conditions (e.g., prisons, homeless shelters)

* Toxins (e.g., solvents, gasoline)

* <u>Endemic</u> exposures (e.g., areas of high <u>Coccidioides</u> and <u>Histoplasma</u> endemicity)

* Contaminated water systems (e.g., in hotels, on cruise ships)

* <u>Zoonotic</u> exposures (e.g., birds, farm animals)

* <u>Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia</u>

* Specific medications;  (e.g., <u>amiodarone</u>, <u>bleomycin</u>) [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nlX7xy)

* Chronic inflammatory disorders (e.g., <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>)

* Surgical procedures 

* Upper abdominal <u>surgery</u>

* Chest <u>surgery</u>





> [!TIP]
> Bear in mind immune status and potential exposures when considering potential pathogens in patients with suspected pneumonia.




> [!TIP]
> Consider <u>aspiration pneumonia</u> in patients with <u>altered mental status</u> or other <u>risk factors for aspiration</u>.

## Classification

Pneumonia can be classified according to etiology, location acquired, clinical features, and the area of the <u>lung</u> affected by the pathology.




### Etiology




* Primary pneumonia: no apparent preexisting conditions that may predispose to pneumonia

* Secondary pneumonia

* <u>Bronchial asthma</u>, <u>COPD</u>, <u>heart failure</u>, <u>cystic fibrosis</u>

* Viral <u>upper respiratory tract infections</u> with bacterial <u>superinfection</u>

* Anatomical abnormalities such as tubercular caverns, <u>bronchial tumors</u>, or stenosis (<u>postobstructive pneumonia</u>) [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nr0-hh)

* <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u>





### Location acquired




* Community-acquired pneumonia (<u>CAP</u>): pneumonia that is acquired outside of a healthcare establishment

* Hospital-acquired pneumonia (<u>HAP</u>): pneumonia onset > 48 hours after a patient is admitted to a hospital without evidence of disease at the time of admission [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)
* Ventilator-associated pneumonia (<u>VAP</u>): pneumonia occurring in patients who are on <u>mechanical ventilation</u> breathing machines in hospitals (typically in the <u>intensive care unit</u>)

* Healthcare-associated pneumonia (<u>HCAP</u>): A historical term for pneumonia acquired in <u>healthcare facilities</u>; this terminology is no longer in use.





### Clinical features




* <u>Typical pneumonia</u>

* Pneumonia featuring classic symptoms (typical findings on <u>auscultation</u> and percussion)

* Manifests as <u>lobar pneumonia</u> or <u>bronchopneumonia</u>

* <u>Atypical pneumonia</u>

* Pneumonia with less distinct classical symptoms and often unremarkable findings on <u>auscultation</u> and percussion

* Manifests as <u>interstitial pneumonia</u>





### Area of <u>lung</u> affected by the pathology




* <u>Lobar pneumonia</u>: pneumonia affecting one lobe of a <u>lung</u>

* Multilobar pneumonia refers to the involvement of multiple lobes in a single <u>lung</u> or both <u>lungs</u>.

* Panlobar pneumonia involves all the lobes of a single <u>lung</u>.

* Bronchial pneumonia: pneumonia affecting the tissue around the <u>bronchi</u> and/or <u>bronchioles</u>

* <u>Interstitial pneumonia</u>: pneumonia affecting the tissue between the <u>alveoli</u>

* <u>Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia</u> (formerly known as <u>bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia</u>): a noninfectious pneumonia of unknown etiology characterized by the involvement of the <u>bronchioles</u>, <u>alveoli</u>, and surrounding tissue

## Pathophysiology

### Routes of infection




* Most common: microaspiration (droplet infection) of airborne pathogens or oropharyngeal secretions

* <u>Aspiration</u> of <u>gastric acid</u> (<u>aspiration pneumonitis</u>) , food, or liquids

* Hematogenous dissemination (rare)





### Pathogenesis



1. * Failure of protective pulmonary mechanisms;  (e.g., <u>cough reflex</u>, <u>mucociliary clearance</u> , <u>alveolar macrophages</u> )
2. * Infiltration of the <u>pulmonary parenchyma</u> by the <u>pathogen</u> → <u>interstitial</u> and alveolar inflammation
3. * Impaired <u>alveolar ventilation</u> → ventilation/<u>perfusion</u> <u>(V/Q) mismatch</u> with intrapulmonary shunting (right to left)
4. * <u>Hypoxia</u> due to increased <u>alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient</u> 

* <u>Hypoxia</u> is worsened when the affected <u>lung</u> is in the <u>dependent position</u>, as <u>perfusion</u> to the dependent <u>lung</u> is better compared to the nondependent <u>lung</u>.

* In the case of a large unilateral <u>pulmonary abscess</u>, it may be helpful to position the patient so that the affected <u>lung</u> is in the <u>dependent position</u> in order to prevent the <u>pus</u> from filling the unaffected <u>lung</u>.

### Pattern of involvement



* Lobar pneumonia

* Classic (typical) pneumonia of an entire lobe; primarily caused by <u>pneumococci</u>

* Characterized by inflammatory intra-alveolar <u>exudate</u>, resulting in consolidation

* Can involve the entire lobe or the whole <u>lung</u>

* Bronchopneumonia: most commonly a descending infection that affects the <u>bronchioles</u> and adjacent <u>alveoli</u>

* Primarily caused by <u>pneumococci</u> and/or other <u>streptococci</u>

* Characterized by acute inflammatory infiltrates that fill the <u>bronchioles</u> and the adjacent <u>alveoli</u> (patchy distribution)

* Usually involves the lower lobes or right middle lobe and affects ≥ 1 lobe

* Manifests as <u>typical pneumonia</u>

* Necrotizing bronchopneumonia and <u>pneumatocele</u> are caused by <u>Staphylococcus aureus</u> and are often preceded by an <u>influenza</u> infection. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Kr0URh)

* Interstitial pneumonia: <u>interstitial</u> inflammation, typically caused by <u>Mycoplasma</u> and viral infections

* Characterized by a diffuse patchy inflammation that mainly involves the alveolar <u>interstitial</u> cells

* Bilateral multifocal opacities are classically found on <u>chest x-ray</u>.

* Manifests as <u>atypical pneumonia</u>

* Often has an indolent course (<u>walking pneumonia</u>)

* Miliary pneumonia: multiple small infiltrations caused by hematogenous dissemination (e.g., of <u>tuberculosis</u>)

* <u>Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia</u>: characterized by inflammation of the <u>bronchioles</u> and surrounding structures

## Clinical features

### Typical pneumonia




<u>Typical pneumonia</u> is characterized by a sudden onset of symptoms caused by lobar infiltration.




* Severe <u>malaise</u>

* High <u>fever</u> and chills

* Productive <u>cough</u> with <u>purulent</u> <u>sputum</u> (yellow-greenish)

* <u>Crackles</u> and bronchial breath sounds on <u>auscultation</u>

* Decreased breath sounds

* Enhanced <u>bronchophony</u>, <u>egophony</u>, and <u>tactile fremitus</u>

* Dullness on percussion

* <u>Tachypnea</u> and <u>dyspnea</u> (<u>nasal flaring</u>, thoracic <u>retractions</u>)

* <u>Pleuritic chest pain</u> when breathing, often accompanying <u>pleural effusion</u>

* <u>Pain</u> that radiates to the abdomen and epigastric region (particularly in children; see also “<u>Pneumonia in children</u>”)





> [!TIP]
> Suspect bacterial pneumonia in <u>immunocompromised</u> patients with acute high <u>fever</u> and <u>pleural effusion</u>.




### Atypical pneumonia




<u>Atypical pneumonia</u> typically has an indolent course (slow onset) and commonly manifests with extrapulmonary symptoms.




* Nonproductive, dry <u>cough</u>

* <u>Dyspnea</u>

* <u>Auscultation</u> often unremarkable

* Common extrapulmonary features include fatigue, <u>headaches</u>, <u>sore throat</u>, <u>myalgias</u>, and <u>malaise</u>.





> [!TIP]
> This classification does not have a major impact on patient management because it is not always possible to clearly distinguish between typical and <u>atypical pneumonia</u>.

## Diagnosis

### General principles [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VkdG5J0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ekdx5J0)




* Pneumonia diagnosis is based on new pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging in patients with respiratory symptoms and systemic inflammatory response.

* The choice of microbiological studies is guided by a <u>severity assessment for CAP</u> or the presence of <u>HAP</u> or <u>VAP</u>.

* In ambulatory settings, <u>normal vital signs</u> and an unremarkable <u>lung examination</u> indicate a very low likelihood of <u>CAP</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/n9c7Le0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/L9cwLe0)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Diagnosis of pneumonia</u> can be challenging due to the lack of a <u>gold-standard test</u> and numerous mimics (e.g., <u>COPD exacerbation</u>, <u>heart failure</u>), especially when diagnostic testing is ambiguous. [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ekdx5J0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)




### Diagnosis of <u>CAP</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VkdG5J0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ekdx5J0)




* New pulmonary infiltrate on chest imaging (e.g., <u>CXR</u>)

* AND ≥ 1 new or increased respiratory symptom: e.g., <u>cough</u>, <u>sputum</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>, or pleuritic <u>pain</u>

* AND ≥ 1 of the following:

* <u>Fever</u> or <u>hypothermia</u>

* <u>Leukocytosis</u>, bandemia, or <u>leukopenia</u>

* <u>Hypoxemia</u>





> [!TIP]
> Consider chest CT or empiric treatment when clinical suspicion for <u>CAP</u> remains high despite a negative <u>CXR</u>, as <u>false negatives</u> are possible. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/69cjoe0)

* <u>Severe CAP</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VkdG5J0)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ekdx5J0)

* Obtain the following in all patients:

* <u>Blood cultures</u> (2 sets)

* <u>Sputum</u> culture and <u>Gram stain</u>

* <u>Influenza</u> and <u>COVID-19 testing</u>

* <u>Pneumococcal urinary antigen</u>

* <u>Legionella pneumophila</u> urinary <u>antigen</u>

* Obtain a <u>MRSA</u> nasal swab for patients with:

* Current empiric coverage for <u>MRSA</u>

* History of parenteral <u>antibiotics</u> in a hospital setting within the past 90 days

* History of <u>MRSA</u> <u>colonization</u> or infection in the past year

* If available:

* Obtain a bacterial molecular panel.

* Consider obtaining an expanded viral molecular panel (e.g., <u>RSV</u>, <u>rhinovirus</u>, <u>enterovirus</u>) when it would alter management.

* Nonsevere <u>CAP</u>

* Obtain the following during periods of community transmission or recent potential exposure (e.g., recent travel to areas with high transmission):

* <u>Influenza</u> and <u>COVID-19 testing</u>

* <u>Legionella pneumophila</u> urinary <u>antigen</u>

* Obtain <u>sputum</u> culture and <u>Gram stain</u> for patients with:

* History of parenteral <u>antibiotics</u> in a hospital setting within the past 90 days

* Current empiric coverage for <u>MRSA</u> or <u>P. aeruginosa</u>

* Structural <u>lung</u> disease

* Obtain a <u>MRSA</u> nasal swab for patients with:

* Current empirical coverage for <u>MRSA</u>

* History of parenteral <u>antibiotics</u> in a hospital setting within the past 90 days

* If available, consider the following when it would alter management: 

* Expanded viral molecular panel (e.g., <u>RSV</u>, <u>rhinovirus</u>, <u>enterovirus</u>)

* Bacterial molecular panel

* <u>HAP</u> or <u>VAP</u> [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)

* Obtain respiratory culture and <u>Gram stain</u> (noninvasive methods are preferred).  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)

* Consider additional testing based on individual patient factors and severity.





> [!TIP]
> Test for <u>COVID-19</u> and <u>influenza</u> in all patients with possible <u>CAP</u> during periods of community transmission or if they are at high risk of exposure (e.g., recent travel to areas with high transmission). [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VkdG5J0)




> [!WARNING]
> Avoid routine <u>blood cultures</u> and <u>sputum</u> <u>Gram stain</u> in patients with <u>CAP</u>, except when severe or there is concern for <u>MRSA</u> or <u>Pseudomonas</u> infection. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)




### Imaging




#### <u>Chest x-ray</u> (posteroanterior and <u>lateral</u>)




* Indications: all patients suspected of having pneumonia

* X-ray findings in pneumonia

* <u>Lobar pneumonia</u>

* Opacity of one or more pulmonary lobes

* Presence of air bronchograms: appearance of translucent <u>bronchi</u> inside opaque areas of alveolar consolidation

* <u>Bronchopneumonia</u> 

* Poorly defined patchy infiltrates scattered throughout the <u>lungs</u>

* Presence of <u>air bronchograms</u>

* Atypical or <u>interstitial pneumonia</u>

* Diffuse reticular opacity

* Absent (or minimal) consolidation

* <u>Parapneumonic effusion</u>





> [!TIP]
> A new pulmonary infiltrate on <u>chest x-ray</u> in a patient with classic signs and <u>symptoms of pneumonia</u> confirms the diagnosis. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)




> [!TIP]
> <u>Typical pneumonia</u> usually appears as <u>lobar pneumonia</u> on <u>x-ray</u>, while <u>atypical pneumonia</u> tends to appear as <u>interstitial pneumonia</u>. However, the underlying <u>pathogen</u> cannot be conclusively identified based on imaging results alone.

## Disposition

### Criteria for hospitalization [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/o9c0oe0)




Every patient should be assessed individually; clinical judgment is the most important factor. The <u>pneumonia severity index</u> (PSI) and the <u>CURB-65</u> score are tools that can help to determine whether to admit a patient.




* Pneumonia severity index (PSI/<u>PORT score</u>)    [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3BYS07)

* Patients are assigned to one of five risk classes based on a more complex point system than in <u>CURB-65</u>.

* Points are distributed based on patient age, comorbidities, and lab results.

* CURB-65 score  ; [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/koYmXJ)

* Confusion (<u>disorientation</u>, impaired consciousness)

* Serum Urea/BUN  > 7 mmol/L

* Respiratory rate ≥ 30/min

* Blood pressure: systolic BP ≤ 90 mm Hg or <u>diastolic</u> BP ≤ 60 mm Hg

* Age ≥ 65 years

* Interpretation

* Each finding is assigned 1 point.

* <u>CURB-65</u> score 0 or 1: The patient may be treated as an outpatient.

* <u>CURB-65 score</u> ≥ 2: Hospitalization is indicated.

* <u>CURB-65 score</u> ≥ 3: Consider <u>ICU</u> <u>level of care</u>.

* CRB-65 score (if serum <u>urea</u> is not known or unavailable)

* <u>CRB-65 score</u> of 0: The patient may be treated as an outpatient.

* <u>CRB-65 score</u> of ≥ 1: Hospitalization is recommended.

## Treatment

### Approach




* Evaluate all patients for <u>hypoxemia</u> and <u>sepsis</u> and manage as indicated.

* Determine the appropriate <u>level of care</u> using clinical judgment and prediction tools (e.g., <u>PSI score</u> and <u>IDSA/ATS criteria for severe CAP</u>).

* Begin <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> based on severity and patient <u>risk factors</u> (e.g., <u>VAP</u> vs. <u>CAP</u>).

* Consider <u>antiviral therapy for influenza</u> and <u>COVID-19</u> as indicated.

* <u>Glucocorticoids</u>

* Consider initiating <u>hydrocortisone</u> DOSAGE within 24 hours of diagnosis in patients being treated for <u>severe CAP</u> in the <u>ICU</u>. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1eW2B40)

* Not recommended for nonsevere <u>CAP</u> [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A4dRNJ0)

* Reevaluate therapy within 48 hours.





> [!TIP]
> Consider deferral of <u>antibiotic therapy</u> in outpatients with a viral diagnosis and no comorbidities. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CzdqEs0)

* Duration of treatment

* <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> should be narrowed and/or de-escalated as soon as feasible.

* Seven days of therapy are usually sufficient. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)

* Additional considerations: Resistance patterns can vary widely; local <u>antibiograms</u> should be considered when starting empiric treatment.

> Patients with structural <u>lung</u> disease and/or at high risk for mortality should receive double antipseudomonal coverage!




### Empiric antibiotic therapy for ventilator-associated pneumonia [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)




* Recommended combination therapy

* An antipneumococcal, antipseudomonal <u>β-lactam</u>

* <u>Aztreonam</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Imipenem</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Meropenem</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Ceftazidime</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Cefepime</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Piperacillin-tazobactam</u> DOSAGE

* PLUS one of the following <u>antibiotics</u> with <u>MRSA</u> activity: 

* <u>Vancomycin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Linezolid</u> DOSAGE

* PLUS one of the following: 

* A <u>fluoroquinolone</u>

* <u>Levofloxacin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Ciprofloxacin</u> DOSAGE

* An <u>aminoglycoside</u>

* <u>Amikacin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Gentamicin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Tobramycin</u> DOSAGE

* A <u>polymyxin</u>

* <u>Colistin</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Polymyxin</u> B DOSAGE

* Duration of treatment: Seven days of therapy are usually sufficient. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)

* Additional considerations:

* <u>Risk factors</u> for <u>multi-drug resistant organisms</u> (e.g., presence of structural <u>lung</u> disease, recent IV <u>antibiotic therapy</u>, local resistance patterns) should be considered when deciding on an empiric regimen

* <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> for ventilator-associated tracheobronchitis is not routinely recommended. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QYYuKn)





### Supportive therapy for pneumonia




* Sufficient rest (not absolute bed rest) and <u>physical therapy</u>

* Hydration with PO or <u>IV fluids</u>, <u>supplemental oxygen</u> as needed

* <u>Incentive spirometer</u>

* <u>Antipyretics</u>, <u>analgesics</u> as needed (e.g., <u>acetaminophen</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Expectorants</u> and <u>mucolytics</u>  [[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uBYpc7)

* <u>Antitussives</u> (e.g., <u>codeine</u> DOSAGE)

## Pathogen-specific pneumonia

### Mycoplasma pneumonia [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/slXtyy)




* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* One of the most common causes of <u>atypical pneumonia</u>

* More common in school-aged children and <u>adolescents</u>

* <u>Outbreaks</u> most commonly occur in schools, colleges, prisons, and military facilities.

* Clinical features

* Generalized <u>papular</u> <u>rash</u>

* <u>Erythema multiforme</u>

* See “<u>Atypical pneumonia</u>”.

* Diagnostics

* Subclinical <u>hemolytic anemia</u>: associated with elevated <u>cold agglutinin</u> <u>titers</u> (<u>IgM</u>)

* <u>Interstitial pneumonia</u>; , often with a reticulonodular pattern on <u>chest x-ray</u>

* <u>Chest x-ray</u> can show extensive pulmonary involvement in patients with mild pneumonia.

* Treatment

* A <u>macrolide</u>, <u>doxycycline</u>, or <u>fluoroquinolones</u>

* <u>Beta-lactam antibiotics</u> are not effective

* See “<u>Empiric antibiotics for CAP</u>” for dosages and duration of treatment.

## Aspiration pneumonia

### Definitions




* Aspiration

* The inhalation of foreign material into the respiratory tract

* Most commonly occurs after instrumentation of the upper <u>airways</u> or <u>esophagus</u> (e.g., <u>upper GI endoscopy</u>) or secondary to <u>vomiting</u> and <u>regurgitation</u> of gastric contents

* <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u>: a type of pneumonia that occurs as a result of oropharyngeal secretions and/or gastric contents <u>aspiration</u>

* Aspiration pneumonitis

* <u>Aspiration</u> of <u>gastric acid</u> that initially causes tracheobronchitis, with rapid progression to <u>chemical pneumonitis</u>

* May cause <u>ARDS</u> in extreme cases





> [!TIP]
> Patients may develop <u>aspiration pneumonitis</u> without pneumonia, <u>aspiration pneumonia</u> without <u>pneumonitis</u>, or <u>aspiration pneumonitis</u> complicated by pneumonia. [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)




> [!TIP]
> The risk of developing <u>aspiration pneumonia</u> and the severity of <u>lung injury</u> are directly related to the volume of aspirated material. [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9pdN7I0)[[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cpdq7I0)




### Etiology




* Pathogens [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)

* Gram-positive and gram-negative <u>aerobic bacteria</u> predominate in <u>community-acquired infections</u> (e.g., <u>S. pneumonia</u>, <u>S. aureus</u>, <u>H. influenza</u>, <u>Enterobacteriaceae</u>).

* <u>Gram-negative bacilli</u> predominate in <u>hospital-acquired infections</u> (e.g., <u>P. aeruginosa</u>, <u>Klebsiella</u> spp.).

* Mixed infections with anaerobic organisms;  may occur (e.g., <u>Fusobacterium</u>, <u>Peptostreptococcus</u>, <u>Bacteroides</u>).

* Risk factors for aspiration (predispose individuals to reduced epiglottic <u>gag reflex</u> and <u>dysphagia</u>)

* Altered consciousness: <u>alcohol</u>, sedation, <u>general anesthesia</u>, <u>stroke</u>

* Apoplexy and <u>neurodegenerative conditions</u>

* <u>Gastroesophageal reflux disease</u>, <u>esophageal motility disorders</u>

* Congenital defects (e.g., <u>tracheoesophageal fistula</u>)

* Use of a nasogastric <u>feeding tube</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Aspiration pneumonitis</u> and pneumonia are unusual following <u>aspiration</u> of tube feeds or blood, which are typically high-<u>pH</u> and sterile. [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)




### Clinical features [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EQ18Bg0)




* <u>Aspiration pneumonitis</u>

* Immediate symptoms: bronchospasms , <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>wheezing</u> and/or <u>crackles</u>, <u>hypoxemia</u>

* Late symptoms: <u>fever</u>, <u>shortness of breath</u>, <u>cough</u>

* <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u>

* Immediate symptoms: often none

* Late symptoms: <u>fever</u>, <u>shortness of breath</u>, <u>cough</u> with foul-smelling <u>sputum</u>





### Diagnostics of pulmonary aspiration




<u>Clinical diagnosis</u> supported by characteristic laboratory and imaging findings




* Laboratory and microbiological studies: same as for the <u>diagnosis of pneumonia</u>.

* <u>ABG</u>: e.g., ↓ <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>

* Imaging: The <u>lung</u> region in which the infiltrates are seen depends on the patient's position on <u>aspiration</u>.    [[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oNX0XA)[[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KNXUXA)

* <u>Supine position</u>: superior segment of the right lower lobe and <u>posterior</u> segment of the right upper lobe (most common sites of <u>aspiration</u>)

* Standing/sitting: <u>posterior</u> basal segment of the right lower lobe

* Right <u>lateral decubitus position</u>: <u>posterior</u> segment of the right upper lobe and right middle lobe

* Left <u>lateral</u> <u>decubitus</u>: <u>posterior</u> segment of the left upper lobe and lingula





> [!WARNING]
> The initial <u>CXR</u> may be negative in early <u>aspiration pneumonia</u>. [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)

### Treatment of pulmonary aspiration [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)




* Acute <u>aspiration</u>: <u>airway management</u> and <u>respiratory support</u>

* Immediate oropharyngeal suctioning

* <u>Intubation</u> if there is ongoing risk of <u>aspiration</u> (e.g., post-<u>extubation</u>, ↓ <u>LOC</u>)

* <u>O<sub>2</sub> therapy</u> and inhaled <u>bronchodilators</u> as needed

* <u>Aspiration pneumonitis</u>: typically requires <u>supportive care</u> only

* <u>Antibiotics</u> are usually not required  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)

* Consider <u>empiric antibiotics</u> for patients with any of the following :

* <u>Respiratory failure</u> or suspected <u>septic shock</u>

* <u>Acid suppression medications</u>

* <u>Small bowel obstruction</u>

* <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u>: <u>antibiotic therapy</u> following standard <u>pneumonia treatment</u> regimens

* Choose agents based on site of acquisition, illness severity, and <u>risk factors</u> for resistant organisms (see “<u>Empiric antibiotics for CAP</u>“ and “<u>Empiric antibiotics for HAP</u>”).

* Consider coverage for <u>anaerobic bacteria</u> (e.g., with <u>ampicillin-sulbactam</u>, <u>moxifloxacin</u>) in patients with severe <u>periodontal disease</u>, <u>lung abscess</u>, or <u>empyema</u>. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)[[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5l1ixS0)

* All patients: <u>supportive care for pneumonia</u>, monitoring, consider serial imaging.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u> requires <u>antibiotic therapy</u> while <u>aspiration pneumonitis</u> typically self-resolves within 24–48 hours with <u>supportive care</u> alone. [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8Yd6I)[[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ii1YGg0)




> [!WARNING]
> Avoid routine anaerobic coverage for <u>aspiration pneumonia</u> without <u>lung abscess</u>, <u>empyema</u>, or severe <u>periodontal disease</u>. [[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5l1ixS0)




### Complications




* Acute <u>respiratory failure</u>, <u>acute respiratory distress syndrome</u> (<u>ARDS</u>)

* <u>Abscess</u>





### Prevention [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/v9cA6e0)




* Treatment of underlying causes to reduce the risk of <u>aspiration</u>

* <u>NPO</u> status prior to elective procedures with <u>general anesthesia</u>

* Formal <u>swallowing evaluation</u> when clinically appropriate

* Aspiration precautions for patients with <u>risk factors for aspiration</u>

* Regular <u>oral care</u>

* Elevation of the head of the bed

* <u>Dysphagia</u>-modified diet

* One-on-one observation with meals

* Suctioning equipment at bedside

## Community-acquired pneumonia in pregnancy

* Overview

* <u>CAP</u> is responsible for approx. 5% of antepartum admissions for nonobstetric complications. [[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MWWMN40)

* <u>Physiological changes during pregnancy</u> (e.g., decreased <u>functional residual capacity</u>) may increase the risk for a severe disease course.

* Diagnostics: <u>Pregnancy</u> should not rule out <u>chest x-ray</u>, if indicated.

* Management

* Outpatient management: only in patients with mild disease and no comorbidities, if optimal follow-up is feasible

* Low threshold for hospital admission: The presence of comorbidities (e.g., <u>asthma</u>, immunologic disorders, renal disease) warrants inpatient management.

* The approach to <u>antibiotic therapy</u> during <u>pregnancy</u> is the same as that for the general adult population, excluding potentially <u>teratogenic medications</u>, e.g.,:

* <u>Clarithromycin</u>

* <u>Fluoroquinolones</u>

* <u>Tetracyclines</u>
