# Secondary brain injury and neuroprotective measures

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Neurologic disorders > Secondary brain injury and neuroprotective measures*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dr01jh)

---

## Summary

<u>Secondary brain injury</u> is an indirect injury caused by physiological changes that are triggered by an <u>acute CNS insult</u> (e.g., <u>traumatic brain injury</u>, <u>stroke</u>, cerebral <u>hypoxia</u> secondary to <u>cardiac arrest</u>) and/or the management of the primary insult. Unlike <u>primary brain injury</u>, which refers to the direct, immediate, and potentially irreversible neuronal damage from an <u>acute CNS insult</u>, <u>secondary brain injury</u> is preventable or can be minimized with the early administration of <u>neuroprotective measures</u>. <u>Neuroprotective measures</u> involve the early and aggressive control of factors that are implicated in the etiology of <u>secondary brain injury</u>. Such measures include optimization of oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure, blood sugar, body temperature, <u>intracranial pressure</u>, and <u>electrolyte</u> levels. In addition, <u>seizure</u> prophylaxis and treatment, <u>nutritional support</u>, and patient positioning are important aspects of <u>neuroprotective measures</u>.

## Oxygenation and ventilation

> [!TIP]
> Avoid <u>hypoxia</u>, <u>hyperoxia</u>, <u>hypocapnia</u>, and <u>hypercapnia</u> in patients with <u>acute CNS insult</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




### Control of <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u> (oxygenation) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* Goals

* Avoid <u>hypoxia</u> to prevent <u>hypoxic</u> <u>brain injury</u>, especially in patients with impaired <u>cerebral autoregulation</u>.

* Avoid prolonged <u>hyperoxia</u> as it can cause or worsen cerebral <u>vasoconstriction</u> and cerebral <u>ischemia</u>.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cCYaIr)

* Target: <u>Normoxia</u> or mild <u>hyperoxia</u>

* Target <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u>: > 60 mm Hg [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sNbtc8)

* <u>Target SpO<sub>2</sub></u>: > 92–94%  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oWb0ms)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)

* Measures to achieve target oxygenation

* Avoid <u>hypotension</u>: See ''Blood pressure and <u>cerebral perfusion pressure</u>''.

* <u>Oxygen therapy</u>

* Administer <u>supplemental oxygen</u> only if <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> is < 94% [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* Use the lowest <u>FiO<sub>2</sub></u> possible to maintain <u>normoxia</u>

* <u>Airway management</u>: if necessary, intubate (<u>rapid sequence intubation</u>) according to local hospital protocols  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QLbux8)

* Carefully consider <u>intubation induction agents</u>: <u>Ketamine</u> DOSAGE usually preferred if no <u>signs of ↑ ICP</u>  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QLbux8)

* Patients with <u>signs of ↑ ICP</u>: See <u>intubation of patients with increased ICP</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)

* See <u>improving oxygenation in mechanically ventilated patients</u> for further details.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Hypoxia</u> and <u>hyperoxia</u> can worsen neurological outcome and should be avoided.




> [!TIP]
> Routine use of <u>supplemental oxygen</u> in nonhypoxic patients is of no clinical benefit in the <u>prevention of secondary brain injury</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




### Control of <u>PaCO<sub>2</sub></u> (ventilation)




* Goals

* Aim for <u>normocapnia</u> as both <u>hypercapnia</u> and <u>hypocapnia</u> can worsen <u>secondary brain injury</u>.

* <u>Hypercapnia</u> (due to <u>hypoventilation</u>) → cerebral <u>vasodilation</u> → <u>cerebral edema</u>, <u>↑ ICP</u>, and <u>intracerebral steal phenomenon</u> [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KWbUms)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6Wbjms)

* <u>Hypocapnia</u> (due to <u>hyperventilation</u>) → cerebral <u>vasoconstriction</u> → ↓ <u>CBF</u> → ↑ risk of <u>ischemia</u> to uninjured <u>brain</u> tissue  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KWbUms)

* Consider limited <u>hypocapnia</u> as a temporizing measure for acute uncontrolled <u>↑ ICP</u> or <u>cerebral herniation syndrome</u>. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)

* Short-term <u>hypocapnia</u> → temporary ↓ <u>CBF</u> → temporary ↓ <u>ICP</u>

* Long-term <u>hypocapnia</u> → ↓ <u>CBF</u> → ↑ cerebral <u>ischemia</u>

* Target <u>PaCO<sub>2</sub></u>: 35–45 mmHg [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DBY117)

* Measures to achieve target ventilation [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* No <u>signs of ↑ ICP</u>: Maintain normoventilation (prophylactic <u>hyperventilation</u> is discouraged).

* <u>Signs of ↑ ICP</u> or signs of <u>cerebral herniation syndromes</u> present: Consider short-term (< 30 minutes) <u>hyperventilation</u> to attain mild <u>hypocapnia</u> (<u>PaCO<sub>2</sub></u>30–35 mm Hg).  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DBY117)

* See <u>ventilation strategy for elevated ICP</u> for suggested initial <u>ventilator settings</u>.

* See <u>ICP management</u> for details.





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Hypercapnia</u> (including <u>permissive hypercapnia</u>) and long-term <u>hypocapnia</u> worsen neurological outcome in patients with <u>acute CNS insults</u> and should be avoided. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




> [!TIP]
> <u>Hypocapnia</u> should only be used as a temporizing measure for patients with signs of <u>cerebral herniation syndromes</u> while simultaneously initiating definitive management for <u>↑ ICP</u>. [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DBY117)




### Monitoring




* Continuous <u>pulse oximetry</u>

* Continuous <u>capnography</u>/<u>capnometry</u>

* Intermittent <u>ABG analysis</u>: Consider <u>arterial line placement</u>.

* See also “<u>Monitoring in mechanical ventilation</u>.”

## Blood sugar

Blood <u>glucose</u> should be checked at presentation and serially monitored. Strict blood <u>glucose</u> control is recommended as <u>hypoglycemia</u> or <u>hyperglycemia</u> worsen the neurological outcome after an <u>acute CNS insult</u>.




* Target blood sugar: <u>normoglycemia</u>

* In nontraumatic <u>acute CNS insult</u>: 140–180 mg/dL  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* In <u>traumatic brain injury</u>: 80–180 mg/dL [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)

* Treatment

* <u>Hypoglycemic</u> patients (blood <u>glucose</u> < 60 mg/dL): See <u>treatment of hypoglycemia</u>.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* <u>Hyperglycemic</u> patients (blood <u>glucose</u> > 180 mg/dL): See <u>management of hyperglycemia in critically ill patients</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)





> [!WARNING]
> Avoid dextrose-containing solutions in the resuscitation of nonhypoglycemic patients with an <u>acute CNS insult</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)

## Electrolyte abnormalities

<u>Sodium disorders</u> and <u>hypokalemia</u> are the most common <u>electrolyte</u> abnormalities seen after an <u>acute CNS insult</u>. Provide <u>electrolyte repletion</u> as needed for <u>electrolyte</u> deficiencies.  [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yWbdLs)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9WbNns)




### <u>Disorders of sodium balance</u> [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OmbIf8)




* General considerations

* Identify and treat the underlying cause.

* <u>Sodium</u> disturbances are often <u>self-limiting</u> in patients with <u>brain injury</u>.

* <u>Hyponatremia</u>  [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OmbIf8)

* Acutely symptomatic patients: prompt treatment with gradual correction (see “Treatment” in <u>hyponatremia</u> and <u>SIADH</u>)

* Asymptomatic patients: Supportive treatment strategy is usually appropriate.

* <u>Hypernatremia</u> [[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2MbTL8)

* Severe elevation (> 160 mEq/L): gradual correction (see “Treatment” section in <u>hypernatremia</u>)

* Mild–moderate elevation (up to 160 mEq/L): consider gradual correction





> [!TIP]
> Symptomatic <u>hypernatremia</u> should be corrected gradually to minimize the risk of cerebral and <u>pulmonary edema</u>. Symptomatic <u>hyponatremia</u> should be corrected gradually to minimize the risk of <u>central pontine myelinolysis</u>. [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OmbIf8)




### <u>Disorders of potassium balance</u>




* <u>Hypokalemia</u>: See “<u>Potassium repletion</u>” ”Treatment” section in <u>hypokalemia</u> and <u>repletion regimens of hypokalemia</u>.

* <u>Hyperkalemia</u>: See “Treatment” section in <u>hyperkalemia</u>.

## Neurogenic fever and targeted temperature management

### Neurogenic fever (<u>central hyperthermia</u>) [[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Elb8AF)[[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vlbAAF)




* Definition: Noninfectious <u>fever</u> after an <u>acute CNS insult</u> is likely caused by injury to the <u>hypothalamic</u> <u>thermoregulation</u> centers.

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: seen in up to 37% of patients with <u>TBI</u>; may also occur after <u>stroke</u> or neurosurgical intervention

* Implication: associated with worse neurological outcomes and increased mortality

* Management: <u>targeted temperature management</u>





> [!TIP]
> In patients with <u>acute CNS insults</u>, <u>fever</u> should be aggressively treated as it is associated with a poor neurological outcome and increased risk of mortality.




### Targeted temperature management (<u>TTM</u>)




* Definition: controlled maintenance of a target body temperature aimed to prevent <u>secondary brain injury</u> after an <u>acute CNS insult</u>

* Target body temperature: depends on the indication 

* 36–37.8°C; /96.8–100°F (controlled <u>normothermia</u>) [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dMYonp)

* <u>TBI</u>  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RWblOs)

* <u>Hemorrhagic stroke</u>

* <u>Ischemic stroke</u>  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pmbLg8)[[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3WbSOs)[[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/smbtS8)

* <u>Neurogenic fever</u>

* 32–37.5°C/89.6–99.5°F: <u>cardiac arrest</u> (in patients who are unresponsive after <u>ROSC</u>) [[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c9Wamn0)

* 33°C/91.4°F  (moderate therapeutic hypothermia): <u>hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy</u> [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4mb3f8)[[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mmbVT8)

* Measures to achieve <u>TTM</u>  [[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rmbfS8)

* Physical/surface cooling: may be local (e.g., head cooling with a cooling helmet) or general (e.g., with cooling blankets/pads)  [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HmbKS8)

* Endovascular cooling (e.g., rapid IV infusion with cold <u>normal saline</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* Pharmacological <u>hypothermia</u> (for <u>therapeutic hypothermia</u>): <u>hypothermia</u>-inducing drugs (e.g., <u>cannabinoids</u>, <u>opioids</u>) [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pmbLg8)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rmbfS8)[[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qmbCg8)

* <u>Antipyretics</u> (e.g., <u>acetaminophen</u> DOSAGE)  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[53]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LmbwT8)

* Monitoring: Continuous/hourly monitoring of temperature is recommended.

* Optimal duration of <u>TTM</u>: unclear  [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pmbLg8)[[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qmbCg8)

* Complications of <u>therapeutic hypothermia</u> [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pmbLg8)

* <u>Bradycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>

* <u>Immunosuppression</u> and increased risk of infection

* <u>Coagulopathy</u>

* Altered serum <u>electrolyte</u> and blood <u>glucose</u> levels

## Other

### <u>Intracranial pressure</u> (<u>ICP</u>)




* Target: Maintain <u>ICP</u> below 20–22 mm Hg  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sNbtc8)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CZbqdH)

* Treatment: See <u>ICP management</u>.

* Monitoring: <u>ICP</u> is not routinely monitored, but patients should be observed for <u>signs of raised ICP</u>.





### Patient positioning




* Patients without <u>features of raised ICP</u> or <u>hypoxia</u>: <u>supine position</u> with no elevation of the head  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* Patients with <u>features of raised ICP</u> or those at risk of <u>aspiration</u> or <u>airway obstruction</u>: head end elevation to 30º [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)





### <u>Transfusion</u>




* Red cell <u>transfusion</u>: Transfuse packed red cells if <u>Hb</u> ≤ 7 g/dL (restrictive threshold) [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/60bjSH)[[54]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4vb3ZD)[[55]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zZbrVH)

* Platelet transfusion in intracranial bleeding

* Spontaneous <u>intracerebral hemorrhage</u> (<u>ICH</u>): <u>Platelet transfusion</u> is not recommended in patients with <u>ICH</u> who are on <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> [[56]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qvbu-v)

* <u>TBI</u>: There is currently no clear recommendation for/against <u>platelet transfusion</u> in patients with <u>TBI</u> and concurrent <u>thrombocytopenia</u>, <u>platelet dysfunction</u>, or <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sNbtc8)[[55]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zZbrVH)

* Consider <u>platelet transfusion</u> if <u>platelet count</u> is less than 80–100,000/mm<sup>3</sup> in patients planned for neurosurgery or invasive procedures.  [[57]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rv0fYR)

* Nonoperative <u>ICH</u>: <u>Platelet transfusion</u> is not recommended for patients with <u>platelet count</u> > 100,000/mm<sup>3</sup>. [[58]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pCdLGH0)





### Nutrition [[59]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZdbZos)[[60]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0dbeos)




* Neurological insult typically leads to a <u>hypermetabolic state</u>. [[61]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-WbDLs)

* Early (ideally within 24 hours) <u>nutritional support</u> (enteral and parenteral) improves neurological outcomes compared to delayed <u>nutritional support</u>.

* <u>Enteral nutrition</u> is preferred if possible; patients with low <u>GCS</u> or <u>dysphagia</u> may require a nasogastric or <u>nasojejunal tube</u>.
