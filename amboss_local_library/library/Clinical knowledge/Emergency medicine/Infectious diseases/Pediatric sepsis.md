## Summary

Pediatric <u>sepsis</u> is an acute, life-threatening condition in which infection leads to organ dysfunction. Without prompt intervention, affected children frequently progress to <u>septic shock</u> and <u>multiple organ failure</u>. The precipitating infection is most commonly bacterial. Clinical features are often nonspecific and include <u>fever</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, signs of organ dysfunction (e.g., reduced <u>GCS</u>, <u>jaundice</u>), and <u>signs of shock</u>. Diagnosis occurs alongside management and involves <u>blood cultures</u>, <u>lactate</u>, assessment for markers of infection and <u>end-organ dysfunction</u>, and investigation of the underlying etiology. Initial stabilization focuses on the delivery of the <u>hour-1 bundle for children</u>, which combines definitive treatment (<u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u>) and resuscitation (<u>intravenous fluids</u>, <u>vasoactive medications</u>). Ongoing management includes <u>nutritional support</u>, tailored <u>antibiotic therapy</u>, and regular reassessment. Prevention of <u>sepsis</u> involves <u>infection control</u> measures and early recognition and management of severe infections.




For <u>neonates</u>, see "<u>Neonatal bacterial infections</u>."
