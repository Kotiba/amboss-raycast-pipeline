## Summary

<u>Talus</u> <u>fractures</u> account for less than 1% of all <u>fractures</u>. They are generally caused by high-energy trauma with axial loading (e.g., due to <u>motor vehicle collisions</u>) or <u>sports injuries</u> (e.g., <u>snowboarder fracture</u>). <u>Talus</u> <u>fractures</u> are classified according to their anatomical location as head, neck, or body <u>fractures</u>. Clinical features include <u>acute pain</u>, tenderness, swelling, and <u>ecchymosis</u> around and/or below the ankle; restricted <u>range of motion</u>; and inability to bear weight. <u>X-rays</u> of the ankle and foot should be obtained for all patients with a suspected <u>talus</u> <u>fracture</u>. CT imaging should be obtained for all patients with a confirmed talar <u>fracture</u> or highly suspected talar <u>fracture</u> with negative <u>x-rays</u>. Most <u>talus</u> <u>fractures</u> require surgical management, with nonoperative management reserved for certain types of nondisplaced <u>fractures</u>. Complications include <u>avascular necrosis</u>, posttraumatic <u>arthritis</u>, and union issues (e.g., <u>malunion</u>, <u>nonunion</u>).
