# Pneumothorax

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Emergency medicine > Trauma > Pneumothorax*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vh0Aff)

---

## Summary

Pneumothorax develops when air enters the <u>pleural space</u> as the result of disease or injury. This leads to a loss of negative pressure between the two <u>pleural membranes</u>, which can result in the partial or complete collapse of the <u>lung</u>. Pneumothorax is classified as spontaneous or traumatic. <u>Spontaneous pneumothorax</u> can be further classified as primary (i.e., no underlying <u>lung</u> disease) or secondary (i.e., due to underlying <u>lung</u> disease). Any type of pneumothorax can progress to <u>tension pneumothorax</u>, which is a life-threatening variant of pneumothorax. Patients with pneumothorax usually present with sudden-onset <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>ipsilateral</u> <u>chest pain</u>, diminished breath sounds, and hyper-resonant percussion on the affected side. <u>Tension pneumothorax</u> further manifests with distended neck <u>veins</u>, <u>tracheal deviation</u>, and <u>hemodynamic instability</u>. There should be a high index of suspicion for both conditions on clinical evaluation. Unstable patients with <u>tension pneumothorax</u> require immediate <u>needle decompression</u>. Chest <u>x-ray</u> may be used to confirm the diagnosis in stable patients. Small pneumothoraces may resorb spontaneously, but larger defects usually require placement of a <u>chest tube</u>.

---

## Definitions

* Pneumothorax: a collection of air within the <u>pleural space</u> between the <u>lung</u> (<u>visceral pleura</u>) and the <u>chest wall</u> (<u>parietal pleura</u>) that can lead to partial or complete pulmonary collapse. May be classified as: [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GCYBtr)

* Spontaneous pneumothorax

* Primary spontaneous pneumothorax: occurs in patients without clinically apparent underlying <u>lung</u> disease

* Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax: occurs as a complication of underlying <u>lung</u> disease

* Recurrent pneumothorax: a second episode of <u>spontaneous pneumothorax</u>, either <u>ipsilateral</u> or <u>contralateral</u>

* Traumatic pneumothorax: a type of pneumothorax caused by a trauma (e.g., penetrating injury, <u>iatrogenic</u> trauma)

* Tension pneumothorax: a life-threatening variant of pneumothorax characterized by progressively increasing pressure within the chest and cardiorespiratory compromise

## Etiology

### <u>Spontaneous pneumothorax</u>




* Primary (<u>idiopathic</u> or simple pneumothorax)

* Caused by ruptured subpleural apical blebs

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* <u>Family history</u>

* Male sex

* Young age

* <u>Asthenic</u> body habitus (slim, <u>tall stature</u>)

* Smoking (90% of cases): up to 20-fold increase in risk (risk increases with the cumulative number of cigarettes smoked)

* <u>Homocystinuria</u>

* Secondary (pneumothorax as a complication of underlying <u>lung</u> disease)

* <u>COPD</u> (smoking) → rupture of <u>bullae</u> in <u>emphysema</u>

* Infections

* <u>Pulmonary tuberculosis</u>

* <u>Pneumocystis pneumonia</u> → alveolitis, rupture of a cavity

* <u>Cystic fibrosis</u> → <u>bronchiectasis</u> with obstructive <u>emphysema</u> and bleb or cyst rupture

* <u>Marfan syndrome</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u>

* Catamenial pneumothorax (thoracic <u>endometriosis</u>): extremely rare





### <u>Traumatic pneumothorax</u>




* <u>Blunt trauma</u> (e.g., <u>motor vehicle accident</u> in which the thorax hits the steering wheel or <u>rib fracture</u> occurs)

* Penetrating injury (e.g., gunshot, <u>stab wound</u>)

* <u>Iatrogenic</u> pneumothorax: <u>mechanical ventilation</u> with high <u>PEEP</u> (<u>barotrauma</u>), <u>thoracocentesis</u>, <u>central venous catheter placement</u>, <u>bronchoscopy</u>, <u>lung</u> <u>biopsy</u>





> [!TIP]
> Any type of pneumothorax may lead to <u>tension pneumothorax</u>.

## Pathophysiology

Increased intrapleural pressure → alveolar collapse → decreased <u>V/Q ratio</u> and increased right-to-left shunting. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/viYAtK)




* <u>Spontaneous pneumothorax</u>: rupture of blebs and <u>bullae</u>  → air moves into <u>pleural space</u> with increasing positive pressure  → <u>ipsilateral</u> <u>lung</u> is compressed and collapses

* <u>Traumatic pneumothorax</u>  

* Closed pneumothorax: air enters through a hole in the <u>lung</u> (e.g., following <u>blunt trauma</u>)

* Open pneumothorax: air enters through a lesion in the <u>chest wall</u> (e.g., following <u>penetrating trauma</u>)

* Air enters the <u>pleural space</u> on inspiration and leaks to the exterior on expiration.

* Air shifts between the <u>lungs</u>.

* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u>  
1. * Disrupted <u>visceral pleura</u>, <u>parietal pleura</u>, or tracheobronchial tree
2. * One-way valve mechanism, in which air enters the <u>pleural space</u> on inspiration but cannot exit
3. * Progressive accumulation of air in the <u>pleural space</u> and increasing positive pressure within the chest
4. * Collapse of <u>ipsilateral</u> <u>lung</u>; compression of <u>contralateral</u> <u>lung</u>, <u>trachea</u>, <u>heart</u>, and <u>superior vena cava</u>; angulation of <u>inferior vena cava</u>
5. * Impaired respiratory function, reduced <u>venous return</u> to the <u>heart</u>
6. * Reduced <u>cardiac output</u>
7. * <u>Hypoxia</u> and <u>hemodynamic instability</u>

## Clinical features

Patients range from being asymptomatic to having features of hemodynamic compromise. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)




* Sudden, severe, and/or stabbing, <u>ipsilateral</u> pleuritic <u>chest pain</u> and <u>dyspnea</u>

* Reduced or absent breath sounds, hyperresonant percussion, decreased <u>fremitus</u> on the <u>ipsilateral</u> side

* <u>Subcutaneous emphysema</u>

* Additional findings in <u>tension pneumothorax</u>  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDYMUr)

* Severe acute <u>respiratory distress</u>: <u>cyanosis</u>, restlessness, <u>diaphoresis</u>

* Reduced chest expansion on the <u>ipsilateral</u> side

* Distended neck <u>veins</u> and <u>hemodynamic instability</u> (<u>tachycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>pulsus paradoxus</u>)

* Secondary injuries may be present (e.g., open or <u>closed wounds</u>).

* Signs of <u>tension pneumothorax</u> in ventilated patients [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDYMUr)

* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u> (<u>obstructive shock</u>)

* Distention of jugular <u>vein</u>

* Rapid decrease in <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u>

* Reduced air flow

* Increased ventilation pressure

* <u>Skin</u> <u>emphysema</u>





> [!NOTE]
> P-THORAX: Pleuritic <u>pain</u>, Tracheal deviation, Hyperresonance, Onset sudden, Reduced breath sounds (and <u>dyspnea</u>), Absent <u>fremitus</u>, X-ray show collapse.

## Diagnosis

### General principles [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)




* The diagnosis of pneumothorax is usually confirmed by chest <u>x-ray</u>.

* <u>Ultrasound</u> is becoming an increasingly accepted modality for identifying pneumothorax and is part of the <u>eFAST</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SCYyrr)

* CT can provide information about the underlying cause (e.g., <u>bullae</u> in <u>spontaneous pneumothorax</u>).

* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u> is primarily a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> and prolonged diagnostic studies should be avoided in favor of initiating immediate treatment.





> [!WARNING]
> In cases of <u>tension pneumothorax</u>, immediate decompression is a priority and should not be delayed by imaging.

### <u>Chest x-ray</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)




* Indications: all patients suspected of having pneumothorax

* Procedure: Upright PA chest <u>x-ray</u> in inspiration is the modality of choice.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/gCYFrr)

* Supportive findings of pneumothorax

* <u>Ipsilateral</u> <u>pleural line</u> with reduced/absent <u>lung markings</u> (i.e., increased transparency)

* Abrupt change in <u>radiolucency</u>

* Deep sulcus sign

* Decreased <u>radiodensity</u> and deep <u>costophrenic angle</u> on the <u>ipsilateral</u> side  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jzY_s7)

* The sign is a result of interpleural air that collects basally and anteriorly in the <u>supine position</u>.

* Hemidiaphragm elevation on the <u>ipsilateral</u> side

* If pulmonary disease is present: <u>airway</u> or <u>parenchymal</u> lesions

* Supportive findings of <u>tension pneumothorax</u>

* <u>Ipsilateral</u> diaphragmatic flattening/<u>inversion</u> and widened <u>intercostal spaces</u>

* <u>Mediastinal</u> shift toward the <u>contralateral</u> side

* Tracheal deviation toward the <u>contralateral</u> side  [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h_YcL7)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/S_Yyn7)





> [!TIP]
> Pneumothorax is very difficult to identify on <u>supine</u> <u>CXR</u>; consider <u>ultrasound</u> or CT chest in patients unable to sit upright.

### Determination of pneumothorax size




The size of a pneumothorax is assessed via imaging (e.g., <u>CXR</u>, CT chest). How a pneumothorax is measured depends on regional guidelines, hospital policies, and personal preferences:




* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u>  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

* Interpleural distance at the level of the <u>lung</u> hilus [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* Collins method: Calculated pneumothorax size in percent of hemithorax  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iAYJj7)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kAYmP7)

* The interpleural distance on a PA <u>CXR</u> is measured in centimeters at three points.

* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u> (A)

* Midpoints of upper (B) and lower (C) half of collapsed <u>lung</u>

* Pneumothorax size as a percent of the <u>ipsilateral</u> hemithorax = 4.2 + 4.7 x (A + B + C)





### <u>Laboratory studies</u>




<u>Laboratory analysis</u> is generally not indicated.



* <u>Arterial blood gas analysis</u> (<u>ABG</u>) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* Indications

* <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> < 92% on room air

* Evaluation for <u>CO<sub>2</sub>retention</u> in patients with <u>lung</u> disease (e.g., <u>COPD</u>) receiving <u>supplemental O<sub>2</sub></u>

* Findings: ↓ <u>PaO<sub>2</sub></u> may be present

## Differential diagnoses

See also “<u>Differential diagnoses of chest pain</u>“ and “<u>Differential diagnoses of dyspnea</u>.”




### Pneumomediastinum [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HkaK6k)




* Definition: presence of gas (usually air) in the <u>mediastinum</u>

* Etiology

* Primary (spontaneous)

* Rupture of <u>pulmonary blebs</u>

* Predisposing factor: smoking

* Secondary

* Traumatic: blunt or <u>penetrating injuries</u> of chest and/or abdomen

* Nontraumatic

* <u>Boerhaave syndrome</u>

* <u>Bronchiectasis</u>

* <u>COPD</u>

* <u>Asthma</u>

* <u>Interstitial lung disease</u>

* <u>Lung cancer</u>

* <u>Iatrogenic</u>

* Endoscopy

* <u>Intubation</u>

* <u>Central line</u> placement

* Pathophysiology: ↑ intra-alveolar pressure → rupture of <u>alveoli</u> → air travel along the peribronchial and perivascular sheaths to enter the <u>mediastinum</u>

* Clinical features

* <u>Chest pain</u>

* Sudden in onset

* Retrosternal

* Radiates to the neck or back

* <u>Dyspnea</u>

* <u>Subcutaneous emphysema</u>

* <u>Cough</u>

* Voice change

* Rhinolalia

* Nasal quality of voice

* Occurs due to the presence of air within the <u>soft palate</u>

* <u>Hoarseness</u>

* Hamman sign: precordial <u>crepitation</u> that is audible synchronous to the heartbeat

* Diagnostics

* <u>CXR</u>  

* Air outlining the <u>mediastinal</u> structures (e.g. aorta, <u>trachea</u>)

* Visible <u>mediastinal</u> <u>pleura</u>

* <u>CT scan</u>: performed if <u>CXR</u> findings are inconclusive

* Management: usually <u>self-limited</u> when associated with <u>barotrauma</u>

* Clinical and radiographic monitoring

* Ventilation pressure reduction

* For traumatic causes, see also “<u>Approach to blunt chest trauma</u>” and “<u>Approach to penetrating chest trauma</u>.”

## Treatment

### Approach  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)




* All patients

* Assess patient stability (see, e.g., “<u>Stability criteria for spontaneous pneumothorax</u>”).

* Provide <u>respiratory support</u> and treat <u>dyspnea</u>.

* Evaluate the type and size of pneumothorax.

* Unstable or high-risk patients: e.g., with <u>tension pneumothorax</u>, bilateral pneumothorax, or who require <u>mechanical ventilation</u>

* <u>Immediate chest decompression</u>

* Treat <u>obstructive shock</u> if present.

* Stable <u>spontaneous pneumothorax management</u>: depends on the risk of progression and recurrence

* Low-risk: <u>conservative management</u>

* Higher risk: <u>chest tube placement</u>

* <u>Traumatic pneumothorax management</u>

* Most patients require <u>chest tube placement</u>.

* Follow the <u>ABCDE approach</u> to trauma to assess for additional injuries.

* Monitoring and disposition

* Consider the following consults : 

* Pulmonology: stable spontaneous pneumothoraces

* Thoracic <u>surgery</u>: <u>traumatic pneumothoraces</u>, large secondary pneumothoraces

* <u>ICU</u>: unstable patients or those with large secondary pneumothoraces

* Regularly reassess patients for:

* Clinical features of deterioration

* Development or recurrence of a <u>tension pneumothorax</u>

* See also “<u>Disposition of patients with pneumothorax</u>.”

Management of pneumothorax




> [!TIP]
> In every patient with pneumothorax who requires <u>mechanical ventilation</u>, immediate <u>tube thoracostomy</u> should be performed first.




> [!WARNING]
> <u>Positive pressure ventilation</u> can turn a simple pneumothorax into a life-threatening <u>tension pneumothorax</u>.




### <u>Respiratory support</u>




* Upright positioning

* Provide supplemental <u>high-flow oxygen</u> as needed (target <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> ≥ 96–100%)  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* If a patient requires <u>mechanical ventilation</u>, emergency <u>chest tube placement</u> is indicated.

* <u>Positive pressure ventilation</u> can turn a simple pneumothorax into a life-threatening <u>tension pneumothorax</u>.

* Decompression of a pneumothorax can sometimes rapidly improve <u>dyspnea</u>, making <u>mechanical ventilation</u> unnecessary.

* See also “<u>Airway management</u>” and “<u>Oxygen therapy</u>”.





> [!TIP]
> Start all patients without <u>risk factors for hypercapnia</u> on <u>high-flow oxygen</u> as soon as pneumothorax is suspected because <u>high-flow oxygen</u> aids reabsorption of the pneumothorax, which accelerates recovery.




### Spontaneous pneumothorax management




#### Stability criteria for spontaneous pneumothorax [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)




* All of the following must be present for the patient to be considered stable:

* <u>Respiratory rate</u> < 24 breaths/minute

* <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> (room air): > 90%

* Patient able to speak in complete sentences

* HR 60–120/minute

* Normal BP

* All other patients are considered unstable.





#### Treatment based on stability, type, and size




For stable patients, management depends on <u>apex-to-cupola distance</u>.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)




* Unstable (high risk) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDYMUr)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

* Suspected <u>tension pneumothorax</u>

* Emergency <u>needle thoracostomy</u>, followed immediately by <u>chest tube placement</u>  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDYMUr)

* Consider a <u>finger thoracostomy</u> if <u>needle thoracostomy</u> is unsuccessful.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDYMUr)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uccpVY0)

* Bilateral pneumothorax OR any patients who either require <u>mechanical ventilation</u> or do not meet <u>pneumothorax stability criteria</u>: Emergency <u>chest tube placement</u>

* Stable (low risk): <u>Primary spontaneous pneumothorax</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u> < 3 cm

* Usually resolves spontaneously within a few days (∼ 10 days) [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4AY3P7)

* Perform a repeat <u>chest x-ray</u> at 3–6 hours:

* Stable or improving appearance: Consider outpatient management (see “<u>Disposition of patients with pneumothorax</u>”). [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

* Enlarging appearance: Place a <u>chest tube</u>.

* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u> ≥ 3 cm

* <u>Chest tube placement</u> typically recommended

* Consider <u>conservative management</u> in otherwise healthy patients able to adhere to management instructions and experiencing : [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kAYmP7)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9TbNGG)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CTbqGG)

* An initial episode

* No <u>respiratory distress</u> or progression on repeat 4-hour <u>CXR</u>

* Needle <u>aspiration</u> may also be considered.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PnbWt8)

* Stable (higher risk): : <u>Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax</u> OR <u>primary spontaneous pneumothorax</u> with <u>risk factors</u> (i.e., age > 50 years, history of smoking, or recurrent disease)  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u> < 3 cm: Consider observation or <u>chest tube placement</u>.   [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

* <u>Apex-to-cupola distance</u> ≥ 3 cm

* <u>Chest tube placement</u>

* <u>ICU</u> transfer and thoracic <u>surgery</u> consultation





> [!TIP]
> <u>Tension pneumothorax</u> is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> and a <u>medical emergency</u> requiring <u>immediate chest decompression</u>.




### Traumatic pneumothorax management [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




* The treatment of unstable or high-risk <u>traumatic pneumothorax</u> (e.g., <u>tension pneumothorax</u>) is identical to the treatment of unstable <u>spontaneous pneumothorax</u>: <u>emergency chest decompression</u>.

* Consider observation only in hemodynamically stable patients with small pneumothoraces.  [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Dcc1eY0)

* A <u>chest tube</u> is required for all patients with any of the following:

* Suspected or proven hemopneumothorax

* Ongoing or anticipated <u>mechanical ventilation</u>

* Moderate to large pneumothorax

* Small pneumothorax with <u>hemodynamic instability</u>

* Suspected hemopneumothorax: Use a large-bore <u>chest tube</u>.

* Significant chest trauma: Assess for other thoracic injuries, e.g., <u>rib fractures</u>, <u>pulmonary contusions</u> (see “<u>Management of trauma patients</u>”).





> [!WARNING]
> As patients with a <u>traumatic pneumothorax</u> may have other injuries affecting multiple areas of the body, a full <u>primary survey</u> should be performed and <u>ATLS</u> management principles followed.




#### <u>Open pneumothorax</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZyYZd7)




* Immediately apply simple, partially <u>occlusive dressings</u> taped at 3 out of 4 sides of the lesion.

* Follow dressing with <u>tube thoracostomy</u>.

* Observe for development of <u>tension pneumothorax</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> Never pack the chest wound in an <u>open pneumothorax</u> as the packing may be sucked into the chest cavity during inspiration!




> [!WARNING]
> Do not tape the dressing on all 4 sides of the lesion because this can lead to a <u>tension pneumothorax</u> resulting from air building up in the <u>thoracic cavity</u> instead of being released during exhalation.




### Procedures




#### Emergency chest decompression




* Needle thoracostomy

* Indication: <u>tension pneumothorax</u>

* Procedure

* Immediate insertion of a large-bore needle

* In adults: 2<sup>nd</sup> intercostal space at the <u>midclavicular line</u> or the 5<sup>th</sup> <u>intercostal space</u> between the <u>anterior</u> and <u>midaxillary line</u>  [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/M1VMht0)

* In children: 2<sup>nd</sup> intercostal space at the <u>midclavicular line</u> or in the 5<sup>th</sup> <u>intercostal space</u> at the <u>midaxillary line</u> [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/M1VMht0)

* Typically followed by the insertion of a <u>chest tube</u>

* If initial decompression fails, consider using a longer needle (especially in muscular/adipose patients)  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OAYIP7)

* Finger thoracostomy

* Indications

* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u> with unsuccessful <u>needle decompression</u>

* <u>Traumatic cardiac arrest</u>

* Procedure

* Initial steps: identical to <u>chest tube placement</u>

* Difference compared to <u>chest tube placement</u>

* A gloved finger is inserted into the <u>pleural space</u> to create an <u>open pneumothorax</u>.

* No <u>chest tube</u> is inserted or secured.

#### <u>Chest tube placement</u>




* Indications: see “Approach” and “<u>Spontaneous pneumothorax management</u>.”

* Procedure

* Most commonly in the 5<sup>th</sup> intercostal space, between the <u>anterior</u> and <u>midaxillary line</u> (<u>safe triangle</u> )

* The <u>chest drainage system</u> may be used with or without suction.  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* Always check <u>CXR</u> after the procedure is complete.

* See “<u>Chest tube placement</u>” for detailed procedural guidance.





> [!TIP]
> Always check a <u>CXR</u> after <u>chest tube placement</u> or <u>needle thoracostomy</u>.




#### Needle <u>aspiration</u> [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9ccNeY0)[[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CccqeY0)




* Indication: stable patients with a large (<u>apex-to-cupola distance</u> ≥ 3 cm) spontaneous primary pneumothorax

* Procedure

* Insert a 16–18 g <u>IV cannula</u> through either of the following landmarks:

* 2<sup>nd</sup>or 3<sup>rd</sup><u>ICS</u>, <u>midclavicular line</u>

* 4<sup>th</sup><u>ICS</u> <u>anterior axillary line</u>

* Attach a <u>three-way stopcock</u> to the needle.

* <u>Aspirate</u> air using a large syringe until resistance is felt or the patient begins to <u>cough</u> excessively.





#### <u>Surgery</u>




* Indications  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* Recurrent <u>ipsilateral</u> pneumothorax episodes

* Bilateral or <u>contralateral</u> pneumothorax

* Persistent air leak or insufficient <u>lung</u> re-expansion for 5–7 days despite <u>chest tube placement</u>

* Extensive underlying <u>lung</u> disease

* High-risk occupation (e.g., pilots)

* Procedures

* Video-assisted thoracoscopic <u>surgery</u> (<u>VATS</u>)

* <u>Thoracotomy</u> if necessary

* Approaches

* <u>Stitching</u> of the leak or resection of the <u>lung</u> regions that have <u>bullae</u>, if necessary.

* <u>Pleurodesis</u> 

* Mechanical/surgical: pleural <u>abrasion</u>, pleurectomy (complete or incomplete)  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)

* Chemical/pharmacological (administration into the <u>pleural space</u>): talc powder , <u>doxycycline</u>, <u>minocycline</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-FYDkI)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/r6YfmJ)

## Complications

* Complete pulmonary collapse → <u>respiratory failure</u>

* <u>Tension pneumothorax</u> → cardiac failure

* Mediastinal flutter in <u>open pneumothorax</u>  → hemodynamic <u>shock</u>

* <u>Hemothorax</u> in cases of trauma

* <u>Pneumomediastinum</u>

* <u>Pneumoperitoneum</u>

* Recurrence

* Post-surgical/procedural complications

* Persistent <u>fistula</u> with continuous air leak

* Injury to <u>intercostal nerves</u> and vessels

* Infection
