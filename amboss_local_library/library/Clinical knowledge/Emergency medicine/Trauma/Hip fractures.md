## Summary

Hip <u>fractures</u> are classified as intracapsular (femoral head, femoral neck) or extracapsular (intertrochanteric, trochanteric, or subtrochanteric). <u>Hip fractures in older adults</u> are typically low-impact injuries and are often associated with <u>osteoporosis</u>. Hip <u>fractures</u> in younger patients are usually caused by a high-energy impact (e.g., <u>motor vehicle collision</u>). Clinical features include groin <u>pain</u> and deformity of the hip. <u>X-rays</u> are usually diagnostic, but an <u>MRI</u> may be required to diagnose <u>occult fractures</u> or <u>pathologic fractures</u>. Comorbid conditions (e.g., <u>anemia</u>, <u>acute kidney injury</u>, <u>delirium</u>) are common with hip <u>fractures</u>. Management typically includes multidisciplinary consultation, <u>early pain</u> management, IV fluid hydration, <u>venous thromboembolism prophylaxis</u>, and early surgical fixation. Nonoperative management may be considered for patients with severe comorbidities, although it is associated with a high <u>mortality rate</u>. Older adults are at the highest risk of <u>morbidity</u> and mortality; early involvement of geriatric care specialists is recommended for these patients. <u>Thromboembolism</u> and <u>osteonecrosis of the femoral head</u> are common severe complications. Hip <u>fractures</u> can be associated with <u>hip dislocation</u>. <u>Posterior</u> <u>hip dislocations</u> account for 90% of <u>hip dislocations</u> and typically follow a <u>dashboard injury</u>. Early reduction is vital to avoid vascular compromise and <u>sciatic nerve injury</u>.

## Etiology

* Mechanism of injury [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VR0GNf)

* Older adults

* Fall onto greater trochanter/<u>lateral</u> hip

* Forced <u>lateral</u> rotation (e.g., from tripping)

* Chronic overburdening can lead to <u>insufficiency fractures</u>, which can then completely <u>fracture</u> spontaneously.

* <u>Pathological fracture</u> due to <u>metastases</u>

* Children and young adults: high-speed trauma (e.g., <u>motor vehicle accidents</u> or falls from great heights) or underlying disease (e.g., <u>fibrous dysplasia</u>)

* <u>Risk factors</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VR0GNf)

* <u>Osteoporosis</u> (especially <u>postmenopausal</u> women and older individuals)

* <u>Muscle weakness</u>

* Difficulty walking and impaired coordination

* <u>Estrogen deficiency</u>

* Low body weight

* Poor nutrition (<u>vitamin D deficiency</u> or <u>calcium</u> deficiency)

* Smoking, <u>alcohol</u> use

## Femoral head fracture

* Occurrence: uncommon but often associated with a <u>posterior hip dislocation</u> following a <u>dashboard injury</u>

* Clinical features

* Groin <u>pain</u>

* Local swelling and <u>ecchymosis</u>

* Diagnostics 

* Hip <u>x-ray</u> (AP with <u>internal rotation</u> and <u>lateral</u> view; should include the <u>proximal</u> thigh) : abnormal trabecular pattern, cortical defects, shortening and angulation of the femoral neck  [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yf1dKT0)

* <u>MRI</u> if findings are unclear or if an <u>occult fracture</u>  is suspected

## Femoral neck fracture

* Clinical features 

* Groin <u>pain</u>

* Shortened and <u>externally rotated</u> leg

* Minimal <u>bruising</u>

* Diagnostics

* <u>X-ray</u> (AP and <u>lateral</u> view of the <u>pelvis</u> with <u>internal rotation</u> of the affected limb)

* <u>MRI</u> or <u>bone scan</u> if clinical suspicion is high despite absent findings on <u>x-ray</u>





| <br>Garden Classification<br> |  |
| --- | --- |
| Garden I | Nondisplaced, incomplete, impaction <u>fracture</u><br> |
| Garden II | Complete, but nondisplaced <u>fracture</u><br> |
| Garden III | Partially displaced, complete <u>fracture</u> with <u>medial</u> contact of the <u>fracture</u> elements and varus displacement of the femoral head |
| Garden IV | Entirely displaced, complete <u>fracture</u><br> |




* Treatment

* <u>Conservative management</u>

* Indication: stable, nondisplaced <u>fractures</u>, especially <u>abduction</u> <u>fractures</u> , mostly in debilitated patients

* Methods

* Temporary bed rest;  or use of crutches followed by mobilization with <u>physical therapy</u>

* <u>Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis</u> [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rMYfJp)

* <u>Surgery</u> (usually within 72 hours ; ) is indicated for <u>unstable fractures</u> , typically <u>adduction</u> <u>fractures</u>, and fragment <u>dislocation</u>

* For children and young adults

* Attempt preservation of the femoral head

* Early <u>open reduction internal fixation</u> (<u>ORIF</u>) (within 6 hours)

* For older adults: <u>total hip replacement</u> (<u>THR</u>) or <u>hip hemiarthroplasty</u>

## Trochanteric fractures

* Etiology 

* Greater <u>trochanteric fracture</u>

* Avulsion of the greater trochanter <u>apophysis</u> due to forceful contraction of the <u>gluteus medius</u> and minimus muscles (typically during physical activity)

* Direct trauma to the greater trochanter (e.g., due to a fall onto the hip)

* Lesser <u>trochanteric fracture</u>

* Avulsion of the lesser trochanter <u>apophysis</u> due to forceful contraction of the <u>iliopsoas muscle</u>

* Most commonly seen in physically active young adults, particularly those who participate in high-impact sports such as wrestling

* Clinical features

* Greater <u>trochanteric fracture</u>: local <u>pain</u> exacerbated by <u>abduction</u>

* Lesser <u>trochanteric fracture</u>: groin <u>pain</u>, which radiates to the knee or <u>posterior</u> thigh and worsens with hip <u>flexion</u> and rotation

* Diagnostics

* <u>X-ray</u> showing avulsion of the greater or lesser trochanter

* <u>MRI</u> if a <u>pathological fracture</u> is suspected or in individuals at risk of <u>fracture</u> extension (e.g., patients with <u>osteoporosis</u>)

* Treatment [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Typically <u>self-limiting</u> and <u>conservative treatment</u> suffices (e.g., no <u>weight-bearing</u> on the affected leg, ice, and <u>physical therapy</u>).

* Surgical repair in <u>fractures</u> with displacement > 1 cm

## Intertrochanteric fracture

* Clinical features

* <u>Hip pain</u> and swelling

* Shortened and <u>externally rotated</u> leg

* Significant <u>ecchymosis</u>

* Often associated with other injuries (e.g. other extremity <u>fractures</u>)

* Diagnostics

* <u>X-ray</u> (AP view with maximal <u>internal rotation</u> and <u>lateral</u> view): <u>proximal</u> <u>femur fracture</u> between the greater and lesser trochanters

* <u>MRI</u> if a <u>pathological fracture</u> is suspected

* Treatment

* Nonsurgical approach for high risk patients

* <u>Surgery</u>

* <u>Dynamic hip screw</u> (DHS) for <u>stable fractures</u>

* <u>Intramedullary nail</u> (Gamma <u>nail</u>) for stable or <u>unstable fractures</u>, <u>fractures</u> extending into the subtrochanteric region, or reverse oblique <u>fractures</u>

* <u>Arthroplasty</u> may be considered for <u>comminuted fractures</u>, <u>pathological fractures</u>, or if other surgical modalities fail.

## Complications

* <u>Osteonecrosis of the femoral head</u>

* <u>Thromboembolism</u>

* Infection

* <u>Chronic pain</u> and posttraumatic <u>arthritis</u>

* <u>Nonunion</u>

* <u>Dislocation</u>

* Nerve injury, e.g., <u>sciatic nerve injury</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Thrombolytic therapy</u> reduces the risk of <u>deep vein thrombosis</u> in patients with hip <u>fractures</u>.

## Prevention

* <u>Fall risk assessment</u>, for example with the <u>Tinetti-Test</u>, which is used to evaluate a patient's gait and balance

* Implementation of fall prevention strategies

* Early preventative efforts such as fall training, <u>physical therapy</u>, removal of tripping hazards, and appropriate shoes

* <u>Osteoporosis</u> prophylaxis
