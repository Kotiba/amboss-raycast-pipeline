## Summary

Radiography is an imaging technique that employs x-rays (high-energy electromagnetic radiation of a wavelength between <u>UV light</u> and gamma rays) to visualize internal structures of the body for diagnostic purposes. Conventional (projectional) radiography produces two-dimensional images of the object studied. It involves an x-ray generator projecting an x-ray beam towards an object. Depending on its density and structure, the object absorbs or scatters a portion of the x-rays. A detector situated behind that object captures the x-rays that pass through the object on photographic film or a digital medium. <u>Computed tomography</u>, which employs rotating x-ray generators and detectors to produce three-dimensional images, is covered in a separate article. Radiography plays a key role in the evaluation of thoracic and abdominal organs, bony structures, the <u>breast</u> (<u>mammography</u>), <u>blood vessels</u> (<u>angiography</u>), and the <u>urinary system</u> (cystourethrography, urography). <u>Contrast radiography</u> uses a contrast agent to highlight certain structures not clearly distinguishable from other structures on plain x-ray (e.g., <u>blood vessels</u>). Because of the health risks involved in exposure to <u>ionizing radiation</u> (<u>cell death</u>, <u>teratogenicity</u>, carcinogenicity), radiographical studies are bound to high safety standards (e.g., proper shielding) and should only be performed when medical need and benefit exceed the health risks associated. Accordingly, the threshold for indication is higher for children and pregnant women, while, generally, the <u>radiation dose</u> should be maintained as low as reasonably possible (<u>ALARA principle</u>).




A systematic approach to interpreting <u>chest x-rays</u> is covered separately in “<u>Chest x-ray</u>.”
