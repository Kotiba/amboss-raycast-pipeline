# Opioids

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Pharmacology > Analgesics > Opioids*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yN0ddg)

---

## Summary

Opioids are a class of natural (<u>endogenous</u> and <u>exogenous</u>), synthetic, and semisynthetic substances that act on μ-, κ-, and <u>δ-opioid receptors</u> as <u>antagonists</u> or <u>agonists</u>. They are distinct from opiates, which refer to <u>exogenous</u> alkaloids derived from opium, the dried latex of the opium poppy (Papavum somniferum). Opioids are commonly used to treat moderate to severe <u>acute pain</u>, and <u>chronic pain</u> refractory to <u>non-opioid analgesics</u>. In addition to their <u>analgesic</u> effects, opioids induce sedation, <u>constipation</u>, and respiratory <u>depression</u>, which represent potentially life-threatening adverse effects but also have clinical uses (e.g., as <u>anesthetic</u>, <u>antidiarrheal</u>, or <u>antitussive drugs</u>).




When prescribing opioids, <u>risk factors for opioid-related harm</u> and <u>risk mitigation strategies for opioid prescribing</u> should be considered. Opioid-<u>receptor</u> <u>agonists</u> induce a strong sense of euphoria, and their recreational use, both in the form of illicit drugs (e.g., <u>heroin</u>) and prescription drugs (e.g., <u>oxycodone</u>, <u>hydrocodone</u>), is widespread, with severe effects on <u>public health</u> and other aspects of society. Continued use of opioids can lead to physical dependence (the physical adaptation to the substance associated with symptoms of tolerance and withdrawal) and psychological dependence (<u>substance-seeking behavior</u> in response to biochemical changes in the <u>brain</u> from continued exposure to the substance; often referred to as “addiction”). Acute <u>opioid intoxication</u> is a life-threatening condition typically characterized by <u>altered mental status</u>, severe respiratory <u>depression</u>, and <u>miosis</u>. Treatment of acute <u>opioid intoxication</u> requires emergency measures and administration of a fast-acting opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u> (e.g., <u>naloxone</u>). Since the duration of action of <u>naloxone</u> is shorter than that of many opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>agonists</u>, a long-acting opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u> (e.g., <u>naltrexone</u>) should be administered after detoxification to prevent <u>opioid dependence</u> relapse.

## Pharmacology of opioids

### Definition




* Opioids

* Classically used to describe only synthetic and semisynthetic substances with opium-like pharmacological properties (e.g., <u>heroin</u>)

* Today used in the broader sense to describe any (i.e., natural, synthetic, or semisynthetic) substance that binds to opioid <u>receptors</u> (<u>agonists</u> as well as <u>antagonists</u>).

* Opiates: alkaloids derived from the opium poppy (e.g., <u>morphine</u>)

* <u>Breakthrough cancer pain</u> in <u>opioid-tolerant</u> patients [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nBW7YL0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/jHW_J50)

> [!WARNING]
> Improper administration of opioids may lead to rapid absorption and risk of <u>opioid overdose</u>. Extended-release and long-acting opioid capsules or tablets should be taken whole (without crushing or breaking). Transdermal patches should be used intact and protected from external heat.




### Opioid <u>receptors</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H90KKR)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/o5Y0Op)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XC09qR)




#### μ (mu), δ (delta), κ (kappa)




* Heptahelical transmembrane <u>G-protein</u>-coupled <u>receptors</u> (<u>GPCRs</u>)

* Binding <u>agonists</u> causes reduction of <u>synaptic transmission</u> through the following mechanisms:

* Presynaptic inhibition: closing of presynaptic <u>Ca<sup>2+</sup></u> channels → <u>hyperpolarization</u>→ ↓ release of <u>acetylcholine</u>, <u>noradrenaline</u>, <u>serotonin</u>, <u>glutamate</u>, <u>nitric oxide</u>, and <u>substance P</u>

* Postsynaptic inhibition: opening of postsynaptic <u>K<sup>+</sup></u> channels → <u>hyperpolarization</u>





#### Effects




* Effects of opioids depend on relative binding affinity of different opioid <u>receptors</u>.

* Mainly used as <u>analgesics</u>, but also used as sedatives, <u>antidiarrheals</u>, and <u>antitussives</u>  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QaXu49)

* <u>Pain</u> relief primarily via the two following mechanisms:

* Raising the <u>pain</u> threshold

* Change in <u>pain</u> perception

### <u>Receptor affinity</u>, <u>intrinsic activity</u>, and <u>ceiling effect</u> [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XC09qR)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/L5Ywkp)




#### <u>Receptor affinity</u>




<u>Receptor affinity</u> describes the extent to which a <u>ligand</u> binds to a target <u>receptor</u>.




* <u>Receptor affinity</u> does not always correspond to <u>potency</u>.

* In some cases, less potent opioids with higher <u>receptor affinity</u> inhibit more potent opioids with lower <u>receptor affinity</u> competitively, rendering them ineffective.





> [!WARNING]
> Opioids of different <u>potency</u> should not be combined!




#### Intrinsic activity (<u>efficacy</u>) [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Ab5mw)




<u>Intrinsic activity</u> is defined as the extent to which a drug activates a <u>receptor</u> after binding to it.




* Governs the <u>potency</u> of the functional response (e.g., <u>analgesic</u> effect)

* If substances with no <u>intrinsic activity</u> have higher <u>receptor affinity</u> than <u>agonists</u>, they may act antagonistically.





#### Ceiling effect




The <u>ceiling effect</u> describes the pharmacological phenomenon that once the therapeutic limit is reached, an increase in dose will no longer increase the functional response, but only the side effects.




* Full opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>agonists</u> (e.g., <u>morphine</u>)

* No <u>ceiling effect</u>

* Increase in dose always leads to increased functional response and there is no cut-off point.

* Partial opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>agonists</u> (e.g., <u>buprenorphine</u>)

* <u>Ceiling effect</u>

* At a certain point, an increase in dose does not increase the functional response, but only the side effects.





### Relative analgesic potency [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uAbplw)




* The <u>analgesic potency</u> of opioids is described in relation to <u>morphine</u>, which, accordingly, has the <u>analgesic potency</u> of 1.

* Higher <u>relative analgesic potency</u> allows for lower doses achieving the same <u>analgesic</u> effect.

* Values for relative <u>potency</u> differ depending on route of administration.

## Indications

### <u>Pain management</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)




* Opioids should only be prescribed if the benefits outweigh the risks and additional precautions are taken to minimize the associated harms (see “<u>Risk mitigation for opioid prescribing</u>”).

* The <u>WHO analgesic ladder</u> can guide the selection of the most appropriate <u>pain management</u> strategy.

* When appropriate, <u>nonpharmacological analgesia</u> and <u>nonopioid analgesics</u> are preferred for <u>pain management</u>.

* Dosages are detailed under “<u>Analgesics</u>” in “<u>Principles of pain management</u>.”





#### <u>Acute pain</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)




* General approach: The lowest effective dose should be prescribed and/or administered for the shortest duration possible.

* Common uses

* Acute severe <u>pain</u> (e.g., <u>ureteric colic</u>, <u>fractures</u>)

* Emergency <u>pain management</u> and sedation (e.g., <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>, <u>pulmonary edema</u>)  [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tAbXlw)

* Analgosedation during <u>surgery</u>

* See also “<u>Acute pain management</u>.”





#### <u>Chronic pain</u> and <u>palliation</u>




* General approach [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)

* Nonpharmacologic <u>analgesia</u> and <u>nonopioid analgesics</u> should be maximized as appropriate for the condition.

* For chronic noncancer <u>pain</u>, opioids should only be initiated if the improvement in <u>pain</u> and function is anticipated to outweigh the risks to the patient.

* Uses

* Commonly used in <u>pain management in palliative care</u>

* Limited role in the <u>management of chronic noncancer pain</u>  [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KGWUZM0)

* See “<u>Chronic noncancer pain management</u>” and “<u>Palliative pain management</u>.”

### <u>Cough</u> management




* Dextromethorphan (<u>DXM</u>): synthetic <u>codeine</u> analog used for <u>cough</u> suppression 

* Weak opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>agonist</u> and <u>NMDA receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u>

* Opioid effects are weak and mostly seen with overdose (can be treated with <u>naloxone</u>).

* Addictive potential is low.

* Inhibition of <u>serotonin</u> and <u>noradrenaline</u> reuptake increases the risk of <u>serotonin syndrome</u> when used with other <u>serotonergic drugs</u>.

* <u>Codeine</u>: taken PO

### <u>Diarrhea</u> management




* Loperamide: <u>μ-receptor</u> <u>agonist</u> 

* Can not pass the <u>blood-brain barrier</u> (low abuse potential due to lack of central opioid effects)

* Inhibits propulsive <u>peristalsis</u>, increases sphincter tone, and inhibits intestinal fluid secretion

* Adverse effects include <u>constipation</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, and <u>nausea</u>.

* Diphenoxylate 

* Inhibits propulsive <u>peristalsis</u>

* Only available as a combination drug with <u>atropine</u> to prevent misuse

* May produce central effects and toxicity at high doses.

## Opioid receptor antagonists

Opioid <u>receptor</u> <u>antagonists</u> bind to opioid <u>receptors</u> without activating them. <u>Antagonists</u> with high affinity to the opioid <u>receptors</u> can be used as <u>antidotes</u> in acute <u>opioid intoxication</u> due to their ability to displace opioids from the <u>receptors</u>.

## Contraindications

* Significant respiratory <u>depression</u> (e.g., due to intoxication with <u>alcohol</u> or sedatives; moderate or severe <u>sleep-disordered breathing</u>)

* Acute or severe <u>bronchial asthma</u> (unless in a monitored setting with access to resuscitative equipment)

* <u>Bowel obstruction</u> (including <u>paralytic ileus</u>)

* Lack of preexisting <u>opioid tolerance</u> (if relevant to specific opioid type and formulation)

* Hypersensitivity: true <u>allergic reaction</u> to opioids and/or compounds

* Patients with multiple <u>risk factors for opioid-related harm</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)




We list the most important contraindications. The selection is not exhaustive.

## Risk factors for opioid-related harm

There is an increased risk of <u>adverse effects with opioid use</u> in individuals with any of the following. Consider alternative <u>analgesia</u>, prescribing emergency <u>naloxone</u> (for overdose reversal), and/or additional monitoring. See also “<u>Prevention of opioid overdose</u>.”




### <u>Risk factors for opioid overdose</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0I1eYR0)[[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)




* Opioid-specific factors

* High dose

* High <u>potency</u> (e.g., <u>fentanyl</u>)

* <u>Long-acting opioids</u> (e.g., <u>methadone</u>)

* Patient-specific factors

* Age ≥ 65 years   [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)

* Concurrent use of <u>sedative-hypnotics</u>

* Prior <u>opioid overdose</u>

* Prescriptions from multiple providers and/or pharmacies [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0I1eYR0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZI1ZYR0)

* Release from incarceration in the past few weeks [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)

* Recent cessation of <u>medications for opioid use disorder</u> [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)

* Comorbidities

* <u>Renal insufficiency</u>

* Hepatic insufficiency

* <u>Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease</u>, <u>sleep-related breathing disorders</u>

* Mental health conditions (e.g., <u>suicidality</u>, <u>depression</u>, <u>anxiety</u>)

* History of <u>opioid use disorder</u> and/or other <u>substance use disorders</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Opioid-induced <u>CNS depression</u> is intensified when combined with other <u>sedative-hypnotics</u> (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, <u>benzodiazepines</u>).




### <u>Risk factors</u> for other harms [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w3WhkO0)




The following circumstances, when combined with opioid use, can lead to harm.




* <u>Pregnancy</u>   [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)

* <u>Breastfeeding</u>   [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)[[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/X619j30)

* <u>Biliary tract</u> disease (including <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>)   [[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2nWTsN0)[[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/roYfWJ)

* <u>Seizure disorders</u>  [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5tWiVM0)[[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MtWMVM0)

* Age ≥ 65 years   [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)

* Concomitant medication use that could lead to life-threatening <u>drug interactions</u> (e.g., the increased risk of <u>serotonin syndrome</u> when certain opioids are used concurrently with <u>SSRIs</u>)   [[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8GWOaM0)[[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xC1E8Q0)

* Safety-critical jobs  [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)





> [!TIP]
> Older adults are at increased risk of opioid-related harm due to decreased <u>renal clearance</u> (even in the absence of renal disease), increased risk of <u>medication error</u> (e.g., due to <u>polypharmacy</u> and/or <u>dementia</u>), and increased <u>drug interactions</u>. [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)




> [!WARNING]
> If opioids are prescribed in older adults, take care to mitigate common risks of opioid therapy (e.g., <u>medication review</u>, bowel regimens to prevent <u>constipation</u>, <u>risk assessment for falls</u>, and frequent patient monitoring for cognitive impairment). [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/FC1gFQ0)
