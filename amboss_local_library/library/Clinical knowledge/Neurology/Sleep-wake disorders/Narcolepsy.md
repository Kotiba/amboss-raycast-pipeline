# Narcolepsy

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kR0mMf)

## Summary

Narcolepsy is a neurological disorder of the <u>sleep</u>-wake cycle characterized by <u>excessive daytime sleepiness</u> and, in some cases, <u>cataplexy</u>, <u>sleep paralysis</u>, and <u>hallucinations</u> upon waking or falling asleep. It most commonly manifests in teenagers and young adults. <u>Primary narcolepsy</u> type 1 may manifest with <u>cataplexy</u> and/or <u>orexin</u> deficiency. Patients with type 2 <u>primary narcolepsy</u> have normal <u>orexin</u> levels. <u>Secondary narcolepsy</u> can occur as a result of <u>brain damage</u> or genetic syndromes. Diagnosis requires a history ≥ 3 months of <u>excessive daytime sleepiness</u> and either typical findings on <u>polysomnography</u> or an abnormal level of <u>hypocretin</u>-1 (<u>orexin</u> A) in the <u>cerebrospinal fluid</u> (<u>CSF</u>). There is no cure for narcolepsy but daytime sleepiness can be managed with optimized <u>sleep hygiene</u> and <u>CNS stimulants</u> or <u>sodium oxybate</u>.

* <u>Bimodal distribution</u>

* First peak at 15 years

* Another smaller peak around age 35

## Etiology

### Primary narcolepsy




* Narcolepsy type 1 

* Loss of <u>lateral hypothalamic</u> <u>neurons</u>, which produce <u>hypocretin</u>-1 and <u>hypocretin</u>-2 (i.e. orexin A and <u>orexin</u> B) → severe <u>hypocretin</u> (<u>orexin</u>) deficiency → dysregulation of <u>sleep</u>-wake cycles  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U7XbOz)

* The exact etiology is unknown, but both genetic and environmental factors seem to be implicated. 

* Genetic predisposition [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/BHYzsq)

* Narcolepsy type 1 is associated with a variation of the <u>HLA</u>-DQB1 <u>gene</u> called <u>HLA</u>-DQB1*0602

* Positive <u>family history</u> increases the risk

* Environmental factors: e.g., <u>streptococcal pharyngitis</u>, exposure to <u>H1N1</u> <u>influenza vaccine</u> Pandemrix  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IqcY-W0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqcf-W0)

* Narcolepsy type 2 

* <u>Idiopathic</u>

* No changes in <u>orexin</u> levels





> [!TIP]
> The <u>HLA</u>-DQB1*0602 <u>genotype</u> alone is insufficient to cause narcolepsy (i.e., without environmental factors).




### Secondary narcolepsy




* Cerebral damage (e.g., <u>tumor</u>, <u>stroke</u>, inflammation, vascular <u>malformation</u>)

* Genetic syndromes (e.g., <u>Niemann-Pick disease</u> type C and <u>Prader-Willi syndrome</u>)

## Clinical features

* Excessive daytime sleepiness (<u>EDS</u>): Affected individuals experience an irresistible urge to <u>sleep</u> and sudden, short <u>sleep</u> attacks (< 30 minutes), which may occur in inappropriate situations (e.g., while driving a car).

* One of the earliest manifestations of narcolepsy

* Can occur despite adequate <u>sleep</u>

* Abnormal <u>REM sleep</u>

* Cataplexy: sudden <u>muscle weakness</u> in a fully conscious person, triggered by strong emotions (e.g., laughing, crying) 

* Typically manifests months or even years after <u>EDS</u>

* The loss of muscle tone is similar to that observed during <u>REM sleep</u>.

* Typically manifests as partial <u>cataplexy</u>: isolated <u>weakness</u> of distinct muscle groups (e.g., neck muscles weaken and head tilts forward)

* Usually resolves within a few seconds, at most two minutes

* Sleep paralysis: Complete <u>paralysis</u> occurs for 1–2 minutes after waking or before falling asleep (either during a nocturnal or narcoleptic <u>sleep</u> episode, i.e., begins or ends with <u>REM sleep</u>)

* Sleep hallucinations

* Hypnagogic hallucinations: vivid, often frightening visual or <u>auditory hallucinations</u> that occur as the patient falls asleep

* Hypnopompic hallucinations: experienced while waking up (less common than <u>hypnagogic hallucinations</u>)

* Automatic behavior: During narcoleptic episodes, patients often perform routine repetitive tasks automatically without conscious awareness of their environment.

* Other: <u>depression</u>, <u>obesity</u>, <u>impotence</u> or low sex drive, <u>headaches</u>, decreased functional performance





> [!NOTE]
> Hypnagogic <u>hallucinations</u> occur while going to <u>sleep</u>.

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/INcYc10)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tja6-4)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tpcXrW0)




* Rule out other causes of <u>EDS</u>, e.g., insufficient <u>sleep</u>, <u>obstructive sleep apnea</u>, or other <u>sleep disorders</u>. 

* Consider using a <u>sleep</u> questionnaire, e.g., the Epworth sleepiness scale.

* Recommend a <u>sleep</u> log for 1–2 weeks and/or <u>actigraphy</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tja6-4)

* Review <u>sleep hygiene</u>.

* Assess history of stimulant use.

* Assess for <u>cataplexy</u> based on <u>patient history</u> and third-party reports.

* Order <u>sleep studies</u> to confirm the diagnosis and refer to <u>sleep</u> medicine.

### Diagnostic criteria  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/INcYc10)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tpcXrW0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yHYdGq)



* Daily periods of <u>excessive daytime sleepiness</u> for ≥ 3 months AND

* In type 1 narcolepsy, either:

* ≥ 1 episode of <u>cataplexy</u> PLUS characteristic findings on a <u>sleep study</u>

* OR low <u>CSF</u> <u>hypocretin</u>-1 (<u>orexin</u> A) levels

* In type 2 narcolepsy: characteristic findings on a <u>sleep study</u> without <u>cataplexy</u> or reduced <u>hypocretin</u> levels




### <u>Sleep studies</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i6cJkW0)




* Daytime multiple <u>sleep latency</u> test (MSLT) 

* Includes 5 opportunities for the patient to nap during the daytime and measures :

* <u>Sleep latency</u>: time needed to fall asleep

* <u>Sleep</u>-onset <u>REM</u> periods (SOREMPs): <u>REM</u> periods that occur within 15 minutes of falling asleep; also referred to as shortened <u>REM sleep latency</u>

* Characteristic findings

* <u>Sleep latency</u> ≤ 8 minutes

* ≥ 2 SOREMPs

* Nocturnal <u>polysomnography</u> (<u>PSG</u>)

* Measures <u>sleep</u> duration, efficiency, and stages

* Used to exclude other <u>sleep disorders</u> and may also show supportive findings for narcolepsy (e.g., SOREMP)





> [!TIP]
> If feasible, medications affecting <u>sleep</u> (e.g., <u>antidepressants</u> and stimulants) should be paused for at least two weeks prior to a <u>sleep study</u>. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i6cJkW0)




### Additional tests




* <u>Lumbar puncture</u>

* Not routinely indicated; may be used to diagnose type 1 narcolepsy  [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wrchjd0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9rcNjd0)

* Supportive finding: decreased <u>CSF</u> <u>hypocretin</u>-1 (<u>orexin</u> A) levels (≤ 110 pg/mL or < ⅓ of the <u>mean value</u> in healthy persons)  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/INcYc10)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tpcXrW0)

* <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u>: Consider if neurological findings suggest <u>secondary narcolepsy</u>. [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EJc8DW0)

* <u>Human leukocyte antigen</u> (<u>HLA</u>) haplotype testing: not usually useful for diagnostic purposes  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/S7YyOq)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/tpcXrW0)

## Treatment

### General measures




* Optimize <u>sleep hygiene</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Tja6-4)

* Ensure regular <u>sleep</u> periods during the night.

* Avoid substances that disturb the <u>sleep</u>-wake cycle (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, <u>antipsychotics</u>, <u>opiates</u>).

* Consider scheduled naps throughout the day to reduce the urge to <u>sleep</u>.  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/66cjNW0)





> [!TIP]
> As <u>motor vehicle collisions</u> are a concern for patients with narcolepsy, to be allowed to drive, they should be symptom-free and taking treatment. State regulations vary on the legally required period of time that patients should be symptom-free before driving.




### Medical therapy  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aNcQ-c0)




#### Principles of medical therapy




* Patients should be treated in consultation with a <u>sleep</u> specialist.

* The choice of medication depends on symptom severity, psychosocial history, and comorbidities.

* All first- and second-line drugs except for <u>methylphenidate</u> are effective treatment for <u>excessive daytime sleepiness</u>.

* <u>Sodium oxybate</u> and <u>pitolisant</u> are also highly effective for treating <u>cataplexy</u>.  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aNcQ-c0)

* <u>SSRIs</u> (e.g., <u>citalopram</u>) and <u>SNRIs</u> (e.g., <u>venlafaxine</u>): controversial role; no longer routinely recommended  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/66cjNW0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aNcQ-c0)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/swXt4Z0)





#### Commonly used agents




* First-line medications

* Modafinil: a nonamphetamine <u>CNS stimulant</u>

* Solriamfetol: selective <u>dopamine</u> and <u>norepinephrine</u> reuptake inhibitor that promotes wakefulness [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h7Yclq)

* Pitolisant: highly selective H<sub>3</sub><u>receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u>/<u>inverse agonist</u> for the treatment of <u>EDS</u> and <u>cataplexy</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/37YSlq)

* Nighttime sodium oxybate: a <u>sodium</u> salt of gamma hydroxybutyric acid  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/swXt4Z0)

* Second-line medications 

* Armodafinil

* <u>Dextroamphetamine</u>

* <u>Methylphenidate</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Sodium oxybate</u> (<u>gamma-hydroxybutyrate</u>) may cause life-threatening respiratory <u>depression</u> and should never be taken with <u>alcohol</u> or other <u>CNS depressants</u>. Since it is used recreationally to induce sedation and euphoria, <u>sodium oxybate</u> has a high potential for misuse. [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aNcQ-c0)




> [!WARNING]
> Stimulants recommended for treating narcolepsy may cause fetal harm and reduce the <u>effectiveness</u> of oral <u>contraception</u>. [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aNcQ-c0)
