# Spasticity

## Summary

Spasticity is defined as a <u>velocity</u>-dependent increase in muscle tone that manifests with resistance to movement and involuntary muscle spasms and contractions. It is caused by a lesion in the descending <u>motor pathways</u>. Common etiologies of spasticity include <u>multiple sclerosis</u>, <u>stroke</u>, <u>tumor</u>, <u>cerebral palsy</u>, and spinal or <u>peripheral nerve injury</u>. <u>Nerve conduction studies</u> and imaging of the <u>brain</u> and/or spinal cord may be requested to determine the underlying etiology. The management of spasticity is broad and may include physical and <u>occupational therapy</u>, pharmacotherapy (i.e., <u>muscle relaxants</u> such as <u>diazepam</u>, <u>tizanidine</u>, <u>baclofen</u>, or <u>dantrolene</u>; <u>paralytics</u> such as <u>botulinum toxin</u>), or <u>surgery</u>.

---

## Etiology

An <u>upper motor neuron lesion</u>, commonly caused by:




* <u>Stroke</u>

* Subdural or <u>epidural hemorrhage</u>

* <u>Cerebral palsy</u>

* <u>Brain tumor</u>

* <u>Multiple sclerosis</u>

* <u>Hydrocephalus</u>

* <u>Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis</u>

* <u>Hereditary spastic paraplegia</u>

* <u>Spinal cord injury</u> (e.g., trauma, <u>tumor</u>)

---

## Pathophysiology

* The mechanism of spasticity is not completely understood.

* It is thought that <u>upper motor neuron damage</u> leads to loss of descending inhibitory inputs → increased muscle stretch reflex → increased muscle tone

## Clinical features

* Increased muscle tone and <u>velocity</u>-dependent resistance to movement

* Elicited by <u>flexion</u> and extension of various muscles with alternating speed

* Clasp knife phenomenon: initial resistance (“catch”) when a limb is moved rapidly, followed by a sudden decrease in resistance; observed in patients with <u>upper motor neuron lesions</u>

* Upper extremity flexors and lower extremity extensors are usually more affected.

* Must be distinguished from rigidity

* Involuntary muscle spasms or contractions

## Treatment

* <u>Physiotherapy</u>: including splinting of the affected extremity

* Medical therapy: The most commonly used medication is <u>baclofen</u>; however, a variety of treatments (single and/or in combination) may be used and depend on patient-specific factors and patient response.

* Local therapy: intramuscular injections with <u>botulinum toxin</u> A, <u>lidocaine</u>, or <u>phenol</u>

* Surgical therapy: for severe spasticity refractory to medical treatment

* <u>Selective dorsal rhizotomy</u>: surgical destruction of nerves in the lower spinal cord in order to reduce muscle tone to the lower extremities

* Orthopedic <u>surgery</u>

* Lengthening of muscles and <u>tendons</u>

* Transfer of <u>tendons</u> to attachment points where they cannot contract the <u>joint</u> into a deformed position

* <u>Osteotomy</u> if muscle <u>contracture</u> has led to <u>joint</u> deformity
