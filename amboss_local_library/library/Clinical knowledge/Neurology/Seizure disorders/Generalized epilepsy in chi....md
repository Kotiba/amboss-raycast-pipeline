## Summary

Pediatric generalized <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> are a diverse group of conditions with onset in <u>infancy</u> or childhood. The International League Against <u>Epilepsy</u> (<u>ILAE</u>) classifies generalized <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> according to the etiology as either <u>idiopathic</u>, symptomatic, or cryptogenic. In <u>idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndromes</u>, the most common form of childhood <u>epilepsy</u>, genetic causes are suspected. The symptomatic forms are associated with metabolic or structural abnormalities, while the etiology of cryptogenic epilepsies is unknown. The syndromes are further classified according to the patient's age at onset, as well as clinical and <u>EEG</u> characteristics. A detailed <u>patient history</u>, including a description of <u>seizures</u>, offers vital diagnostic clues. The diagnosis may be confirmed with an <u>EEG</u>. With adequate treatment, <u>idiopathic</u> <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> have a good prognosis. In comparison, symptomatic <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> do not respond well to treatment and have a relatively poor prognosis, often resulting in <u>developmental delays</u> and cognitive impairments.

## Idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndromes

<u>Idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndromes</u> are the most common forms of <u>epilepsy</u> in children. Genetic causes are suspected and, in some cases, have been identified. The majority of these syndromes occur in otherwise healthy individuals.

## Symptomatic or cryptogenic generalized epilepsy syndromes

Symptomatic generalized <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> typically occur in association with structural or metabolic abnormalities, whereas cryptogenic generalized <u>epilepsy syndromes</u> describe <u>seizures</u> of unknown etiology. These syndromes do not respond well to <u>antiseizure drug</u> therapy and are commonly associated with <u>developmental delays</u>, as well as motor and cognitive impairments.
