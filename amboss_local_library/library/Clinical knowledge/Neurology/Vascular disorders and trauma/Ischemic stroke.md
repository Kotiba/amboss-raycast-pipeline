# Ischemic stroke

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Neurology > Vascular disorders and trauma > Ischemic stroke*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iJ0JFS)

---

## Summary

<u>Ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> is an acute neurological condition caused by impaired <u>cerebral blood flow</u> (e.g., vascular occlusion or systemic <u>hypoperfusion</u>). The most important <u>risk factors</u> are chronic systemic <u>hypertension</u> and cardiovascular disease. <u>Modifiable risk factors</u> should be managed (e.g., <u>primary prevention of ASCVD</u>, anticoagulation for patients with <u>atrial fibrillation</u>) for <u>primary prevention of stroke</u>. Clinically, <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> is characterized by the acute onset of <u>focal neurological deficits</u>, which are dependent on the cerebral territory covered by the relevant vessel. If <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> is suspected, a noncontrast head CT should immediately be performed to rule out <u>intracranial hemorrhage</u> and blood <u>glucose</u> should be measured as it is a <u>stroke mimic</u>. <u>Revascularization</u> of the vessels affected in <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u>, e.g., via <u>tissue plasminogen activator</u> (<u>tPA</u>) or thrombectomy, can preserve <u>brain</u> tissue and improve outcomes if given early. Further treatment consists of <u>supportive care</u>, <u>neuroprotective measures</u>, management of underlying causes, and <u>reducing subsequent stroke risk</u> with <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> and other <u>management of ASCVD</u>.




<u>Central retinal artery occlusion</u>, <u>transient ischemic attack</u>, <u>intracerebral hemorrhage</u>, and <u>subarachnoid hemorrhage</u> are covered in separate articles. See also “<u>Overview of stroke</u>.”

## Epidemiology

<u>Ischemic</u> <u>strokes</u> account for ∼ 85% of all <u>strokes</u>.




### Risk factors for ischemic stroke




* <u>Nonmodifiable risk factors</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R3alR4)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Q3auR4)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2-bT9w)

* Age ≥ 65 years

* Sex <u>♂</u> > <u>♀</u>

* African Americans, Native Americans, Alaska Natives, and Hispanics are at higher risk.

* <u>Family history</u> of cardiovascular or <u>cerebrovascular disease</u>

* Genetic disorders (e.g., <u>sickle cell disease</u>)

* History of <u>TIA</u>

* <u>Migraine with aura</u>

* <u>Low birth weight</u>

* <u>Modifiable risk factors</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R3alR4)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2-bT9w)

* Systemic <u>hypertension</u>

* <u>Hyperlipidemia</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Atherosclerosis</u>

* Cardiovascular disease

* <u>Carotid artery stenosis</u>

* <u>Atrial fibrillation</u>

* <u>Obesity</u>

* <u>Coagulopathy</u> (e.g., <u>protein C</u> or S deficiency), <u>hyperhomocysteinemia</u>

* <u>Heavy alcohol use</u>

* Tobacco use

* <u>Recreational drug</u> use (e.g., <u>cocaine</u> can cause cerebral vasospasm)

* <u>Oral contraceptive</u> use

* <u>Hormone replacement therapy</u>

* Sedentary lifestyle

* Suboptimal diet (e.g., high salt intake, inadequate fruit and vegetable intake)





> [!TIP]
> For both <u>ischemic</u> and <u>hemorrhagic strokes</u>, age is the most important <u>nonmodifiable risk factor</u> and <u>arterial hypertension</u> is the most important <u>modifiable risk factor</u>.




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

## Etiology

![Types of ischemic stroke](../../../../media/zd5cfaf4zouvrv9me6tly7cs.jpg)

Types of ischemic stroke




### Embolic <u>strokes</u> (∼ 20% of all <u>strokes</u>)




* Most commonly affect the <u>middle cerebral artery</u> (<u>MCA</u>)

* Dislodged <u>emboli</u> can affect multiple cerebral vascular territories simultaneously.

* Cardiac emboli

* <u>Atrial fibrillation</u>

* Atrial or ventricular <u>thrombi</u>

* <u>Rheumatic heart disease</u>

* <u>Ventricular aneurysms</u>

* <u>Atheroemboli</u> 

* <u>Internal carotid artery</u>

* <u>Aortic arch</u> (less common)

* <u>Infectious emboli</u>: bacterial <u>endocarditis</u>

* Paradoxical embolism: <u>venous thromboembolism</u> (especially due to <u>deep vein thrombosis</u>) in patients with right-to-left cardiac shunt (e.g., <u>persistent foramen ovale</u> or <u>atrial septal defect</u>)

* Spectacular shrinking deficit: <u>distal</u> migration of the <u>embolus</u> leads to recanalization of the affected vessel and rapid clinical improvement after symptom onset





![Pathophysiology of paradoxical embolism](../../../../media/lts909inzf8lgve3xtd4oisf.jpg)

Pathophysiology of paradoxical embolism




### <u>Thrombotic</u> <u>strokes</u> (∼ 40%)




* Large vessel <u>atherosclerosis</u> (∼ 20%) 

* Rupture of an <u>atherosclerotic plaque</u> and exposure of subendothelial <u>collagen</u> → <u>formation of a thrombus</u>

* <u>Thrombus formation</u> most commonly occurs at branch points in <u>arteries</u> (e.g., ; <u>internal carotid artery</u> bifurcation or where the <u>MCA</u> branches from the <u>circle of Willis</u>).

* Small vessel occlusion (e.g., <u>lacunar infarct</u>) (∼ 20%): see “Subtypes and variants” below.





### <u>Global</u> cerebral <u>ischemia</u>




* Systemic <u>hypoperfusion</u>




* <u>Shock</u> or bilateral large <u>artery</u> <u>atherosclerosis</u> (e.g., of carotid <u>arteries</u>) → decreased effective <u>oxygen delivery</u> to the whole <u>brain</u>

* Common during cardiac surgeries

* Can result in <u>watershed infarct</u>

* <u>Hypoglycemia</u>: Repeated episodes of <u>hypoglycemia</u> (e.g., due to <u>insulinoma</u>) increase the risk of cerebral <u>ischemia</u>.

* Severe and/or chronic <u>hypoxia</u>: <u>hypoxemia</u> (e.g., due to <u>respiratory arrest</u>) → <u>global</u> tissue <u>hypoxia</u> in the <u>brain</u>





### Other causes




* <u>Hypercoagulable states</u>




* <u>Inherited thrombophilia</u> (e.g., <u>factor V</u> Leiden mutation, <u>protein C</u> deficiency)

* <u>Polycythemia</u>

* <u>Hormonal contraceptive</u> use

* <u>Hormone replacement therapy</u>

* <u>Sickle cell disease</u>

* <u>Vasculitis</u> (e.g., <u>giant cell arteritis</u>)

* <u>Arterial dissection</u> (e.g., due to trauma or <u>fibromuscular dysplasia</u>)

## Clinical features

* Sudden onset of <u>focal neurological deficits</u> (e.g., <u>weakness</u>/<u>paralysis</u>, <u>paresthesias</u>, <u>aphasia</u>, <u>dysarthria</u>)

* Nonspecific symptoms (e.g., impaired consciousness, <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>headache</u>, <u>seizures</u>)

* Symptoms depend on the location of the <u>stroke</u> (see “<u>Stroke symptoms by affected vessel</u>” and “<u>Stroke symptoms by affected region</u>”)

* Symptoms suggesting a specific etiology:

* <u>Aortic dissection</u>: <u>chest pain</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>

* <u>Endocarditis</u>: <u>fever</u>, <u>heart murmur</u>

* <u>ICH</u>/<u>SAH</u>: sudden, severe <u>headache</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Stroke</u> should be ruled out in patients presenting with first-time <u>epileptic seizures</u> and subsequent neurological deficits, as the <u>seizure</u> may have been caused by an acute cerebral pathology.

## Subtypes and variants

### Lacunar infarct [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/P3aWi4)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bMYHMp)




* Definition: noncortical <u>infarcts</u> characterized by the absence of <u>cortical signs</u> (e.g., no <u>aphasia</u>, <u>hemianopsia</u>, <u>agnosia</u>, <u>apraxia</u>)

* Etiology  

* Most common: chronic hypertensive vasculopathy → <u>lipohyalinosis</u> of the small vessels → occlusion of small, penetrating <u>arteries</u> (e.g., <u>lenticulostriate artery</u>);   → <u>lacunar stroke</u> resulting in specific <u>lacunar syndromes</u> (see “<u>Lacunar syndromes</u>”)

* Less common

* Cardioembolic event

* <u>Microatheroma</u> formation

* Microbleed (rare)

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* Clinical features

* Acute transient <u>focal neurological symptoms</u> that often have a <u>stuttering</u> course

* Symptoms depend on the affected territory.

* See “<u>Lacunar syndromes</u>.”

* Commonly affected areas

* <u>Internal capsule</u>, corona radiata

* <u>Pons</u>

* <u>Basal ganglia</u> (<u>striatum</u>, <u>putamen</u>, <u>globus pallidus</u>, <u>thalamus</u>, caudate)

* Diagnosis: <u>diffusion-weighted MRI</u>

* Pathology: results in a <u>pale infarction</u> at the periphery of the cortex

* Treatment: same as for other <u>ischemic</u> <u>strokes</u> (see “Treatment”)





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Infarction</u> of the <u>posterior</u> limb of the <u>internal capsule</u> is the most common type of <u>lacunar stroke</u> and may manifest clinically with <u>pure motor stroke</u>, <u>pure sensory stroke</u> (rare), <u>sensorimotor stroke</u>, <u>dysarthria-clumsy hand syndrome</u>, and/or <u>ataxic hemiparesis</u>.




### Watershed infarct [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i3aJR4)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ES08Yf)




* Definition: border-zone <u>infarct</u> in the region between the territory of two major <u>arteries</u> that supply the <u>brain</u> (watershed area)

* Etiology: sudden decrease in blood pressure or cessation of blood flow through both vessels → <u>ischemia</u> in the susceptible region between two vascular territories

* Cortical <u>border zones</u>: territories between the <u>anterior</u> and <u>middle cerebral arteries</u> and between the middle and <u>posterior cerebral arteries</u>

* Internal <u>border zones</u>: territories between the <u>superficial</u> and deep branches of the <u>MCA</u>

* Clinical features

* Signs of systemic <u>hypoperfusion</u> (e.g., <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>low blood pressure</u>, <u>pallor</u>, sweating)

* Diffuse neurological deterioration

* PCA-<u>MCA</u> <u>watershed region</u>: bilateral visual loss (<u>cortical blindness</u>)

* <u>ACA</u>-<u>MCA</u> <u>watershed region</u>: <u>proximal</u> limb <u>weakness</u> with sparing of the face, hands, and feet (“man-in-the-barrel syndrome”)

## Initial evaluation and acute stabilization

### <u>Primary survey</u>




Clinical assessment and management should occur simultaneously with the goals of stabilizing the patient, keeping the door-to-<u>neuroimaging</u> time to a minimum, and identifying candidates for reperfusion therapy as soon as possible.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3DbSVD)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NDb-eD)




* <u>Airway management</u>: <u>Secure airway</u> if <u>airway protective reflexes</u> are impaired, e.g., due to depressed <u>level of consciousness</u> or <u>bulbar dysfunction</u>.

* <u>Respiratory support</u>

* Provide <u>oxygen therapy</u> to keep <u>SpO<sub>2</sub></u> > 94%.

* Consider <u>mechanical ventilation</u> for <u>respiratory failure</u>.

* <u>Hemodynamic support</u>: See “<u>Blood pressure management in ischemic stroke</u>.”

* Rapid focused <u>neurological assessment</u>

* Determine the time of symptom onset or, if this is unknown, the time the patient was last seen well or at neurological baseline.

* Identify <u>risk factors for ischemic stroke</u> and <u>risk factors for hemorrhagic stroke</u>.

* Minimum <u>neurological examination</u>

* <u>GCS</u> score

* <u>Pupillary examination</u>

* Identification of <u>lateralizing signs</u>, e.g., <u>hemiparesis</u>, facial droop, <u>pronator drift</u>

* Screening for signs of <u>cerebral herniation</u>

* Focused <u>neurological examination</u> 

* Attempt to localize lesion by identifying <u>stroke symptoms by affected vessel</u>.

* Perform severity assessment: e.g., <u>NIHSS</u>

* Assess for a central cause in patients presenting with <u>vertigo</u> (e.g., using <u>HINTS examination</u>). [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)

* Critical management steps [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/n9b7LD)

* Check <u>POC glucose</u> and treat immediately if < 60 mg/dL or > 400 mg/dL to rule out <u>stroke mimic</u>.

* Arrange emergency <u>neuroimaging</u>, e.g., noncontrast CT head, to rule out <u>intracranial hemorrhage</u>.

* Treat eligible patients with reperfusion therapy for acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> (e.g., IV <u>thrombolytics</u>, <u>mechanical thrombectomy</u>)

* Identify inclusion and exclusion criteria.

* Lower BP to < 185/110 mm Hg prior to initiation.

* Initiate <u>neuroprotective measures</u> and <u>supportive care for ischemic stroke</u>.

* Start <u>ICP management</u> if needed: Avoid <u>steroids</u>, <u>hypothermia</u>, and <u>barbiturates</u>.

* Consults and disposition

* Consult <u>neurology</u> immediately if acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> is identified.

* Consult neurosurgery for any <u>hemorrhagic transformation</u> or if there are indications for <u>ventriculostomy</u> or <u>decompressive craniectomy</u>: e.g., <u>cerebral edema</u> with refractory <u>↑ ICP</u>, large <u>cerebellar</u> <u>infarction</u> with obstructing <u>hydrocephalus</u>.

* Admit the patient to a dedicated <u>stroke unit</u> (or <u>ICU</u>) for at least 24 hours .

* Arrange immediate transfer to a <u>stroke center</u> if specialized care is unavailable locally.  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)

* Next steps: See “<u>Diagnostics of ischemic stroke</u>” and “<u>Treatment of ischemic stroke</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> Only <u>POC glucose</u> and noncontrast <u>neuroimaging</u> (e.g., CT head or <u>MR</u> <u>brain</u>) are required prior to <u>thrombolytic therapy</u>. Do not delay treatment to complete the remainder of the diagnostic evaluation. A classic clinical presentation without evidence of a <u>stroke mimic</u> or <u>intracranial bleeding</u> on initial <u>neuroimaging</u> is typically enough to diagnose acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> in time-limited settings. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




> [!WARNING]
> Malignant infarctions in the <u>MCA</u> territory or large PICA infarctions may require surgical intervention before <u>edema</u> reaches its maximum extent to prevent <u>brain herniation</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

### Blood pressure management in acute ischemic stroke [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* Provide <u>immediate hemodynamic support</u> for <u>hypotension</u> and <u>hypovolemia</u> (see “<u>Neurogenic shock</u>”).

* <u>Treatment of hypertension</u> in patients not undergoing reperfusion therapy

* BP < 220/120 mm Hg 

* Permissive hypertension: Moderate <u>hypertension</u> is tolerated within the first 48–72 hours with the goal of maintaining cerebral <u>perfusion</u>.

* Earlier BP reduction is indicated in patients with certain comorbidities, e.g., concomitant <u>heart failure</u> or <u>aortic dissection</u>, and after <u>thrombolysis</u>.

* BP ≥ 220/120 mm Hg: Careful BP reduction, e.g., by ∼ 15% within the first 24 hours of <u>stroke</u> onset may be considered.

* Patients undergoing reperfusion therapy: Reduce BP to < 185/110 mmHg beforehand and keep BP < 180/105 mm Hg for the first 24 hours after treatment.

* Recommended agents: <u>labetalol</u> DOSAGE, <u>clevidipine</u> DOSAGE, or <u>nicardipine</u> DOSAGE

* Once the patient is neurologically stable, start (or restart) oral <u>antihypertensive therapy</u> if BP > 140/90 mm Hg.

## Severity assessment

The following scales can be calculated at initial presentation to guide treatment decisions and estimate prognosis, or repeated to monitor progression and response to therapy during admission, <u>rehabilitation</u>, and follow-up. They are also used as <u>outcome measures</u> in clinical trials.




### National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (<u>NIHSS</u>) [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/iDbJVD)




* A severity score that quantifies neurological impairment for specific categories within the following broad domains:

* <u>Level of consciousness</u>, orientation, and ability to follow commands

* <u>Cranial nerve palsies</u>: e.g., visual impairment, facial droop

* Motor and sensory deficits or neglect

* Language impairment

* Coordination

* Scores between are assigned for each category are combined and totals can range from 0 (no impairment) to 42 (most severe).





> [!WARNING]
> The <u>NIHSS</u> is weighted towards <u>anterior</u> circulation <u>strokes</u> and underestimates <u>stroke</u> severity in the <u>posterior</u> circulation. [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PwXWQZ0)

### Modified Rankin scale [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LXcwAa0)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oXc0_a0)




* A scale used to quantify the degree of disability and dependence in daily activities before and after cerebral <u>stroke</u>.

* The scale ranges from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (<u>death</u>).

## Diagnosis

### Diagnostic approach  [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4wX3QZ0)[[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lwXvQZ0)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s9XtKZ0)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)




* Prioritize <u>neuroimaging for ischemic stroke</u> as soon as possible.

* Obtain initial noncontrast imaging without delay.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* CT head is usually the initial imaging modality

* <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u> is more sensitive for early <u>stroke</u> detection than CT , however, it is less commonly available and takes longer.

* Consider advanced <u>neuroimaging</u> in consultation with a <u>neurologist</u>, but do not delay reperfusion therapy if indicated.  [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4wX3QZ0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TXc6xa0)

* Identify any <u>stroke mimics</u> on <u>neuroimaging</u>: e.g., <u>brain tumor</u>, <u>brain abscess</u>

* Consider follow-up <u>neuroimaging</u> for patients with high clinical suspicion and equivocal initial CT and <u>MRI</u>.

* Order additional studies within the first 48 hours of symptom onset to determine the underlying etiology.

* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>ECG</u> and further cardiac evaluation (e.g., to identify <u>atrial fibrillation</u>)

* Neurovascular studies: to identify stenosis or <u>dissection of the carotid or vertebral arteries</u>

* <u>Anterior</u> circulation <u>infarct</u>

* Obtain noninvasive carotid imaging: e.g., <u>CTA</u>, <u>MRA</u>, or <u>carotid artery duplex</u>

* Consider noninvasive intracranial arterial imaging: e.g., <u>CTA</u>, <u>MRA</u>, transcranial doppler

* See also “<u>Carotid artery stenosis diagnostics</u>.”

* <u>Posterior</u> circulation <u>infarct</u>: Consider noninvasive imaging of intracranial and extracranial <u>arteries</u> (including vertebrobasilar circulation)

* If etiology remains uncertain (i.e., cryptogenic <u>stroke</u>): Consider advanced studies, e.g., prolonged <u>ambulatory ECG monitoring</u>, evaluation for <u>patent foramen ovale</u>, screening for genetic diseases or <u>vasculitides</u>





> [!TIP]
> Obtain noncontrast <u>neuroimaging</u> as soon as possible.




> [!WARNING]
> The decision to obtain advanced imaging should not delay the administration of <u>thrombolytic therapy</u> in appropriate candidates. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

### Cardiac evaluation [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)




All patients with a suspected <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> should receive an initial <u>ECG</u> and <u>cardiac monitoring</u>.




* All patients

* <u>ECG</u>: to evaluate for <u>atrial fibrillation</u>, <u>atrial flutter</u>, or other cardiac conditions (e.g., <u>acute myocardial infarction</u>)

* <u>Cardiac monitoring</u>: indicated for a minimum of 24 hours to detect occult <u>atrial fibrillation</u>

* Select patients

* Consider prolonged <u>cardiac monitoring</u> (i.e., 30 days) for suspected intermittent <u>atrial fibrillation</u> or <u>atrial flutter</u>. [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IHbY7E)

* <u>Echocardiography</u>: Consider in patients with suspected embolic <u>stroke</u> or symptoms of <u>heart</u> disease.

* <u>TTE</u> is the preferred initial modality.

* <u>TEE</u> may be added if the identification of a <u>PFO</u> , valvular disease, or <u>aortic arch</u> <u>atherosclerosis</u> will change management. [[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IHbY7E)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/75Y4lp)

* Possible findings include left atrial or ventricular <u>thrombus</u>, dilated or <u>restrictive cardiomyopathy</u>, <u>patent foramen ovale</u> (<u>PFO</u>), and cardiac <u>tumor</u>.

* Consider <u>cardiac CT</u> or <u>cardiac MRI</u> if embolic <u>stroke</u> of undetermined source (ESUS) is suspected.





### Additional investigations [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)




Consider these in select patients, e.g., to identify the underlying cause, assess the risk of recurrence, and evaluate comorbidities or complications.




* Guiding treatment and <u>reducing subsequent stroke risk</u>

* <u>Lipid profile</u>

* <u>Fasting</u> <u>glucose</u> or <u>OGTT</u>

* <u>Hemoglobin A1C</u>

* <u>Pregnancy test</u>

* <u>Liver chemistries</u>

* <u>TSH</u>  [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCX9qZ0)[[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J-Xsy00)

* Evaluating underlying cause

* <u>Thrombophilia screening</u>: consider if patient is < 50 years of age and/or has a history of <u>thrombosis</u>  [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)

* <u>CNS</u> infection screening: e.g., <u>blood culture</u>, <u>CSF</u> culture, <u>HIV test</u>, <u>VDRL</u>, <u>RPR</u>

* Toxicology screen: e.g., <u>blood alcohol level</u>, urine screen for <u>cocaine</u> or <u>amphetamines</u>  [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)

* <u>ESR</u>/<u>CRP</u>

* Others

* <u>Electroencephalogram</u> (<u>EEG</u>): Consider if there is a concern for <u>seizures</u>.

* <u>Chest x-ray</u>: Consider if cardiac or respiratory diseases are suspected.

## Neuroimaging

### Noncontrast CT head [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lwXvQZ0)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s9XtKZ0)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RwbliD)[[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kwXmQZ0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/u9Xp6Z0)




* Indication: all patients with a suspected acute <u>stroke</u> to rule out <u>intracerebral hemorrhage</u> and potential <u>stroke mimics</u> (e.g., tumors) and detect early <u>signs of stroke</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* <u>Stroke</u> protocols: can be paired with <u>CTA</u> head and neck in some centers.

* Findings: may be normal or show evolving <u>ischemic</u> changes over time  [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4wX3QZ0)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4ZX3b9)

* < 2 hours after event

* Usually, no signs of <u>infarction</u> are visible.

* In a large <u>artery</u> occlusion, there may be <u>hyperdense</u> occluded vessels (e.g., hyperdense MCA sign, which indicates an acute thromboembolic occlusion of the <u>MCA</u> ).

* < 6 hours after the event: in some cases, early signs of cytotoxic <u>edema</u>

* <u>Hypodense</u> <u>parenchyma</u> of infarcted region

* Loss of corticomedullary (i.e., gray-<u>white matter</u>) differentiation, especially in the <u>basal ganglia</u> and <u>insula</u>

* Effacement of the sulci: e.g., obscuring of the <u>lateral sulcus</u> (also known as Sylvian <u>fissure</u>) due to cortical swelling

* 12–24 hours after event; : <u>Hypodense</u> <u>parenchyma</u> starts becoming more clearly demarcated.

* 3–5 days after event: maximum extent of <u>edema</u> and <u>mass effect</u> [[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/z9XrJZ0)

* 2–3 weeks after event: Infarcted region appears <u>isodense</u>.  [[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-9XDJZ0)

* Chronic <u>infarcts</u> appear <u>hypodense</u> (<u>isodense</u> to cerebral spinal fluid) and well-demarcated, with negative <u>mass effect</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> Infarctions in the <u>cerebellum</u> and <u>brainstem</u> may be harder to detect with noncontrast head CT than infarctions in other regions. [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/W3bPht)

### <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RwbliD)[[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9AbNNw)




* Indications

* If readily available, <u>brain</u> <u>MRI</u> may be performed instead of noncontrast head CT. [[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ewb8kD)

* Unclear time of onset or wake-up <u>stroke</u>: to determine eligibility for <u>thrombolysis</u>

* Signs or symptoms of a <u>posterior</u> circulation <u>infarct</u>

* <u>Stroke</u> protocols: includes <u>T1-weighted</u>, <u>T2-weighted</u>, fluid-attenuated <u>inversion</u> recovery (<u>FLAIR</u>), and <u>diffusion-weighted imaging</u> (<u>DWI</u>).

* Findings: The appearance of early <u>ischemic</u> lesions depends on the time and imaging sequence  [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s9XtKZ0)[[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4CX3HZ0)

* Early acute (< 6 hours after event)

* Arterial <u>enhancement</u> or a <u>hyperdense</u> media sign may be visible.

* <u>DWI</u>: <u>Ischemic</u> lesion appears <u>hyperintense</u> within a few minutes (most sensitive modality).

* Late acute (6–24 hours after event)

* <u>T1-weighted</u>: <u>Ischemic</u> lesion appears <u>hypointense</u>.

* <u>T2-weighted</u> and <u>FLAIR</u>: <u>Ischemic</u> lesion appears <u>hyperintense</u>.

* Chronic <u>infarcts</u> can show variable signal intensity.

* Typical signal patterns can suggest an underlying etiology [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OCXIHZ0)

* Cardioembolic: multiple lesions in different vascular territories

* Large <u>artery</u> <u>atherosclerosis</u>: scattered lesions in one vascular territory





> [!TIP]
> <u>DWI</u>-<u>FLAIR</u> mismatch indicates hyperacute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> that occurred within the past 6 hours. [[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4CX3HZ0)




### Neurovascular studies [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eRaxN4)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)




* Goal: visualize intracranial and/or extracranial vascular occlusions and collateral vessels  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RwbliD)

* Indications

* Confirming the diagnosis in patients with high clinical suspicion for <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> and equivocal noncontrast <u>neuroimaging</u>

* Identify candidates for vascular recanalization

* Large vessel <u>revascularization</u> (e.g., <u>carotid endarterectomy</u>): e.g., patients with nondisabling <u>stroke</u> (<u>modified Rankin scale</u> < 2) in the territory of the carotid <u>arteries</u>

* <u>Mechanical thrombectomy</u>: patients with acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> presenting in a late time window (6–24 hours since time last seen normal)

* Timing: within 24 hours if not already done at admission

* Options: Choose modality based on individual patient risks and resource availability.

* Advanced <u>neuroimaging</u>

* <u>CTA</u> head and neck (most common): often directly follows noncontrast CT head in many <u>stroke neuroimaging</u> protocols

* <u>MR angiography</u> head and neck

* <u>Perfusion-weighted imaging</u>: to assess the presence and size of the <u>ischemic penumbra</u>  [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)[[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/s9XtKZ0)[[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kbbmFH)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ObbIFH)

* <u>Ultrasound</u> studies

* <u>Carotid artery duplex</u>: to evaluate extracranial vasculature

* Transcranial doppler: to evaluate intracranial vasculature  [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9wbNOD)

* <u>Digital subtraction angiography</u> (<u>DSA</u>): Invasive, catheter-based <u>gold standard</u> for cerebral vessel assessment  [[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/m9bVLD)

* Findings

* <u>CTA</u>/<u>MR angiography</u> [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RwbliD)

* Large vessel occlusion(s)

* Vascular dissection (e.g., <u>dissection of the carotid or vertebral artery</u>)

* <u>Carotid artery stenosis</u>

* CT <u>perfusion</u>

* <u>Ischemic penumbra</u>: reduced <u>cerebral blood flow</u> and blood volume

* <u>Ischemic core</u>: severely reduced <u>cerebral blood flow</u> and blood volume PLUS increased mean transit time

* <u>MR perfusion</u>: A <u>perfusion-diffusion mismatch</u> indicates the presence of a potentially salvageable <u>penumbra</u>.





> [!TIP]
> In potential candidates for <u>mechanical thrombectomy</u>, perform <u>CTA</u> immediately following noncontrast CT. If indicated, <u>thrombolysis</u> can be performed simultaneously. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




> [!WARNING]
> If indicated, do not delay <u>CTA</u> to wait for <u>creatinine</u> or <u>TSH</u> levels, as the risk of <u>iodine-induced hyperthyroidism</u> and <u>contrast-induced nephropathy</u> is relatively low, especially in patients with no known history of <u>thyroid</u> or renal abnormalities. [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XCX9qZ0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/u9Xp6Z0)[[53]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8zaOuM)[[54]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aCXQqZ0)

## Pathology

### Patterns of <u>necrosis</u> in <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> [[55]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUYkco)




* General: <u>infarction</u> → <u>liquefactive necrosis</u>;   → cystic cavity formation

* Two main responses to <u>brain</u> tissue <u>ischemia</u>

* <u>Selective neuronal necrosis</u>: more likely to occur with transient <u>ischemia</u>

* Pannecrosis: more likely to occur with irreversible <u>ischemia</u> (e.g., <u>infarction</u>)

* Irreversible neuronal injury begins ∼ 5 minutes after tissue <u>hypoxia</u>.

* See “<u>Cellular changes and adaptive responses</u>.”





> [!NOTE]
> <u>Infarction</u> of <u>brain</u> tissue is typically followed by <u>liquefactive</u> <u>necrosis</u>, in contrast to the coagulative <u>necrosis</u> seen after <u>infarction</u> in other organs.




#### Selective neuronal necrosis




* Definition: selective destruction of nerve cells with sparing of <u>glial cells</u>

* Mechanism

* Transient <u>ischemia</u> → subsequent reperfusion (e.g., resuscitation following <u>cardiac arrest</u>) → increased metabolic demand, release of toxic excitatory <u>neurotransmitters</u> → <u>ischemic</u> injury

* Certain <u>neurons</u> are more susceptible to <u>ischemic</u> injury. 

* <u>Pyramidal cells</u> of the <u>hippocampus</u>: Damage causes <u>anterograde amnesia</u>.

* Purkinje cells of the <u>cerebellum</u>: Damage causes <u>intention tremor</u>, <u>nystagmus</u>, and <u>ataxia</u>.

* <u>Pyramidal cells</u> of <u>neocortex</u>: Symptoms depend on the affected <u>brain</u> region.

* <u>Histology</u>: neuronal <u>necrosis</u> with viable <u>glial cells</u> (which can proliferate and cause a laminar or pseudolaminar tissue architecture)





![Selective neuronal necrosis](../../../../media/cvv9d9zzzqwdnszf5nz23a9w.jpg)

Selective neuronal necrosis




#### Pannecrosis




* Definition: the <u>death</u> of all cell types in a given region of the <u>brain</u>, including <u>neurons</u>, <u>glial cells</u>, and vascular cells

* Mechanism: permanent <u>ischemia</u>

* <u>Histology</u>: cystic lesions and loss of tissue architecture

## Treatment

### Therapeutic approach [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* Treat all eligible patients with reperfusion therapy for acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u> within recommended time frames.

* Continue <u>supportive care for ischemic stroke</u> including <u>neuroprotective measures</u>.

* Initiate measures for <u>reducing subsequent stroke risk</u>.

* Start antiplatelet treatment with <u>aspirin</u> or <u>clopidogrel</u> within 24–48 hours after symptom onset.

* Treat underlying conditions: e.g., <u>atrial fibrillation</u>, <u>carotid artery stenosis</u>, <u>carotid or vertebral artery dissection</u>

* Reduce <u>modifiable risk factors</u>: e.g., smoking, <u>hypertension</u>, <u>hyperlipidemia</u>, <u>diabetes</u>

* Monitor and treat any complications.

* Provide early <u>rehabilitation</u> and mobilization.

## Reperfusion therapy

### General principles [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* Identify eligible candidates and initiate treatment as soon as possible.

* Goal: to prevent further tissue <u>ischemia</u> and irreversible <u>infarction</u>

* Commonly used treatment options include intravenous <u>thrombolytic therapy</u> and <u>mechanical thrombectomy</u>.

* The <u>effectiveness</u> of intraarterial <u>thrombolysis</u> is unclear.





> [!NOTE]
> Time is <u>brain</u>! Reperfusion therapy should not be delayed. However, <u>intracranial hemorrhage</u> is a contraindication for reperfusion therapy and must be ruled out first.




### Intravenous <u>thrombolysis</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




Inclusion and exclusion criteria for <u>thrombolysis</u> are not strict and treatment decisions should be made in consultation with a <u>neurologist</u> taking into account multiple individual patient factors.




* Agents: intravenous <u>recombinant tissue plasminogen activator</u> (<u>tPA</u>)

* <u>Alteplase</u> DOSAGE

* OR <u>tenecteplase</u> DOSAGE  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)[[57]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/neb7zs)

* Inclusion criteria

* No evidence of <u>stroke mimic</u> or <u>intracranial hemorrhage</u>

* Acute disabling neurological symptoms

* Time from onset of symptoms or last seen normal (or at baseline)

* < 3 hours: patients ≥ 18 years old with any disabling <u>stroke</u> (including mild disabling or severe <u>stroke</u>)  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* 3–4.5 hours: select patients (consult <u>neurology</u>)

* Unclear symptom onset > 4.5 hours from last known baseline state (e.g., wake-up <u>stroke</u>): <u>MRI</u> findings determine whether <u>thrombolysis</u> is indicated.





> [!TIP]
> If a patient is unable to consent to treatment (e.g., <u>altered mental status</u>, <u>aphasia</u>) and a legal representative is not immediately present, IV <u>alteplase</u> can still be administered in eligible patients with disabling <u>stroke</u> symptoms. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




> [!WARNING]
> Do not wait on <u>coagulation parameters</u> before administering <u>tPA</u> in patients with no known history of <u>coagulopathy</u> or <u>thrombocytopenia</u>. Discontinue treatment if <u>platelets</u> are < 100,000/mm<sup>3</sup>, <u>INR</u> > 1.7 or <u>PT</u> is abnormally elevated. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

> [!TIP]
> Some conditions commonly misconceived as contraindications for <u>thrombolysis</u> therapy include <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>, <u>end-stage renal disease</u>, and concurrent <u>MI</u>. In patients with preexisting disability or <u>dementia</u>, treatment decisions should be based on prestroke functionality and quality of life. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[59]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C7dqLr0)




> [!WARNING]
> Severe hypo- or <u>hyperglycemia</u> (<u>glucose</u> < 50 mg/dL or > 400 mg/dL) and severe <u>hypertension</u> > 185/110 mm Hg should be treated before <u>tPA</u> administration. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




#### Complications of IV thrombolytic therapy [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* <u>Angioedema</u>: can compromise <u>airway</u>

* Stop <u>tPA</u> infusion and any <u>ACE inhibitors</u>.

* Secure <u>difficult airway</u>: See “<u>Management of angioedema</u>.”

* Medication protocol: differs from standard <u>treatment of bradykinin-mediated angioedema</u>. 

* Administer in rapid sequence.

* <u>Methylprednisolone</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Diphenhydramine</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Famotidine</u> DOSAGE

* If <u>edema</u> progresses despite initial treatment: Administer <u>epinephrine</u> DOSAGE OR <u>icatibant</u> DOSAGE. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* <u>Intracranial hemorrhage</u>: suspect if sudden neurological deterioration or <u>headache</u>

* Stop <u>tPA</u> infusion and obtain immediate noncontrast head CT.

* Order urgent <u>CBC</u>, <u>coagulation panel</u>, <u>fibrinogen</u> level, blood <u>type and screen</u>, <u>crossmatching</u>.

* Consult neurosurgery and <u>hematology</u>.

* Administer <u>cryoprecipitate</u> DOSAGE.

* Administer <u>tranexamic acid</u> DOSAGE OR ε-<u>aminocaproic acid</u> DOSAGE.

* Maintain <u>neuroprotective measures</u>.

* See also “<u>Intracerebral hemorrhage</u>.”

* Extracranial hemorrhage: Specific management depends on the site and severity of bleeding.





#### Additional measures after <u>thrombolysis</u>




* Check blood pressure and neurological status frequently.

* Avoid invasive procedures, if possible.

* Obtain a follow-up head CT (without IV contrast) or <u>brain</u> <u>MRI</u> 24 hours after <u>thrombolysis</u>, prior to starting <u>anticoagulants</u> or <u>antiplatelet agents</u>.





### Mechanical thrombectomy [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* Description

* Physical retrieval or <u>aspiration</u> of the occluding <u>thrombus</u> via the <u>femoral artery</u> using a <u>stent</u> retriever and/or an <u>aspiration</u> catheter

* Must be performed at a dedicated <u>stroke center</u>

* Inclusion criteria  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* Age ≥ 18 years

* Acute large <u>artery</u> occlusion;  causing a <u>stroke</u>: e.g., <u>proximal</u> <u>artery</u> occlusion in <u>anterior</u> cerebral circulation (M1) or occlusion of the <u>internal carotid artery</u>   [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/97dNLr0)

* Disabling <u>stroke</u> symptoms (<u>NIHSS</u> ≥ 6)

* Limited affected area on CT (<u>Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score</u> ≥ 6)

* Previously independent in <u>ADLs</u> (prestroke <u>modified Rankin scale</u> (<u>mRS</u>) ≤ 1)

* Patients with symptom onset within the past 6 hours or selected patients with symptom onset between 6 and 24 hours ago





> [!TIP]
> Patients who are eligible for <u>tPA</u> should receive <u>thrombolysis</u> immediately, while <u>mechanical thrombectomy</u> is being considered. If indicated, <u>mechanical thrombectomy</u> should be performed without delay to assess the response to <u>thrombolysis</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[60]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c7dakr0)

## Supportive care and neuroprotective measures

### <u>Neuroprotective measures</u>




Follow standard measures, including the following specific targets for acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u>: [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)




* <u>Targeted temperature management</u>: treat any temperature > 38°C

* Goals: Maintain <u>normothermia</u> and avoid <u>neurogenic fever</u>.

* <u>Hypothermia</u> is not recommended in acute <u>ischemic</u> <u>stroke</u>.

* <u>Glucose management for secondary brain injury</u> prevention

* <u>Hypoglycemia</u>: Treat if blood <u>glucose</u> is < 60 mg/dL.

* <u>Hyperglycemia</u>; : Treat to maintain blood <u>glucose</u> levels in a range of 140 to 180 mg/dL [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* Blood pressure: see “<u>Blood pressure management in acute ischemic stroke</u>” for details.





### <u>Supportive care</u>




* <u>Dysphagia</u> screening

* Keep patient <u>NPO</u> until the risk of <u>aspiration</u> has been assessed.

* See “<u>Dysphagia</u>” for further information on diagnostics and management.

* <u>VTE prophylaxis</u>: in addition to <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> and hydration [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

* <u>Mechanical VTE prophylaxis</u> is preferred: intermittent pneumatic compression (<u>IPCs</u>).

* Benefits of <u>pharmacological VTE prophylaxis</u> are unclear.

* Early <u>rehabilitation</u> and mobilization

* Start normal enteral diet within 7 days if possible.

* Order <u>physiotherapy</u>, <u>occupational therapy</u>, and <u>speech therapy</u>. [[61]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TxX6DZ0)

* <u>Screen for depression</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)

## Reducing subsequent stroke risk

Further therapeutic goals consist of identifying and treating <u>risk factors</u> and underlying conditions to prevent recurrent <u>stroke</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)




### <u>Antiplatelet therapy</u> [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)




* Starting within first 24–48 hours after symptom onset

* <u>Aspirin</u> DOSAGE

* OR <u>clopidogrel</u> DOSAGE

* <u>Dual antiplatelet therapy</u> (<u>DAPT</u>): Consider starting 21-day course in patients with minor <u>stroke</u> symptoms (e.g., <u>NIHSS</u> ≤ 3–5), <u>aspirin</u> DOSAGE AND <u>clopidogrel</u> DOSAGE  [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cCbaID)

* Long-term <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>

* Indicated in patients with noncardioembolic <u>stroke</u> to reduce the risk of recurrence

* Choose an agent based on individual <u>risk factors</u>, e.g., <u>aspirin</u> DOSAGE, <u>clopidogrel</u> DOSAGE, or combination therapy. [[62]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HzbK8w)[[63]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8WbOMs)

* Consult <u>neurology</u> for indication in patients with cardioembolic <u>stroke</u> and significant <u>atherosclerosis</u>.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)





> [!WARNING]
> Wait at least 24 hours before initiating antiplatelet treatment after <u>thrombolysis</u>.




### Management of underlying causes [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jmbsg8)[[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ciaaq4)




#### Large vessel disease




* All patients: Optimize medical management.

* Extracranial <u>artery</u> stenosis

* <u>Carotid artery stenosis</u>: Consider <u>carotid endarterectomy</u> or <u>carotid artery stenting</u> patients with relevant <u>ipsilateral</u> stenosis within 2–7 days of the event.

* <u>Vertebral artery</u> stenosis: intensive medical therapy advised (see “Treatment of <u>modifiable risk factors</u>”)

* Intracranial <u>artery</u> stenosis 

* 50–99%

* <u>Aspirin</u> DOSAGE is preferred over <u>warfarin</u>.

* Target BP < 140/90 mm Hg, start high-intensity <u>statin</u>, and advise at least moderate physical activity

* 70–99%: Consider adding <u>clopidogrel</u> DOSAGE to <u>aspirin</u> for up to 90 days

* Routine <u>angioplasty</u>, <u>stenting</u>, and <u>bypass surgery</u> are discouraged.

* <u>Arterial dissection</u>: treatment with <u>antithrombotic agents</u> for 3–6 months





#### Cardioembolic sources




* <u>Atrial fibrillation</u> or <u>atrial flutter</u>: anticoagulation with <u>warfarin</u> or <u>direct oral anticoagulant</u> is indicated regardless of frequency or persistence.

* Agents and dosages: See “<u>Anticoagulation regimens in atrial fibrillation</u>.”

* Timing: depends on risk of <u>hemorrhagic conversion</u> (specialist consultation advised)

* High risk (e.g., large <u>infarct</u> territory): Consider delaying > 14 days.

* Low risk: can start in 2–14 days

* Avoid concurrent oral anticoagulation and <u>antiplatelet therapy</u>, except in specific circumstances.

* <u>Patent foramen ovale</u>: Initiate <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> and refer to <u>neurologist</u> and cardiologist to consider <u>PFO</u> closure.

* <u>LV thrombus</u> or <u>cardiomyopathy</u> with left atrial <u>thrombus</u>: anticoagulation for 3 months

* <u>Valvular heart disease</u> and/or <u>prosthetic valve</u>

* <u>Valvular AF</u>: anticoagulation with <u>warfarin</u>

* <u>Mechanical valve</u> (mitral) and <u>stroke</u> prior to <u>surgery</u>: <u>aspirin</u> PLUS <u>warfarin</u>

* Other valvular diseases without another indication for anticoagulation: <u>antiplatelet therapy</u> typically preferred.

* Patients already on anticoagulation for <u>stroke</u> prevention: Consult <u>neurology</u> regarding management in the immediate post-<u>stroke</u> period.  [[64]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/x7dELr0)[[65]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/B7dzLr0)

* <u>Hypercoagulable states</u>: See “<u>Thrombophilia therapy</u>.”





> [!WARNING]
> Consult <u>neurology</u> for patients who experience breakthrough <u>stroke</u> while on <u>antithrombotic therapy</u>.




### Treatment of <u>modifiable risk factors</u> [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)




#### <u>Management of ASCVD</u>




* <u>Lifestyle modifications for ASCVD prevention</u>

* Smoking: Offer <u>counseling on smoking cessation</u>.

* Advise <u>smoking cessation</u> or reduction for those who cannot discontinue tobacco.

* Avoid second-hand smoke exposure.

* Consider pharmacotherapy: e.g., <u>nicotine</u> replacement (<u>gums</u>, patches), <u>varenicline</u>, <u>bupropion</u>

* Diet and exercise

* A <u>heart</u>-healthy diet, e.g., a <u>Mediterranean diet</u>, is preferred over a low-fat diet.

* Patients who can exercise

* Moderate intensity: minimum 10 minutes 4 times/week

* High intensity: minimum 20 minutes 2 times/week

* Patients who cannot exercise: Refer to <u>physical therapy</u> and <u>rehabilitation</u>.

* <u>Lipid-lowering therapy for ASCVD</u>

* No <u>coronary artery disease</u> or cardioembolic source and <u>LDL cholesterol</u> > 100 mg/dL: Start <u>atorvastatin</u> DOSAGE.

* Other ASVCD present: lipid-lowering therapy with <u>statin</u> ± <u>ezetimibe</u> to a target <u>LDL cholesterol</u> < 70 mg/dL

* Management of comorbidities

* <u>Management of hypertension</u>

* Recommend <u>lifestyle changes for managing hypertension</u>, e.g., reducing <u>sodium</u> intake.

* <u>Antihypertensives</u>: for patients with known <u>hypertension</u> or sustained average BP ≥ 130/80 mm Hg

* Target BP < 130/80 mm Hg  [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wXchZY0)

* Preferred agents: <u>thiazide diuretics</u>, <u>ACE-Is</u>, <u>ARBs</u>

* Optimize agent and dosage on an individual basis.

* <u>Management of diabetes mellitus</u>: glycemic control with <u>antihyperglycemics</u> as needed to maintain <u>HbA1c</u> < 7%





> [!TIP]
> The single most important treatable <u>risk factor</u> for preventing recurrent <u>stroke</u> is <u>hypertension</u>.




#### Other <u>modifiable risk factors</u>




* <u>Management of obesity</u>: Weight loss is recommended.

* Screen <u>BMI</u> of all patients.

* Provide directed <u>counseling on weight and diet changes</u> and referral to comprehensive weight loss program.

* <u>Obstructive sleep apnea</u> (<u>OSA</u>): Consider screening in patients with <u>risk factors for OSA</u>, and <u>CPAP</u> therapy for established <u>OSA</u>.

* Substance use

* <u>Alcohol</u> consumption: Eliminate OR reduce to ≤ 2 drinks/day for men and ≤ 1 drink/day for women.

* Provide <u>counseling on substance use disorders</u> and <u>rehabilitation</u> referrals for the following: 

* Patients using <u>CNS stimulants</u>

* Patients with <u>infectious endocarditis</u> due to IV substance use
