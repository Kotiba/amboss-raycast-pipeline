# Opioid withdrawal

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Neurology > Toxic and metabolic disorders > Opioid withdrawal*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ss0y8h)

---

## Summary

<u>Opioid</u> withdrawal syndrome (OWS) refers to the constellation of symptoms that can develop after a sudden cessation or reduction of <u>opioid</u> use following a period of prolonged chronic use. This condition is characterized by <u>sympathetic</u> hyperactivity, <u>flu-like symptoms</u>, and gastrointestinal symptoms. OWS is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> that can be supported using the <u>DSM-V</u> criteria; the severity of the condition can be scored using validated tools such as the <u>Clinical Opioid Withdrawal Scale</u> (<u>COWS</u>). <u>Opioid</u> replacement therapy with a long-acting <u>opioid</u> <u>agonist</u> (<u>methadone</u> or <u>buprenorphine</u>) and psychosocial support are the cornerstones of treatment. Management of <u>withdrawal symptoms</u> may help to bridge patients to outpatient <u>medication-assisted treatment</u> programs and provide an opportunity to reinforce strategies for <u>harm reduction in OUD</u> for overdose and relapse prevention. To prevent the development of <u>withdrawal symptoms</u> upon the discontinution of <u>chronic opioid therapy</u>, <u>tapering chronic opioid therapy</u> is essential.

---

## Clinical features

Symptoms are caused by sudden cessation or reduction of <u>opioid</u> intake after prolonged chronic use. The onset, peak, and duration of OWS varies primarily based on the <u>half-life</u> of the <u>opioids</u> used (e.g., <u>heroin</u> , <u>methadone</u> ). [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OscIEd0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MscMvd0)




* <u>CNS</u> arousal and <u>sympathetic</u> hyperactivity

* <u>Tachycardia</u>, <u>hypertension</u>

* <u>Anxiety</u>, <u>insomnia</u>, irritability, <u>agitation</u>  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KscUDd0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l01vT20)

* <u>Mydriasis</u>, yawning, <u>lacrimation</u>, sneezing

* <u>Hyperreflexia</u>, muscle cramps

* <u>Flu-like symptoms</u>

* <u>Rhinorrhea</u>, <u>diaphoresis</u>, <u>piloerection</u>, ; <u>thermoregulation</u> disturbances (e.g., chills)

* <u>Myalgia</u>, <u>arthralgia</u>

* Gastrointestinal symptoms

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>

* Abdominal <u>pain</u>





> [!TIP]
> Use of <u>short-acting opioids</u> is associated with a higher risk of severe OWS compared to use of <u>long-acting opioids</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KscUDd0)




> [!TIP]
> OWS can be severely uncomfortable, but it is generally not fatal unless the affected individual experiences <u>severe dehydration</u> and <u>electrolyte</u> disturbances that are left untreated. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kscmEd0)

## Management

### Approach [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l01vT20)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)




* Offer pharmacological therapy

* Acute <u>opioid</u> withdrawal: <u>methadone</u>/<u>buprenorphine</u> alone or in combination with symptom-based therapy

* <u>Maintenance therapy</u> (for <u>OUD</u>): <u>methadone</u>, <u>buprenorphine</u>, or <u>naltrexone</u> (see “<u>Management of opioid use disorder</u>” for details)

* Start general supportive measures

* Psychosocial support (e.g., <u>shared decision making</u>)

* Hydration (e.g., <u>IV fluids</u>), <u>electrolyte replacement</u>, <u>nutritional support</u>

* Strategies for <u>harm reduction in OUD</u> (e.g., <u>naloxone for opioid overdose</u> reversal)

* Choose an appropriate <u>withdrawal management (WM) setting</u> (i.e., <u>hospital WM</u>, intensive <u>outpatient WM</u>, <u>outpatient WM</u>)

* Choice depends on patient characteristics.

* Forced withdrawal is unethical in any setting.

* Consider special patient groups: Involve specialists for pregnant individuals, incarcerated individuals, and those with comorbid <u>chronic pain</u>.





> [!WARNING]
> Hospital admission is indicated for patients with severe <u>opioid</u> withdrawal who are unstable or have significant comorbidities (e.g., <u>sepsis</u>, polysubstance intoxication).




### Psychosocial support [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nsc-Ed0)




* Apply a <u>trauma-informed approach</u>.

* Engage patients with <u>motivational interviewing</u> and <u>shared decision making</u>.

* Inquire about the following:

* Prior experiences with <u>opioid</u> withdrawal

* Concerns about uncontrolled withdrawal and <u>pain</u> symptoms  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/l01vT20)





> [!TIP]
> The experience of withdrawal is strongly influenced by psychological <u>distress</u>, e.g., <u>anxiety</u> and concerns about <u>withdrawal symptoms</u> being unaddressed. [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4d13Jf0)




> [!WARNING]
> A patient who declines to engage in <u>shared decision making</u> should not be excluded from prompt initiation of appropriate medication management. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Xsc9td0)

## Special patient groups

### Neonatal abstinence syndrome [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6yajTM)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pyaLTM)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/65YjOp)




<u>Neonatal abstinence syndrome</u> is caused by maternal drug use during <u>pregnancy</u> (typically <u>opioids</u>) that subsequently leads to a withdrawal reaction in the <u>infant</u>.




#### Clinical features




* <u>Flu-like symptoms</u>: <u>fever</u> and sweating

* Gastrointestinal symptoms

* Poor feeding

* <u>Vomiting</u>, <u>diarrhea</u>

* <u>Failure to thrive</u>

* Respiratory symptoms

* <u>Tachypnea</u>, <u>apnea</u>

* Sneezing, nasal congestion

* <u>Sympathetic</u> hyperactivity: <u>hypertension</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>

* <u>CNS</u> stimulation

* High pitched crying, irritability

* Muscle tone and movement disorders (e.g., <u>hyperreflexia</u>, <u>tremor</u>, jerking)

* <u>Seizures</u>

* Uncoordinated <u>sucking reflexes</u>

* Disturbance of <u>sleep</u>-wake rhythm





#### Treatment [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/65YjOp)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IAbYOw)




* Supportive: the preferred method of management because pharmacological treatment is associated with side effects, longer hospitalization, and increased risk of infection

* Swaddling

* <u>Fluid resuscitation</u>

* Reduced sensory stimulation (e.g., quiet room, no sudden movements)

* Pharmacological 

* First-line

* <u>Buprenorphine</u>

* <u>Methadone</u>

* <u>Morphine</u>

* Second-line

* <u>Phenobarbital</u>

* <u>Clonidine</u>
