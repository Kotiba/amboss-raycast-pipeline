# Cholinergic poisoning

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Neurology > Toxic and metabolic disorders > Cholinergic poisoning*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/I80YL3)

---

## Summary

Cholinergic <u>poisoning</u> is most commonly caused by exposure to substances that decrease <u>acetylcholinesterase</u> activity, increasing the concentration of <u>acetylcholine</u> at muscarinic and <u>nicotinic receptors</u>. <u>Organophosphorus compounds</u> such as insecticides and <u>nerve agents</u> are the most likely substances to cause life-threatening <u>poisoning</u>. Classic symptoms include <u>miosis</u>, <u>bronchospasm</u>, bronchorrhea, <u>diarrhea</u>, <u>diaphoresis</u>, and increased <u>lacrimation</u> and salivation. Severe <u>poisoning</u> results in profound <u>muscle weakness</u> and <u>respiratory failure</u>, usually within minutes to hours after exposure. <u>Diagnosis of cholinergic poisoning</u> is typically based on history and <u>physical exam</u>. Decreased levels of <u>acetylcholinesterase</u> and/or <u>butyrylcholinesterase</u> are <u>surrogate</u> markers for cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>, but these tests are not often readily available and reporting of results may be delayed. <u>Treatment of cholinergic poisoning</u> includes <u>respiratory support</u>, high doses of <u>atropine</u>, <u>oxime</u> therapy, and extensive <u>body surface decontamination</u>. Providers caring for these patients must wear <u>personal protective equipment</u> at all times to prevent personal contamination and secondary <u>poisoning</u>.

## Etiology

### Insecticides [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5xWiCn0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MxWMCn0)




Individuals may be exposed to these agents unintentionally, e.g., while working in a field treated with the chemical, or intentionally for self-harm. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




* Organophosphorus compounds: irreversibly inhibit <u>acetylcholinesterase</u>

* Parathion (<u>E605</u>)

* Broad spectrum insecticide used primarily in agriculture

* Persists in the environment for days to weeks

* Garlic-like or petrol-like odor

* <u>Malathion</u>

* Broad-spectrum insecticide currently used in agriculture, gardening, and for mosquito abatement

* Used topically for the treatment of head <u>lice</u>

* Tetrachlorvinphos: found in <u>flea</u> and tick treatments and collars

* Carbamates: reversibly inhibits <u>acetylcholinesterase</u>





### Nerve agents [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kDWmen0)




These potent <u>organophosphorus compounds</u> were developed for use in chemical warfare, large-scale terror attacks, and, more recently, targeted assassinations. They irreversibly inhibit <u>acetylcholinesterase</u>.




* Sarin

* Volatile liquid <u>organophosphate</u> that is odorless, tasteless, and easily vaporized

* Much higher <u>potency</u> than <u>organophosphate</u> insecticides

* VX [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LxWwCn0)

* Amber-colored oily liquid with low volatility

* Usually absorbed through the <u>skin</u> but droplets can also be inhaled

* Persists in the environment after deployment

* Novichok agents [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nxW7Cn0)

* A new class of weaponized <u>organophosphorus compounds</u> that are more potent than VX

* Have been used to intentionally poison individual people





### <u>Parasympathomimetic drug</u> overdose




<u>Parasympathomimetic drugs</u> are commonly prescribed for a variety of diseases, e.g., <u>myasthenia gravis</u>, <u>glaucoma</u>, <u>Alzheimer disease</u>, <u>Sjogren syndrome</u>, <u>postoperative ileus</u>, and <u>neurogenic bladder</u>.




* <u>Direct parasympathomimetics</u>: e.g., <u>pilocarpine</u>, <u>methacholine</u>

* <u>Indirect parasympathomimetics</u> (usually reversible <u>acetylcholinesterase inhibitors</u>): e.g., <u>physostigmine</u>, <u>neostigmine</u>, <u>pyridostigmine</u>, <u>edrophonium</u>, <u>rivastigmine</u>, <u>donepezil</u>, <u>galantamine</u>





### Other




* Cholinergic <u>mushroom poisoning</u> [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MDWMUn0)

* An uncommon type of <u>mushroom poisoning</u>

* Most commonly caused by Clitiocybe and Inocybe species

* <u>Nicotine poisoning</u>  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_xW5_n0)

## Pathophysiology

* Absorption of the causative agent through the <u>skin</u>, respiratory system, or <u>gastrointestinal tract</u> → inhibition of <u>acetylcholinesterase</u> → ↑ <u>acetylcholine</u> levels → hyperstimulation of muscarinic and <u>nicotinic acetylcholine receptors</u> ; → life-threatening overactivation of the <u>parasympathetic nervous system</u> → <u>bronchospasm</u>, <u>bradycardia</u>, <u>weakness</u>, and <u>miosis</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Many agents also cause a non-cholinergic-mediated neural inflammatory response, which leads to early <u>depression</u> of the central <u>respiratory drive</u> and/or chronic neurocognitive deficits; see “<u>Gulf War illness</u>.” [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ES18Yg0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IxWYBn0)

## Clinical features

Cholinergic <u>toxidrome</u>  is an acute manifestation of cholinergic <u>poisoning</u> that occurs minutes to hours after exposure. Manifestations of both muscarinic and <u>nicotinic receptor</u> hyperactivation are present with the clinical presentation varies based on the agent, route of absorption, total dose, and time since exposure. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kDWmen0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




### Muscarinic hyperstimulation




* <u>Diarrhea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>

* <u>Urinary incontinence</u>

* <u>Miosis</u>, blurred <u>vision</u>

* <u>Bronchospasm</u>, bronchorrhea

* <u>Bradycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>

* Increased <u>lacrimation</u> and salivation

* <u>Diaphoresis</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Miosis</u> is the most common feature of cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




> [!WARNING]
> Though the clinical features of muscarinic hyperstimulation usually predominate in cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>, nicotinic hyperstimulation can cause contrasting symptoms of <u>mydriasis</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, and <u>hypertension</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




### Nicotinic hyperstimulation




* <u>Fasciculations</u>

* <u>Muscle weakness</u>

* <u>Respiratory failure</u> secondary to <u>paralysis</u>





### <u>CNS</u> effects




* Confusion, <u>agitation</u>, <u>tremors</u>, <u>seizures</u>, <u>lethargy</u>, <u>coma</u>

* Decreased <u>respiratory drive</u>





> [!NOTE]
> “DUMBBBELLSS” is an acronym for the clinical features of cholinergic <u>toxidrome</u>: Diarrhea, Urination, Miosis, Bronchospasm, Bradycardia, Bronchorrhea, Emesis, Lacrimation, Lethargy, Sweating, Salivation.




> [!NOTE]
> “SLUDGE-M” is another acronym for the clinical features of the cholinergic <u>toxidrome</u>: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI upset, Emesis, Miosis.

## Diagnosis

Cholinergic <u>poisoning</u> is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> based on the presence of cholinergic <u>toxidrome</u> and potential exposure to a poison; confirmatory studies may be obtained but empiric treatment should not be delayed. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




### Supportive studies [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




#### Atropine challenge test [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-BWDWL0)




* Improvement in cholinergic symptoms after <u>atropine</u> administration suggests cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>.

* Expert opinion differs on the diagnostic utility of the test; criticisms include: [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Patients with severe <u>poisoning</u> may not experience improvement in cholinergic symptoms.

* <u>Atropine</u> can cause antimuscarinic symptoms in patients without cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>.





#### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




<u>Laboratory studies</u> are used to assess for secondary complications and guide subsequent management.




* <u>CBC</u>

* <u>BMP</u>

* <u>ECG</u>

* <u>Chest x-ray</u>





### Confirmatory studies [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Confirmatory studies must be performed at regional reference laboratories, and the results are unlikely to be available in time to guide initial management. Trends in enzyme activity may help guide ongoing therapy. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Cholinesterase</u> activity assays: Decreased enzyme activity is an indirect marker of cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>.

* <u>Red blood cell</u> <u>acetylcholinesterase</u> activity assay

* <u>Butyrylcholinesterase</u> activity assay

* Serum or urine assays for specific cholinergic compounds or their metabolites

## Treatment

### Approach [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




* Don <u>level C personal protective equipment</u>.

* Follow the <u>ABCDE approach in poisoning</u>, especially:

* Monitor closely for <u>signs of impending respiratory failure</u>.

* Consider early <u>intubation</u> in patients with <u>muscle weakness</u> to reduce the risk of <u>aspiration</u>.  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SnXysA)

* Avoid <u>succinylcholine</u> for <u>rapid sequence intubation</u>.

* Initiate <u>atropine</u> therapy as quickly as possible.

* Initiate <u>oxime</u> therapy (e.g., <u>pralidoxime</u>) with, or shortly after, <u>atropine</u>.

* <u>Manage agitation</u> and <u>toxic seizures</u> with <u>benzodiazepines</u>.

* Begin thorough decontamination of the patient.

* Follow protocols for <u>body surface decontamination</u> (if not already performed in the field).

* Consider <u>single-dose activated charcoal</u> in intubated patients if the poison was ingested. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Dispose of contaminated clothes and shoes following local hazardous material protocols.

* Call poison control: In the US, the national Poison Help line is 1-800-222-1222.





> [!TIP]
> Always wear <u>personal protective equipment</u> when caring for patients with potential exposure to <u>organophosphorus compounds</u> or other <u>nerve agents</u>. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




> [!WARNING]
> <u>Respiratory failure</u> is the leading <u>cause of death</u> in cholinergic <u>poisoning</u>; initial treatment should focus on <u>airway management</u> and <u>respiratory support</u>. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ES18Yg0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




### Atropine for cholinergic poisoning [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Mechanism

* Acts as a <u>competitive inhibitor</u> of <u>muscarinic receptors</u> to reverse hyperstimulation

* No effect on <u>nicotinic receptor</u> hyperstimulation

* Dosing and administration

* <u>Loading dose</u>(s): <u>atropine</u> (<u>off-label</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eJdxGI0)

* Adults and <u>adolescents</u>: <u>atropine</u> 1–3 mg IV

* Children: <u>atropine</u> 0.05–0.1 mg/kg IV[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eJdxGI0)

* Double and repeat the previous dose every 3–5 minutes until <u>heart rate</u> is > 80 bpm, systolic blood pressure is > 80 mm Hg, and chest sounds are clear.[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/eJdxGI0)

* <u>Maintenance dose</u>: <u>atropine</u> (<u>off-label</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)
* <u>Atropine</u> 10–20% of the total <u>loading dose</u> of <u>atropine</u> per hour as a continuous IV infusion (max. 2 mg/hour)

* Autoinjectors containing fixed amounts of <u>atropine</u> and/or <u>pralidoxime</u> are commercially available; follow local protocols for use.





> [!TIP]
> Extremely high doses of <u>atropine</u> are often required; notify the pharmacy early if therapy is initiated. [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mxWVCn0)




> [!WARNING]
> Continue to monitor patients for <u>muscle weakness</u> and <u>impending respiratory failure</u> after <u>atropine</u> administration; <u>atropine</u> does not reverse nicotinic effects.




### Oxime therapy [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




The <u>effectiveness</u> of <u>oxime</u> therapy (e.g., <u>pralidoxime</u>, obidoxime) in cholinergic <u>poisoning</u> is not well-established but it is still recommended as an adjunct to <u>atropine</u> by most experts. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Q-WuCL0)




* Mechanism

* Dephosphorylates <u>acetylcholinesterase</u>, resulting in enzyme reactivation [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Reverses both nicotinic and muscarinic effects of <u>poisoning</u>

* Timing

* Administered with <u>atropine</u> or shortly after initiation of <u>atropine</u>, as <u>oximes</u> may transiently increase <u>acetylcholinesterase</u> inhibition

* Most effective if given early  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Dosing and administration

* <u>Loading dose</u>: pralidoxime (<u>2-PAM</u>) DOSAGE [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Some experts recommend higher <u>loading doses</u> and continuous infusions; consult poison control for further guidance. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)





### Disposition [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Admit patients with severe symptoms (e.g., <u>seizures</u>, <u>respiratory failure</u>) to the <u>intensive care unit</u>.

* Most patients require hospitalization to monitor for worsening neurological function and <u>muscle weakness</u>.

* Consider discharging patients who are asymptomatic or have mild symptoms after 6 hours of observation in consultation with poison control.
