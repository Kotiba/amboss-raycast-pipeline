[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nF07i3)

## Summary

Stimulants encompass a wide variety of prescription and <u>recreational drugs</u> that are used to enhance arousal and/or alter mood and awareness. <u>Nicotine</u> and <u>caffeine</u> are the most commonly used uncontrolled stimulants. <u>Amphetamines</u> and <u>cocaine</u> are among the most commonly encountered controlled stimulants. Stimulants can also be prescribed medications, e.g., for <u>attention deficit hyperactivity disorder</u>. All stimulants have the potential for dependence and substance misuse. Most stimulants achieve their desired neuropsychiatric effect by increasing the concentration of <u>synaptic</u> <u>neurotransmitters</u> in the <u>central nervous system</u>, but a simultaneous increase of <u>neurotransmitters</u> in the <u>peripheral nervous system</u> can cause generalized <u>sympathetic nervous system</u> hyperactivity. Stimulant intoxication often manifests in a <u>sympathomimetic toxidrome</u> (<u>agitation</u>, <u>hyperthermia</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, and <u>hypertension</u>) and may result in rapid <u>end-organ damage</u> and/or sudden <u>death</u>. Treatment focuses on rapidly controlling <u>agitation</u>, <u>hyperthermia</u>, and <u>hypertension</u>. Chronic stimulant use may cause complications related to long-standing <u>sympathetic</u> stimulation (e.g., <u>cardiomyopathy</u>) or direct tissue toxicity (e.g., <u>CNS</u> damage). Withdrawal from chronic stimulant use is not typically associated with severe physiologic effects but may have profound psychological effects, e.g., <u>suicidal ideation</u>.




For the mechanisms of action and specific considerations for <u>nicotine</u> and <u>caffeine</u>, see “<u>Tobacco product use and smoking cessation</u>” and “<u>Caffeine-related disorders</u>.”




See also “<u>Sympathomimetic drugs</u>” for details on stimulant pharmacology and “<u>Substance-related and addictive disorders</u>” for details on managing related <u>substance-use disorders</u>.

## Stimulant intoxication

### Clinical features of stimulant intoxication [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* The neuropsychiatric and physiologic effects of stimulant use are highly variable and dose-dependent.

* The distinction between desired effect and overdose is also variable and depends on the patient, stimulant, and the context of exposure (especially with recreational use).





#### Neuropsychiatric effects




* Positive neuropsychiatric effects  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yPWdhl0)

* Increased concentration

* Increased alertness and arousal

* Euphoria and heightened feelings of well-being

* Appetite suppression

* Increased libido

* Negative neuropsychiatric effects

* <u>Disorganized thinking</u>

* Impaired judgment

* <u>Sleep disorders</u>, prolonged wakefulness, <u>hypervigilance</u>

* <u>Megalomania</u>

* <u>Psychotic symptoms</u>, e.g., <u>tactile hallucinations</u> (<u>formication</u>)   [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NpW-pm0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mpWVJm0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/65WjON0)





> [!WARNING]
> Stimulants can trigger <u>substance-induced psychosis</u>, especially in individuals with high-dose, daily use, and/or other <u>risk factors</u> for <u>psychosis</u>. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ipWJ6m0)




#### Physiologic effects




* Sympathomimetic toxidrome [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d4WoRl0)

* <u>Hypertension</u>

* <u>Tachycardia</u> and/or <u>tachyarrhythmias</u>

* <u>Hyperthermia</u>

* <u>Tachypnea</u>

* <u>Diaphoresis</u>

* <u>Mydriasis</u>

* <u>Psychomotor agitation</u>: <u>anxiety</u>, <u>agitation</u>, <u>psychotic symptoms</u>, e.g., <u>delusions</u>, <u>hallucinations</u>

* <u>Seizures</u> and/or <u>dyskinesias</u>

* Other: hyperactive <u>bowel sounds</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>chest pain</u>, abdominal <u>pain</u>





### <u>DSM-V</u> criteria for stimulant intoxication [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)




Both criteria must be fulfilled during or shortly after the use of a stimulant and not be attributable to another cause.



1. * Behavioral/mental features: euphoria, mood elevation, <u>anxiety</u>, anger, impaired judgment, etc.
2. * At least 2 of the following somatic features:

* Evidence of weight loss

* <u>Mydriasis</u>

* <u>Psychomotor agitation</u> or retardation

* <u>Diaphoresis</u>

* <u>Hypertension</u> or <u>hypotension</u>

* <u>Tachycardia</u> or <u>bradycardia</u>

* <u>Nausea</u> and/or <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Muscle weakness</u>, <u>chest pain</u>, or <u>arrhythmia</u>

* Neurological features: <u>seizures</u>, <u>dyskinesias</u>, <u>coma</u>




### Differential diagnosis [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d4WoRl0)




* Infection: <u>sepsis</u>, <u>meningitis</u>, <u>encephalitis</u>

* <u>Alcohol withdrawal</u> and/or sedative medication withdrawal

* Other causes of <u>drug-induced hyperthermia</u>

* <u>Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome</u>

* <u>Pheochromocytoma</u>

* <u>Thyroid storm</u>

* <u>Status epilepticus</u>

* <u>Heatstroke</u>

## Diagnosis

Initial <u>management of stimulant intoxication</u> is based on the <u>clinical diagnosis</u>. Diagnostic testing is primarily used to identify alternative diagnoses and/or complications.




### Initial diagnostics [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>BMP</u>

* <u>Hypoglycemia</u>: due to <u>hyperadrenergic state</u>

* <u>Hyponatremia</u>: especially with <u>MDMA</u>

* <u>Hyperkalemia</u>: due to <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>

* ↑ <u>BUN</u> and <u>creatinine</u>: may indicate <u>renal failure</u>

* <u>ECG</u>: may show <u>tachyarrhythmia</u> and/or features of <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u>  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* <u>Urinalysis</u>, <u>CPK</u>: ↑ <u>CPK</u>, <u>hematuria</u>, or <u>myoglobinuria</u> suggests <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>.

* <u>Urine drug screening</u>: rarely helpful for acute management 

* <u>Cocaine</u>: A positive test confirms the diagnosis.

* <u>Amphetamines</u>: <u>False positives</u> may result from the use of other substances.  [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/I5WYlN0)





### Additional diagnostics [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Further diagnostic testing is guided by the clinical presentation.




* <u>Chest pain</u>: <u>troponin</u> to assess for <u>myocardial</u> <u>ischemia</u>

* <u>Dyspnea</u>: <u>CXR</u> and/or CT chest to assess for <u>barotrauma</u>, <u>aspiration</u>, and alveolitis

* Persistent <u>hyperthermia</u>: See “<u>Diagnostic evaluation of fever</u>.”

* Persistent <u>CNS</u> changes: CT head, additional <u>diagnostics for altered mental status</u>

* Abdominal <u>pain</u>: CT abdomen to assess for <u>intestinal ischemia</u>

* Evidence of bleeding or <u>thrombosis</u>: <u>coagulation panel</u>, <u>CBC</u>

## Management

### Initial resuscitation [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Conduct a <u>primary survey in poisoning</u> (including core temperature).

* Check blood <u>glucose</u> and begin <u>IV fluid resuscitation</u>.

* Administer <u>benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication</u> as needed.

* <u>Manage agitation</u> and rapidly lower body temperature to â¤ 38.8Â°C (â¤ 101.8Â°F).

* <u>Manage hypertensive emergencies</u> and <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>.

* <u>Replete electrolytes</u> and <u>treat hypoglycemia</u>.

* Consider <u>GI decontamination</u> if stimulant intake was oral or <u>body packing</u> is suspected.

* Obtain additional <u>diagnostics for stimulant intoxication</u> as needed.





> [!TIP]
> During initial management, focus on controlling <u>agitation</u> and <u>hyperadrenergic state</u> with <u>benzodiazepines</u> and lowering the body temperature to â¤ 38.8Â°C with ice baths. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Avoid <u>succinylcholine</u> for <u>airway management</u> and muscle relaxation because it may precipitate fatal <u>hyperkalemia</u> in patients with <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




### Benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)




* Indications

* <u>Agitation</u>

* <u>Seizures</u>

* <u>Hyperadrenergic state</u> (e.g., <u>hypertension</u>, <u>cardiac arrhythmia</u>)

* Administration and monitoring

* See “<u>Benzodiazepines for agitation</u>” for initial dosages.

* Administer repeat doses until a clinical effect is seen.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* <u>Benzodiazepines</u> with rapid onset (e.g., <u>midazolam</u> or <u>diazepam</u>) are preferred because of a lower risk of <u>oversedation</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Consider IM administration if <u>IV access</u> cannot be obtained.

* Monitor patients closely for <u>signs of oversedation</u> and <u>paradoxical reactions to benzodiazepines</u>.

* Adverse effects

* Respiratory <u>depression</u>

* <u>Oversedation</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Benzodiazepines</u> are administered until a clinical effect is seen. Large doses are often required, e.g. 1 mg/kg of <u>diazepam</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




### Symptom-based management




#### <u>Agitation</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)




Immediate sedation is the primary treatment for <u>agitation</u> caused by stimulant intoxication.




* Pharmacotherapy

* <u>Benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication</u> are preferred.

* Consider;  <u>antipsychotics</u> (e.g., <u>haloperidol</u>) as a first-line or in combination with <u>benzodiazepines</u> if <u>psychotic features</u> are present.  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Z1cZ2Y0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/01ce2Y0)

* Consider <u>ketamine</u> for refractory <u>agitation</u>.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cacq5a0)

* Other sedatives for refractory <u>agitation</u> include <u>dexmedetomidine</u>  and <u>propofol</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)

* Additional considerations

* Avoid restraints because they increase the risk of <u>hyperthermia</u> and <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Consider other <u>critical causes of agitation</u> (e.g., <u>hypoxia</u>, <u>pain</u>).

* See also “<u>Approach to agitated or violent patients</u>” and “<u>Treatment of acute psychosis</u>.”





#### <u>Hyperthermia</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Rapidly cool patients to â¤ 38.8Â°C (â¤ 101.8Â°F), ideally within 20 minutes.

* Initiate <u>active cooling</u> (see “<u>Management of hyperthermia</u>” for details).

* Sedation reduces heat generation, but additional muscle relaxation may be required for adequate cooling. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Institute continuous monitoring of core temperature.





#### Cardiovascular symptoms




##### <u>Hypertensive emergencies</u>




* Reduce <u>sympathetic</u> tone with <u>benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication</u>.

* If <u>hypertension</u> persists, consider <u>phentolamine</u> (<u>off-label</u>). DOSAGE [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OzaIGM)

* Alternative treatments include short-acting <u>calcium channel blockers</u> (e.g., <u>clevidipine</u> or <u>nicardipine</u>), <u>hydralazine</u>, and <u>nitroglycerin</u>.

* <u>Beta blockers</u> are generally avoided for treating stimulant-induced <u>hypertension</u> alone (see “<u>Safety of beta blockers with stimulant use</u>”).

* See “<u>Management of hypertensive emergencies</u>” for additional considerations and dosages.





##### <u>Cardiac arrhythmias</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7RW4oO0)




* Sedation, cooling, <u>fluid resuscitation</u>, and <u>electrolyte repletion</u> often suffice to resolve <u>cardiac arrhythmias</u>.

* For persistent <u>supraventricular tachycardia</u> (<u>SVT</u>), consider a <u>calcium channel blocker</u>.

* For persistent <u>ventricular tachycardia</u> (VT), administer hypertonic <u>sodium bicarbonate</u>. DOSAGE  [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HnXKuA)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4WKkl0)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DgW1xk0)

* Use <u>beta blockers</u> and <u>class IA</u> and <u>class IC antiarrhythmics</u> with caution (see “<u>Safety of beta blockers with stimulant use</u>”). [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k6bmOu)

* See also “<u>Acute management of SVT</u>” and “<u>Initial management of VT</u>.”





##### <u>Chest pain</u> [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4WKkl0)




<u>Chest pain</u> is a common symptom in patients who have used stimulants (especially <u>cocaine</u>).




* <u>Differential diagnosis of chest pain</u> in patients using stimulants [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* <u>Acute coronary syndrome</u> (<u>ACS</u>), e.g., coronary vasospasm or <u>thrombosis</u>

* <u>Aortic dissection</u>

* <u>Pulmonary barotrauma</u>

* <u>Pulmonary infarction</u>

* <u>Intestinal ischemia</u>

* <u>ACS</u> suspected (e.g., <u>cocaine</u>-induced <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>): Follow the <u>ACS management</u> algorithm.

* Reduce <u>sympathetic</u> tone with <u>benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication</u>.

* Give <u>aspirin</u>.

* <u>Manage hypertension</u> with <u>nitrates</u> and/or <u>phentolamine</u>.

* Consult a specialist about <u>beta blockers</u> (see “<u>Safety of beta blockers with stimulant use</u>”).





##### Safety of beta blockers with stimulant use [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k6bmOu)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RJWlFm0)




* Beta blockade in stimulant-induced cardiovascular toxicity is controversial, especially with <u>nonselective beta blockers</u>, due to concerns about unopposed <u>alpha-adrenergic</u> stimulation, which could worsen <u>hypertension</u> and coronary vasospasm.

* <u>Beta blockers</u> have been safely used in patients with stimulant use and concomitant <u>ACS</u>, <u>hypertensive crises</u>, <u>acute heart failure</u>, and <u>tachyarrhythmias</u>.  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k6bmOu)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RJWlFm0)

* Many experts nevertheless recommend avoiding <u>beta blockers</u> in acute stimulant intoxication, especially if adequate alternatives are available. [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OnYItp)

* Consider balancing this risk against the benefits of <u>beta blockers</u> for the individual patient, using <u>shared decision-making</u>. [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RJWlFm0)

* Dual beta- and <u>alpha-blockers</u> (e.g., <u>labetalol</u>, <u>carvedilol</u>) are most likely safe. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/a1cQ2Y0)[[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/k6bmOu)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/RJWlFm0)





#### Other [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




* <u>Seizures</u>

* First-line: <u>benzodiazepines</u>

* Second-line: <u>barbiturates</u>

* See “<u>Management of acute seizures</u>” for additional recommendations.

* Vasospasm and/or <u>thrombosis</u>

* Assess for signs of <u>ACS</u>, <u>ischemic stroke</u>, <u>intestinal ischemia</u>, and limb <u>ischemia</u>.

* Obtain imaging to distinguish between vasospasm and <u>thrombosis</u>.

* Vasospasm: Begin <u>vasodilators</u> (e.g., <u>phentolamine</u>, <u>nitroglycerin</u>, or <u>nicardipine</u>).

* <u>Thrombosis</u>: Begin appropriate management depending on location, e.g., <u>thrombolysis</u> for <u>ischemic stroke</u>.

* Percutaneous intervention or surgical <u>revascularization</u> may be required, e.g., for limb <u>ischemia</u>.

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u>: <u>Manage rhabdomyolysis</u> with aggressive <u>IV fluid therapy</u>.

* <u>Flash pulmonary edema</u>: See “<u>Management of acute heart failure</u>.”





### <u>Body packing</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TpW6Km0)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fpWkKm0)




* Obtain imaging, e.g., <u>abdominal x-ray</u> or CT abdomen

* Admit for <u>whole-bowel irrigation</u> and monitoring until all packets have passed.

* Ruptured packet

* Urgent surgical consultation for operative <u>GI decontamination</u>

* Interim stabilization with <u>benzodiazepines for stimulant intoxication</u> and other symptom-based therapies

* See “<u>Body packing</u>” for further details.





> [!WARNING]
> Do not delay operative therapy in patients with ruptured packets filled with stimulants. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TpW6Km0)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fpWkKm0)




### Disposition [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Resolved symptoms and no sign of <u>end-organ damage</u>: Consider discharge after 4–6 hours of observation.

* <u>Hyperthermia</u>, <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>, and/or other evidence of <u>end-organ damage</u>: inpatient admission

* Patients with <u>chest pain</u> [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/H4WKkl0)

* Perform <u>risk stratification for acute coronary syndrome</u>.

* High risk: Admit to a monitored bed.

* Low and intermediate-risk, resolved <u>pain</u>, and no <u>ECG</u> or <u>troponin</u> changes: Consider discharge after 9–12 hours of observation.

* Body packers: Admit until all packets have passed from the <u>GI tract</u>.

* All patients: Offer <u>counseling on substance use disorders</u> before discharge.

* Cardiovascular

* Vasospasm and <u>ischemia</u>: <u>myocardial infarction</u>, <u>intestinal ischemia</u>, <u>ischemic stroke</u> [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/A4WRNl0)

* Cardiac <u>tachyarrhythmias</u> and <u>sudden cardiac death</u>

* Pulmonary (primarily with inhalation) [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_4W5Nl0)

* Diffuse alveolar damage and hemorrhagic alveolitis (primarily with <u>crack</u> <u>cocaine</u>)

* <u>Barotrauma</u>

* Exacerbation of <u>asthma</u>

* <u>Pulmonary edema</u>

* Other

* Negative neuropsychiatric effects, e.g., <u>psychosis</u>

* <u>Serotonin syndrome</u> (primarily after <u>MDMA</u> use)

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u>-induced acute renal <u>tubular necrosis</u> (due to <u>myoglobinuria</u>)





### Chronic [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QQWuDO0)




* Cardiovascular

* <u>Myocarditis</u> and/or <u>cardiomyopathy</u>

* Accelerated <u>atherosclerosis</u> and necrotizing <u>vasculitis</u>

* Neuropsychiatric

* <u>Stimulant use disorder</u>

* <u>Psychosis</u>, <u>depression</u>, <u>anxiety</u>, <u>suicide</u>, violence [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c4WaRl0)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/14W2Rl0)

* <u>CNS</u> toxicity, e.g., <u>Parkinson disease</u>

* Movement disorders [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/c4WaRl0)

## Stimulant withdrawal

### Clinical features of stimulant withdrawal [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZkWZml0)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PQWWwO0)




Symptoms of withdrawal occur in most individuals who abruptly discontinue stimulants after prolonged use, typically within 24 hours of stimulant cessation.




* Acute phase (first week, peak at 2–3 days)

* Severe <u>dysphoria</u>, <u>depression</u>, <u>suicidal ideation</u>

* <u>Anxiety</u> and/or irritability

* Fatigue, <u>lethargy</u>, and <u>hypersomnia</u>

* Difficulty concentrating

* Hunger, <u>headaches</u>, body aches, <u>dental pain</u>

* Unpleasant, vivid dreams

* Protracted phase (weeks to months)

* Cognitive dysfunction (e.g., impaired <u>memory</u>)

* Difficulty making decisions

* <u>Anhedonia</u>, <u>anxiety</u>, and/or <u>depression</u>

* Cravings, drug-related dreams





> [!WARNING]
> While the physiologic signs and symptoms are not typically life-threatening, the psychological symptoms of withdrawal are severe. They may lead individuals to start using stimulants again and carry a high risk of self-harm. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)




### <u>DSM-V</u> criteria for stimulant withdrawal [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Nta-dm)




Both criteria must be fulfilled and not be attributable to another cause.



1. * <u>Dysphoria</u> and at least 2 of the following symptoms occurring within hours to days of stimulant cessation:

* Fatigue

* Psychomotor slowing or <u>agitation</u>

* <u>Somnolence</u>, <u>lethargy</u>, or <u>insomnia</u>

* Increased appetite

* <u>Sleep</u> disturbance, vivid nightmares
2. * Clinically significant <u>distress</u> or functional impairment resulting from the above features




### Management of stimulant withdrawal [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZkWZml0)[[34]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PQWWwO0)




#### General principles




* Management is primarily supportive, with an emphasis on preventing self-harm.  [[33]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZkWZml0)

* Provide symptomatic treatment as needed (e.g., <u>management of acute panic attacks</u>, <u>management of agitation</u>).

* <u>Screen for substance use disorders</u> involving other substances, as patients may self-medicate and/or experience withdrawal.

* Remain vigilant for evidence of concurrent psychiatric disorders and <u>suicidal ideation</u>.





#### Consults and disposition




* Consult <u>psychiatry</u> for evaluation and <u>treatment of depression</u>, <u>suicidal ideation</u>, and/or <u>substance use disorders</u>.

* Acute symptoms are typically safe to manage in an outpatient <u>withdrawal management setting</u>.

* Consider admission for <u>inpatient withdrawal management</u> if the patient has:

* Severe psychiatric symptoms

* Severe polysubstance use

* Unsafe living conditions, e.g., is experiencing <u>intimate partner violence</u>

## Cocaine

### Background [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




* Routes of administration [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/QQWuDO0)[[35]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NQW-wO0)

* Crystallized freebase (<u>crack</u>): smoked, injected

* Water-soluble salts (“<u>coke</u>,” “blow,” “snow”): nasal inhalation, intravenous

* Mechanism of action

* Inhibition of <u>dopamine</u>, <u>serotonin</u>, <u>epinephrine</u>, and <u>norepinephrine</u> reuptake → increased <u>synaptic</u> <u>neurotransmitter</u> concentration → autonomic stimulation, <u>CNS</u> excitation

* Inhibition of Na<sup>+</sup>channels → slowed neuronal <u>action potential</u> transmission → <u>local anesthesia</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>

* <u>Half-life</u>: 40–90 minutes [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)

* Therapeutic uses (limited)

* Diagnosis of <u>Horner syndrome</u>: Topical <u>cocaine</u> causes <u>mydriasis</u> in an <u>eye</u> with intact <u>sympathetic</u> innervation, but not in an <u>eye</u> with <u>sympathetic denervation</u>. [[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6kWjKl0)

* <u>Topical anesthesia</u> for otolaryngology procedures





### Cocaine intoxication [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Clinical features

* <u>Clinical features of stimulant intoxication</u>, e.g., <u>sympathomimetic toxidrome</u>, euphoria

* <u>Chest pain</u> and/or <u>angina</u> due to <u>cocaine</u>-induced coronary vasospasm

* Treatment: : See “<u>Management of stimulant intoxication</u>.”





### Cocaine withdrawal [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Clinical features

* <u>Clinical features of stimulant withdrawal</u>, e.g., fatigue, <u>anhedonia</u>

* Physiologic crash: severe <u>depression</u> with <u>suicidal ideation</u>, constricted <u>pupils</u>, <u>psychomotor agitation</u>

* Management: : See “<u>Management of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”





### Complications of <u>cocaine</u> use




The following are common <u>complications of stimulant use</u> associated with <u>cocaine</u>:




* <u>Cocaine</u>-induced vasospasm

* ↓ Reuptake of <u>norepinephrine</u> → ↑ α- and β<sub>1</sub>‑stimulation → <u>vasoconstriction</u> and vasospasm

* Manifestations include <u>myocardial infarction</u>, <u>cerebrovascular accident</u>, and <u>ischemic colitis</u>

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u>  [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pkWLKl0)

* Multifactorial: direct <u>myocyte</u> toxicity, <u>ischemia</u> caused by vasospasm, and/or <u>compartment syndrome</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* <u>Myoglobinuria</u> can lead to acute renal <u>tubular necrosis</u>.

* Acute pulmonary cocaine toxicity (<u>crack lung</u>) [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_4W5Nl0)

* Pulmonary syndrome of diffuse alveolar damage and hemorrhagic alveolitis that occurs within 48 hours of <u>crack</u> <u>cocaine</u> inhalation

* <u>Histopathology</u>: alveolar hemorrhage, alveolar and <u>interstitial</u> <u>eosinophil</u> infiltration, diffuse alveolar damage

* Clinical findings: <u>dyspnea</u>, <u>chest pain</u>, productive <u>cough</u>, <u>fever</u>, <u>hemoptysis</u>, <u>hypoxemic respiratory failure</u>

* Radiographic findings: <u>atelectasis</u>, <u>pulmonary edema</u>, <u>pneumothorax</u>, <u>pneumomediastinum</u>, diffuse <u>ground glass opacities</u>, consolidation

* Nasal pathology

* Nasal inhalation can damage nasal <u>blood vessels</u> and result in <u>epistaxis</u>.

* Chronic local <u>vasoconstriction</u> may cause <u>ischemic necrosis</u> and perforation of the <u>nasal septum</u>.

* <u>Substance use disorders</u>: <u>cocaine use disorder</u>





> [!NOTE]
> Suspect <u>cocaine</u> use in individuals with weight loss, behavioral changes, and <u>erythema</u> of the turbinates and <u>nasal septum</u>.

## Amphetamines

### Background [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)




* Definition: a group of compounds with a shared phenylethylamine structure

* Substances 

* Alpha-methylphenethylamine (“speed”)

* <u>Methamphetamine</u> (“meth,” “crank”)

* 3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (“<u>MDMA</u>,” “<u>ecstasy</u>,” “molly”)

* <u>Cathinone</u> (“<u>bath salts</u>”)

* Mechanism of action: ↑ release and ↓ reuptake of presynaptic <u>monoamine neurotransmitters</u> (e.g., <u>adrenaline</u>, <u>norepinephrine</u>, <u>serotonin</u>, and <u>dopamine</u>)

* Central effect: <u>CNS</u> <u>sympathetic</u> stimulation and hallucinogenic effects

* Peripheral effect: <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>hypertension</u>, <u>arrhythmias</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Amphetamines</u> are controlled substances in the United States, along with some precursors to <u>amphetamine</u> synthesis, e.g., <u>pseudoephedrine</u> and <u>ephedrine</u>.




### <u>Amphetamine</u> intoxication




* Clinical features

* <u>Clinical features of stimulant intoxication</u>, e.g., <u>sympathomimetic toxidrome</u> [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d4WoRl0)

* Behavioral changes: <u>disorganized thinking</u>, impaired judgment, increased libido, <u>bruxism</u>, <u>sleep disorders</u> [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KMWUpN0)

* <u>Physical exam</u> findings: <u>skin</u> excoriations due to <u>formication</u>

* See individual drugs for unique concerns.

* Management: : See “<u>Management of stimulant intoxication</u>.”





### Amphetamine withdrawal




* Clinical features

* <u>Clinical features of stimulant withdrawal</u>

* Physiologic crash after use due to <u>receptor</u> downregulation

* Management: See “<u>Management of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”





### Complications of <u>amphetamine</u> use




* General complications of stimulant intoxication, e.g., <u>rhabdomyolysis</u>

* <u>Memory</u> impairment (especially with chronic <u>MDMA</u> use) [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kQWmwO0)

* Ongoing <u>anxiety</u>, <u>mood disorders</u>, or <u>psychosis</u>

* <u>Amphetamine use disorder</u>

## Methamphetamines

### Background [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lQWvwO0)




* Street names: meth, crank, ice, crystal meth

* Route of administration: smoked (most common), injected, inhaled, oral, sublingual

* Mechanism of action: similar to <u>amphetamines</u> but with higher <u>potency</u> and a longer duration of action  [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kQWmwO0)





### <u>Methamphetamine</u> intoxication [[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lQWvwO0)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rkWf6l0)




* Clinical features

* Typical <u>clinical features of stimulant intoxication</u>, e.g., <u>agitation</u>

* High <u>incidence</u> of psychological and behavioral symptoms and <u>psychosis</u>  [[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4QW3wO0)

* Management: See “<u>Management of stimulant intoxication</u>.”





> [!WARNING]
> Fatalities from acute <u>methamphetamine</u> intoxication are typically related to vascular complications. Remain vigilant for <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>, acute <u>stroke</u>, and <u>tachyarrhythmias</u> in these patients. [[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hJWcFm0)




### <u>Methamphetamine withdrawal</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qRWCLO0)[[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/lQWvwO0)




* Clinical features

* Psychological <u>symptoms of stimulant withdrawal</u> (e.g., <u>depression</u>) may be severe.

* Drug craving can be more pronounced than with other stimulants (e.g., <u>MDMA</u>). [[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VOWGrl0)

* Management: See “<u>Management of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”





### Complications of chronic use [[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rkWf6l0)




* Chronic psychological and behavioral changes, including <u>high-risk sexual behavior</u>

* <u>Dilated cardiomyopathy</u> and <u>heart failure</u>

* <u>CNS</u> toxicity: e.g., <u>substance-induced psychosis</u>, cognitive impairment, <u>CNS</u> <u>vasculitis</u> [[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3JWSFm0)

* <u>Gingivitis</u> and <u>dental disorders</u> (e.g., <u>tooth decay</u>, <u>dental injuries</u>): “meth mouth”

## MDMA

### Background [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)[[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cmWaeN0)




* Substance: 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (<u>MDMA</u>)

* Street names: <u>ecstasy</u>, E, molly

* Route of administration: oral

* Mechanism of action

* ↑ Release and ↓ reuptake of <u>serotonin</u>, <u>dopamine</u>, and <u>norepinephrine</u>

* <u>MDMA</u> increases secretion of <u>antidiuretic hormone</u> (<u>ADH</u>) → thirst → increased water intake without <u>electrolyte</u> repletion → <u>hyponatremia</u>

* <u>Half-life</u>: ∼ 9 hours [[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bKbHUu)





### <u>MDMA</u> intoxication [[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cmWaeN0)[[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bKbHUu)




* Clinical features

* <u>Clinical features of stimulant intoxication</u>, e.g., <u>sympathetic</u> <u>toxidrome</u>, euphoria, <u>agitation</u>

* Entactogen: social empathy (feeling of “oneness”), loss of <u>anxiety</u>, promotion of introspection and personal reflection [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/6MWjpN0)

* <u>Hyponatremia</u>: can manifest with <u>seizures</u>, cerebral and/or <u>pulmonary edema</u>

* <u>Features of serotonin syndrome</u> (especially with concomitant use of an <u>SSRI</u>)

* Other: grinding <u>teeth</u> (<u>bruxism</u>), <u>trismus</u>, distorted perceptions of sensation and time

* Management [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* See “<u>Management of stimulant intoxication</u>.”

* <u>Treat hyponatremia</u> with fluid restriction and <u>hypertonic saline</u>.

* <u>Manage serotonin syndrome</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Common preventive actions to avoid harm from <u>MDMA</u> include prehydration with water or isotonic solutions, limiting or reducing drug use, taking breaks, and testing ingested drugs for other substances. [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UpWbKm0)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2pWTKm0)




### <u>MDMA</u> withdrawal [[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cmWaeN0)




* Clinical features

* Physical symptoms of withdrawal are uncommon.

* Psychological symptoms of withdrawal are similar to other stimulants: See “<u>Clinical features of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”

* Management: See “<u>Management of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”

## Cathinones

### Background [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/NoY-XJ)[[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9kWNJl0)




<u>Cathinones</u> are chemically similar to <u>amphetamines</u> and have similar clinical effects.




* Substances

* Naturally occurring <u>cathinone</u> is found in Catha edulis plant and is released by chewing on the leaves.

* Synthetic cathinones

* <u>Bupropion</u> is the only <u>cathinone</u> with a medical indication.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Y11n2f0)

* Street names

* Naturally occurring: khat, qat, kat, chat, miraa

* Synthetic: <u>bath salts</u>, bloom, vanilla sky

* Mechanism of action: ↑ release and ↓ reuptake of <u>monoamines</u> (<u>norepinephrine</u>, <u>dopamine</u>, <u>serotonin</u>) → <u>sympathetic</u> stimulation and hallucinogenic effects (can last for several days) [[53]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kNdmYq0)





### <u>Cathinone</u> intoxication [[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9kWNJl0)




* Clinical features 

* Typical <u>clinical features of stimulant intoxication</u> (e.g., <u>hyperthermia</u>, <u>mydriasis</u>)

* Negative neuropsychiatric effects include <u>agitation</u>, <u>hallucinations</u>, <u>paranoia</u>, and aggression.

* <u>Hyponatremia</u> occurs with some <u>synthetic cathinones</u>.

* Diagnostics: <u>Synthetic cathinones</u> cannot be detected with routine <u>urine toxicology screening</u>.

* Management

* Provide <u>supportive care</u>.

* See “<u>Management of stimulant intoxication</u>.”





### <u>Cathinone</u> withdrawal [[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9kWNJl0)




* Clinical features: <u>Symptoms of stimulant withdrawal</u> may begin immediately after stopping use.

* Management: See “<u>Management of stimulant withdrawal</u>.”





### Complications [[52]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9kWNJl0)[[54]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/moYVcJ)




See also “<u>Complications of stimulant use</u>.”




* Acute

* <u>Acute renal failure</u>

* <u>Myocardial infarction</u>

* <u>Rhabdomyolysis</u>

* Chronic

* <u>Insomnia</u>

* <u>Psychosis</u>
