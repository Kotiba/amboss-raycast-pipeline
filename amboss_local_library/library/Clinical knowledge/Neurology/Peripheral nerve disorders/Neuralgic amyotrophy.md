## Summary

Neuralgic amyotrophy (also referred to as brachial <u>neuritis</u> or Parsonage-Turner syndrome) is a <u>self-limiting</u> inflammatory disorder of the <u>brachial plexus</u>, that mainly affects males between 20–30 years of age. There are two clinically similar, yet etiologically distinct forms of neuralgic amyotrophy (NA). <u>Idiopathic</u> NA is more common, often unilateral, and non-recurrent. It is thought to be an immune-mediated process triggered by preceding viral infection, <u>immunization</u>, or trauma. Hereditary NA is a rare, <u>autosomal dominant</u> condition that often affects both shoulders and is characterized by recurrent symptoms. In both types, patients present with acute onset of excruciating shoulder <u>pain</u> that lasts for weeks followed by patchy <u>lower motor neuron</u> <u>paresis</u> of the <u>proximal</u> muscles in the affected arm. Diagnosis is clinical, with <u>nerve conduction studies</u> and <u>needle electromyography</u> performed to identify which nerves are affected. Treatment is mainly supportive and involves <u>analgesia</u> and <u>physical therapy</u>. Most patients recover complete muscle strength within 2 years.

## Etiology

* <u>Idiopathic</u> NA (more common)

* Likely immune-mediated

* Potential triggers: recent viral infection/<u>immunization</u>; ; mechanical strain on the arm/shoulder; trauma; <u>surgery</u>; <u>childbirth</u>; <u>vasculitis</u> (<u>temporal arteritis</u>, <u>PAN</u>, <u>SLE</u>)

* Hereditary NA 

* Rare

* <u>Autosomal dominant</u>

## Clinical features

* Shoulder <u>pain</u> 

* Acute phase

* Sudden onset of excruciating, burning <u>pain</u> which does not decrease with <u>NSAIDs</u>

* Mostly unilateral (esp. affects the right shoulder ), radiates to the arm and/or the neck

* Usually lasts for several weeks

* Chronic phase

* Continuous <u>pain</u> lasting ∼ 1 year or

* Intermittent exacerbations of shooting <u>pain</u>, lasting for a few hours; brought on by certain shoulder positions/actions (arm <u>abduction</u>/elevation)

* Progressive <u>weakness</u> and <u>atrophy</u> of the shoulder/arm muscles

* Occurs ∼ 2 weeks after the acute onset of <u>pain</u>

* Patchy involvement of more than one nerve of the <u>brachial plexus</u>

* Different degrees of <u>lower motor neuron</u> <u>paresis</u> of muscles innervated by the same peripheral nerve

* Nerves commonly affected: 

* <u>Suprascapular nerve</u>;  → weak <u>abduction</u> and <u>external rotation</u> of the shoulder

* <u>Long thoracic nerve</u> → <u>winging of the scapula</u>

* <u>Axillary nerve</u>;  → weak <u>abduction</u> of the arm

* Subscapular nerve;  → weak <u>internal rotation</u> of the shoulder

* Sensory loss: <u>hypoesthesia</u>:  (numbness); <u>paresthesia</u> or <u>dysesthesia</u> (painful sensation to <u>light touch</u>) in the arm, forearm, and/or hand

* <u>Autonomic dysfunction</u>: <u>edema</u>, <u>excessive sweating</u>, faster growth of <u>hair</u> and <u>nails</u> on the affected limb

## Diagnosis

NA is mainly a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>.




* Assessment of damage localization and severity

* <u>Nerve conduction studies</u>: show conduction block in the affected nerves

* <u>Needle electromyography</u>: show axonal degeneration; help differentiate between muscular and neural causes of <u>weakness</u>

* Exclusion of differential diagnoses 

* Chest <u>x-ray</u>: to rule out <u>Pancoast tumor</u> or <u>thoracic outlet syndrome</u>

* <u>MRI</u> of the <u>cervical spine</u>: to rule out cervical disc disease/degeneration

* <u>MRI</u> of the shoulder: to rule out <u>rotator cuff tears</u>/impingement

## Differential diagnoses

* Upper limb <u>weakness</u> and/or <u>proximal</u> muscle <u>atrophy</u>

* <u>Pancoast tumor</u>

* <u>Rotator cuff tendinitis</u>/tear

* Cervical <u>radiculopathies</u>; , <u>mononeuritis multiplex</u>, <u>amyotrophic lateral sclerosis</u>, <u>thoracic outlet syndrome</u>, acute <u>poliomyelitis</u>

* Acute severe <u>pain</u>: <u>myocardial infarction</u>, <u>herpes zoster</u> <u>neuritis</u>

## Treatment

There is no specific causative treatment for NA. The condition is <u>self-limiting</u> and is treated symptomatically.




* <u>Analgesia</u>

* Acute phase: combination of a long-acting <u>NSAID</u> (e.g., <u>diclofenac</u>) and an <u>opiate</u> (e.g., <u>morphine</u>)

* Chronic phase: <u>gabapentin</u>, <u>carbamazepine</u>, or <u>amitriptyline</u>

* <u>Physical therapy</u>

* Initiated after <u>acute pain</u> has subsided

* Prevents/minimizes muscular <u>atrophy</u> and preserves normal <u>range of motion of the shoulder</u> <u>joint</u>

* <u>Surgery</u>: nerve grafts or <u>tendon</u> transfers are possible; only considered in protracted disease courses (> 2 years)
