# Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma

## Summary

Juvenile <u>nasopharyngeal</u> <u>angiofibroma</u> (JNA) is a rare, benign, but locally aggressive <u>tumor</u> occurring almost exclusively in <u>adolescent</u> males. JNAs originate from the <u>posterior</u> <u>choanal</u> tissues and rapidly extend into the surrounding regions, including the <u>nasopharynx</u>, the <u>orbits</u>, and even the intracranial cavity. As the <u>tumor</u> is largely space-occupying and highly vascular, patients typically present with a progressive, unilateral nasal obstruction, and recurrent, severe <u>epistaxis</u>. Other symptoms include <u>rhinorrhea</u>, <u>anosmia</u>, and facial swelling (e.g., <u>exophthalmos</u>). The diagnosis is based on the clinical findings and confirmed through <u>cranial</u> <u>CT scans</u>. Surgical excision is the treatment of choice, since JNAs have a high recurrence rate. Radiation (stereotactic <u>gamma knife</u>) is reserved for recurrent cases or JNAs with intracranial extension.

## Epidemiology

* <u>Incidence</u>: rare, accounts for 0.05% of all head and neck tumors

* Sex: occurs exclusively in male individuals

* Age: 10–20 years

## Clinical features

* The severity of the clinical features depends on the extent of the <u>tumor</u>.

* Progressive unilateral nasal obstruction with septum deviation to the <u>contralateral</u> side (80–90% of cases)

* Recurrent <u>epistaxis</u>, often torrential , (45–60% of cases)

* <u>Rhinorrhea</u>, nasal intonation of voice, impaired nasal breathing, <u>anosmia</u> (in very large tumors)

* Unilateral <u>conductive hearing loss</u>

* Recurrent <u>headaches</u> due to <u>chronic sinusitis</u>

* On examination

* Lobular, pink mass; hard, rubbery texture; bleeds easily on touch

* Facial swelling

* Widened nasal bridge

* <u>Exophthalmos</u> with frog-face deformity

* Cheek or <u>infratemporal fossa</u> swelling

## Diagnosis

* Contrast-enhanced <u>CT scan</u>: investigation of choice to assess extent of <u>tumor</u> and bone involvement (e.g., invasion into the orbital cavity, <u>paranasal sinuses</u>, <u>fossa infratemporalis</u>)

* <u>MRI</u>: Complementary to <u>CT scan</u> to evaluate intracranial extension of the <u>tumor</u>

* <u>X-ray</u> of <u>paranasal sinuses</u>

* Opacified sinuses

* Displaced <u>nasal septum</u>

* Carotid <u>angiogram</u>: demonstrates feeding vessels (if preoperative embolization is planned)

* <u>Biopsy</u>: recommended only if the diagnosis is uncertain

## Treatment

* Surgical excision of the <u>tumor</u>

* Surgical approach depends on size and extent of the <u>tumor</u> (e.g., transmaxillary, transnasal)

* Preoperative embolization of feeding vessels necessary to reduce intraoperative blood loss

* <u>Stereotactic radiotherapy</u> (e.g., <u>gamma knife</u>)

* Reduces <u>tumor</u> vascularization and size

* Indicated in recurrent cases or evidence of intracranial extension

* Risk of damage to adjacent structures (e.g., <u>eye</u>, <u>brain</u>, spinal cord) is minimized compared to <u>external beam radiotherapy</u>.

* <u>Hormone</u> therapy with <u>flutamide</u>

* <u>Androgen</u> <u>receptor</u> blocker for presurgical <u>tumor</u> reduction

* <u>Adverse reactions</u> (e.g., <u>gynecomastia</u>, loss of libido)

* Complete <u>tumor</u> eradication not possible
