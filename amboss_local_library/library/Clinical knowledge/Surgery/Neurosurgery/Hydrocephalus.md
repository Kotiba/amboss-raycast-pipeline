[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EM08qg)

## Summary

Hydrocephalus is the abnormal enlargement of <u>cerebral ventricles</u> and/or <u>subarachnoid space</u> as a result of excess <u>cerebrospinal fluid</u> (<u>CSF</u>) accumulation. There are two types of true hydrocephalus: <u>communicating hydrocephalus</u>, which is caused by decreased <u>CSF</u> resorption or increased <u>CSF production</u> in the absence of <u>CSF flow</u> obstruction, and <u>noncommunicating hydrocephalus</u>, which is caused by the obstructed passage of <u>CSF</u> from the ventricles to the <u>subarachnoid space</u>. Both forms typically manifest with <u>signs of elevated intracranial pressure</u> (<u>ICP</u>). Other clinical manifestations may include changes in <u>vital signs</u> resulting from <u>brainstem</u> compression and, in congenital hydrocephalus, <u>macrocephaly</u>. <u>Normal pressure hydrocephalus</u> (<u>NPH</u>) is a gradual-onset chronic form of <u>communicating hydrocephalus</u> that primarily occurs in adults > 60 years of age. <u>ICP</u> is normal or mildly elevated; manifestations include a classic triad of <u>gait apraxia</u>, <u>dementia</u>, and <u>urinary incontinence</u>. All types of hydrocephalus are diagnosed using <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u> or CT head; <u>ultrasound</u> is used in children < 18 months of age with an open <u>anterior fontanelle</u>. A <u>lumbar puncture</u> may be required (once intracranial lesions with <u>mass effect</u> have been ruled out) to measure <u>opening pressure</u>, obtain <u>CSF</u> samples for analysis, and, if <u>NPH</u> is suspected, perform a <u>CSF tap test</u>. Temporizing measures to reduce <u>CSF</u> volume, such as <u>external ventricular drain</u> placement or serial <u>lumbar punctures</u>, may be used in the acute setting. Definitive treatment often involves surgical insertion of a shunt that drains excess <u>CSF</u> into another area of the body. <u>Hydrocephalus ex vacuo</u> is the enlargement of the ventricles and <u>subarachnoid space</u> due to loss of <u>brain</u> tissue (e.g., <u>cerebral atrophy</u>). It is not considered a true hydrocephalus because ventricular enlargement does not result from <u>CSF</u> accumulation and it does not affect <u>ICP</u> or <u>CSF flow</u>.

## Overview

* Hydrocephalus is a condition in which <u>CSF</u> accumulates within the <u>cerebral ventricles</u>, causing their enlargement.

* In hydrocephalus, <u>ICP</u> can be elevated or normal.

## Etiology and pathophysiology

### Communicating hydrocephalus




Dysfunction of <u>subarachnoid cisterns</u> or <u>arachnoid villi</u> resulting in decreased <u>CSF</u> absorption or increased <u>CSF production</u>.




* ↓ <u>CSF</u> absorption

* Inflammatory diseases of the <u>central nervous system</u> (e.g., <u>meningitis</u>) → inflamed <u>arachnoid villi</u> → scarring and obliteration of <u>arachnoid villi</u>

* Subarachnoidal or <u>intraventricular hemorrhage</u> → inflammatory response → <u>fibrosis</u> → obliteration of <u>arachnoid villi</u>

* Congenital absence of <u>arachnoid villi</u>

* ↑ <u>CSF</u> production

* Choroid plexus papilloma

* Rare <u>benign tumor</u> of the <u>choroid plexus</u>

* Typically manifests with <u>headache</u> (and other symptoms of <u>intracranial hypertension</u>) due to hydrocephalus secondary to <u>cerebrospinal fluid</u> overproduction

* <u>Choroid plexus carcinoma</u>

* Inflammation of the <u>choroid plexus</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/woYhVJ)

### Noncommunicating hydrocephalus (<u>obstructive hydrocephalus</u>)




Obstruction of the <u>cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius</u>, the <u>lateral foramen of Luschka</u>, or the <u>median foramen of Magendie</u> results in obstructed passage of <u>CSF</u> from the ventricles to the subarachnoidal space.




* Congenital: <u>Noncommunicating hydrocephalus</u> is the most common form of congenital hydrocephalus.

* <u>Arnold-Chiari malformation</u>

* Dandy-Walker malformation: congenital <u>malformation</u> caused by failure of the <u>fourth ventricle</u> to close, which leads to persistence of Blake's pouch (cyst in the <u>4<sup>th</sup> ventricle</u>) and <u>cerebellar vermis</u> <u>hypoplasia</u>  

* Causes a variety of neurologic abnormalities (e.g., <u>ataxic gait</u>) and <u>noncommunicating hydrocephalus</u>

* Associated with a variety of extracranial abnormalities (e.g., craniofacial abnormalities, cardiac defects, <u>spina bifida</u>)

* Intrauterine infections: e.g., <u>congenital toxoplasmosis</u> (see “<u>Congenital TORCH infections</u>”)

* Colloid cyst obstructing the <u>interventricular foramen</u>

* Congenital stenosis of the <u>cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius</u>

* Acquired

* <u>Brain tumor</u> (especially <u>medulloblastomas</u>, <u>pinealoma</u>, <u>ependymomas</u>, and <u>astrocytomas</u>)

* Other space-occupying lesions, e.g., <u>brain abscesses</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AVWRC40)

* Clots due to hemorrhage

## Clinical features

* <u>Features of increased ICP</u>

* <u>Headache</u>, <u>nausea</u>, and <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Papilledema</u>

* Abnormal gait

* Impaired consciousness

* <u>Cushing triad</u> (irregular breathing, widening <u>pulse pressure</u>, <u>bradycardia</u>)

* <u>Abducens nerve palsy</u>

* Clinical features in <u>infants</u>  

* <u>Macrocephaly</u>: an enlarged head with a circumference greater than the average for age and sex by two <u>standard deviations</u> (as indicated by a <u>percentile</u> growth chart)

* Tense <u>fontanelle</u>

* Setting sun sign: persistent downward deviation of the eyes due to <u>increased ICP</u> in <u>infants</u> and young children

* <u>Developmental delays</u> (e.g., psychomotor delays)

* Behavioral changes (e.g., irritability)

* MacEwen sign (<u>cracked pot sign</u>): percussion of the <u>skull</u> near the junction of the frontal, temporal, and <u>parietal bones</u> evokes an unusually resonant sound

* Caused by separation of <u>cranial sutures</u> due to <u>increased intracranial pressure</u>

* Found in late hydrocephalus

* Other findings

* Changes in <u>vital signs</u> resulting from <u>brainstem</u> compression due to <u>herniation</u>

* Lower extremity <u>spasticity</u>, <u>hyperreflexia</u>





> [!TIP]
> Because the <u>fontanelles</u> of <u>infants</u> are still open, the accumulation of <u>CSF</u> can lead to <u>macrocephaly</u>; this <u>accommodation</u> offsets the elevation in <u>ICP</u>, meaning that neurological symptoms often develop later than in older patients, whose <u>fontanelles</u> are closed.




> [!TIP]
> Adults with chronic hydrocephalus may present with insidious onset of nonspecific <u>headaches</u> and visual disturbances without other features of <u>elevated ICP</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yVWdC40)

## Subtypes and variants

### Normal pressure hydrocephalus




#### Definition [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HVWKD40)




* A form of chronic <u>communicating hydrocephalus</u> that primarily affects adults > 60 years of age

* Characterized by a distinct clinical triad (<u>gait apraxia</u>, <u>dementia</u>, and <u>urinary incontinence</u>) and normal or only mildly <u>elevated ICP</u>





#### Etiology [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HVWKD40)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IVWYD40)




* <u>Idiopathic</u> (iNPH, most common in adults > 60 years of age)

* Possible secondary causes that result in obstruction and/or <u>fibrosis</u> of subarachnoid villi

* Inflammatory diseases of the <u>CNS</u> (e.g., <u>meningitis</u>)

* <u>Intraventricular hemorrhage</u>

* Subarachnoidal hemorrhage





#### Pathophysiology [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HVWKD40)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IVWYD40)




* ↓ <u>CSF</u> absorption → <u>CSF</u> accumulation → enlargement of the ventricle

* <u>ICP</u>: normal or only mildly elevated because ventricular dilation compensates for the slow accumulation of <u>CSF</u>





#### Clinical features [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HVWKD40)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IVWYD40)




A classic triad of:




* Gait abnormalities: frequent falls, broad-based gait with short shuffling steps (<u>gait apraxia</u>, also known as <u>magnetic gait</u>)

* <u>Dementia</u>: short attention span, loss of motivation, disturbances of spatial orientation [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/EpX8r_)

* <u>Urinary incontinence</u>

* Initially, only increased urgency and frequency of <u>micturition</u>; later also <u>urge incontinence</u>

* Dilation of the <u>lateral ventricles</u> results in a distortion of the fibers of the corona radiata, which causes a loss of inhibition of <u>detrusor</u> activity.

* Incontinence worsens as cognitive impairment progresses.

* Gait disturbances can make reaching the toilet more difficult, leading to further incontinence-related issues.





> [!TIP]
> <u>Normal pressure hydrocephalus</u> does not manifest with <u>signs of increased ICP</u> (e.g., <u>headache</u>, <u>papilledema</u>).




> [!NOTE]
> Patients present with the classic triad of the 3 Ws: Wet (<u>urinary incontinence</u>), Wacky (<u>dementia</u>), and Wobbly (<u>gait apraxia</u>).

Normal pressure hydrocephalus




#### Diagnostics [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HVWKD40)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sVWtD40)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GVWBD40)




For additional diagnostics in patients with <u>dementia</u>, see also “<u>Initial studies in dementia</u>.”




* Imaging: <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u> (preferred) ; , CT head  

* <u>Ventriculomegaly</u> without or out of <u>proportion</u> to sulcal enlargement

* No evidence of <u>CSF flow</u> obstruction

* Periventricular <u>white matter</u> changes are often seen.

* CSF tap test: <u>lumbar puncture</u> with measurement of <u>opening pressure</u>, followed by removal of 30–50 mL of <u>CSF</u>

* <u>Opening pressure</u> is typically normal or mildly elevated: 5–18 mm Hg

* Gait is assessed immediately before and after the test.

* Gait improvement following <u>CSF</u> removal supports the diagnosis and can help predict response to <u>cerebral shunt</u> placement.

* <u>CSF analysis</u>: to assess for causes of secondary <u>NPH</u> (e.g., <u>chronic meningitis</u>)





> [!TIP]
> <u>NPH</u> is diagnosed based on typical clinical features, evidence of <u>communicating hydrocephalus</u> on imaging, and normal or only mildly elevated <u>opening pressure</u>. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GVWBD40)




> [!WARNING]
> Gait improvement following <u>high-volume CSF removal</u> supports the diagnosis of <u>NPH</u>, but lack of improvement does not rule it out. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/IVWYD40)

## Diagnosis

The diagnosis of hydrocephalus is made on imaging. For the initial approach to patients with <u>signs of elevated ICP</u>, see “<u>Diagnostics for elevated ICP</u>.”




### Imaging [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VeWGB40)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f1WkT40)




* Modalities

* Antenatal period and children with an open <u>anterior fontanelle</u> (< 18 months of age): <u>ultrasonography</u>

* Adults and children with a closed <u>anterior fontanelle</u> (≥ 18 months of age): <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u>;  (preferred) , CT head      [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AVWRC40)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f1WkT40)

* Findings

* <u>Ventriculomegaly</u> without or out of <u>proportion</u> to sulcal enlargement 

* <u>Communicating hydrocephalus</u>: All ventricles are dilated.

* <u>Noncommunicating hydrocephalus</u>: Ventricles upstream of the obstruction are dilated.

* Evans <u>ratio</u> (<u>ratio</u> of largest width of frontal horns to largest <u>biparietal diameter</u>): > 30%

* Findings of underlying cause, e.g., congenital <u>malformations</u> or tumors

### <u>Lumbar puncture</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AVWRC40)




Consider the following with specialist guidance if no <u>contraindications to lumbar puncture</u> are present:




* <u>Opening pressure</u> measurement: to estimate <u>ICP</u>

* <u>CSF tap test</u>: if <u>NPH</u> is suspected

* <u>CSF analysis</u>: e.g., to assess for evidence of <u>meningitis</u> or <u>neoplastic</u> seeding





> [!WARNING]
> To avoid precipitating <u>brain herniation</u>, consider <u>lumbar puncture</u> only after intracranial lesions with <u>mass effect</u> have been ruled out on imaging. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AVWRC40)

## Differential diagnoses

### Hydrocephalus ex vacuo




* Definition: <u>Hydrocephalus ex vacuo</u> is often classified as a form of hydrocephalus, however, this is a misnomer as it is not a true hydrocephalus. The ventricles and <u>subarachnoid space</u> appear enlarged secondary to loss of <u>brain</u> tissue; however, <u>intracranial pressure</u> and flow of <u>cerebrospinal fluid</u> are normal.

* Etiology

* Primary <u>cerebral atrophy</u>

* Cerebral destructive lesions or degeneration

* <u>Alzheimer disease</u>

* <u>Huntington disease</u>

* <u>Frontotemporal dementia</u>

* <u>AIDS</u>

* Pathophysiology

* Loss of <u>brain</u> tissue results in the expansion of the <u>subarachnoid space</u> filled with <u>CSF</u>

* The ventricles appear dilated as well, with an apparent increase in <u>CSF</u> because of reduced <u>brain</u> tissue. However, there is no increase in <u>CSF production</u>, decreased <u>CSF</u> absorption, or obstruction. [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KpXUq_)

* Clinical features

* Usually asymptomatic (<u>signs of elevated ICP</u> and <u>NPH</u> triad are absent)

* Symptoms of the underlying condition (see “Etiology” and “Pathophysiology” above)

* Diagnostics

* Resembles hydrocephalus on imaging (i.e., enlarged <u>CSF</u> spaces, especially <u>lateral ventricles</u>)

* Cortical <u>atrophy</u> may be prominent.

* <u>ICP</u> is normal.

## Treatment

### General principles [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VeWGB40)




* Management must be guided by neurosurgery.

* Temporizing <u>CSF</u> drainage and/or medications may be used in the acute setting for <u>management of elevated ICP</u>.

* Definitive treatment involves surgical intervention for permanent <u>CSF</u> drainage (e.g., through a <u>ventriculoperitoneal shunt</u>).





> [!TIP]
> Initiate treatment promptly, as most types of hydrocephalus are progressive and can result in neurological damage if left untreated.




### Temporizing measures [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VeWGB40)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/m1WVh40)




* Interim drainage procedures: typically used for acute posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus or if infection precludes definitive treatment

* Serial <u>lumbar punctures</u> or lumbar drain placement: for <u>communicating hydrocephalus</u>

* Ventricular tap: for <u>preterm infants</u> with <u>intraventricular hemorrhage</u> (performed through the open <u>anterior fontanelle</u>)

* <u>External ventricular drain</u>: for <u>noncommunicating hydrocephalus</u> (e.g., after <u>subarachnoid hemorrhage</u>)

* Pharmacotherapy: may be considered, although the benefit is likely limited  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/m1WVh40)

* <u>Diuretics</u>;  (e.g., <u>furosemide</u>, <u>acetazolamide</u>): to reduce <u>CSF production</u>

* Intraventricular fibrinolytic agents: for acute hydrocephalus following subarachnoid or <u>intraventricular hemorrhage</u>





### Definitive treatment [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VeWGB40)




#### Cerebral shunt




* Definition: a definitive treatment for hydrocephalus that diverts excess <u>CSF</u> from the ventricles to a body cavity where it is absorbed into the <u>systemic circulation</u>

* Types

* <u>Ventriculoperitoneal shunt</u>: most common

* Others: e.g., <u>ventriculoatrial shunt</u>, <u>ventriculopleural shunt</u>

* Components

* Inflow catheter

* Reservoir

* Shunt valve

* Outflow catheter

* Complications [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MgWMDk0)

* Shunt infection

* Most commonly with <u>gram-positive bacteria</u> (e.g., <u>Staphylococcus epidermidis</u>)

* Clinical features include <u>fever</u>, <u>headache</u>, and <u>signs of elevated ICP</u>.

* Initial diagnostics: <u>CSF analysis</u> with cultures , further <u>septic workup</u> as indicated

* Management: immediate neurosurgical consult for shunt removal and <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* See also “<u>Management of neurosurgical device-related infection</u>.”

* Shunt obstruction

* A neurosurgical emergency that manifests with <u>signs of elevated ICP</u>

* Initial diagnostics: <u>shunt series</u> to confirm shunt placement and integrity, CT head

* Management: immediate neurosurgical consult for shunt revision

* Shunt overdrainage or underdrainage

* Underdrainage: can lead to <u>elevated ICP</u>

* Overdrainage: can lead to <u>intracranial hypotension syndrome</u>

* Slit ventricle syndrome: ventricular collapse and shunt obstruction due to chronic <u>CSF</u> overdrainage  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/geWFy40)





> [!WARNING]
> Shunt infection and obstruction are neurosurgical emergencies.

#### Other surgical procedures [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VeWGB40)




* Endoscopic third <u>ventriculostomy</u>: for hydrocephalus due to obstruction at or <u>distal</u> to the <u>cerebral aqueduct</u>

* Cerebral aqueductoplasty: a procedure in which a stenotic or closed <u>cerebral aqueduct</u> is opened to allow adequate passage of <u>CSF</u> between the third and <u>fourth ventricles</u>

* Excision of obstructive lesion: e.g., <u>posterior fossa</u> <u>tumor</u>
