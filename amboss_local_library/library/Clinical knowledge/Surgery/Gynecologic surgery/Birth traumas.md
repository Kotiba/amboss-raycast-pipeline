## Summary

<u>Birth</u> trauma is an injury to the <u>newborn</u> caused by mechanical forces during <u>birth</u>. <u>Risk factors</u> include <u>macrosomia</u>, abnormal <u>fetal presentation</u>, prolonged or rapid <u>labor</u>, and forceps or <u>vacuum deliveries</u>. <u>Soft tissue</u> injuries of the scalp include benign <u>cephalohematoma</u> and <u>caput succedaneum</u>, as well as subgaleal hemorrhages, which are associated with a high risk of significant blood loss and require monitoring. The most common skeletal injury is the <u>clavicle fracture</u>, which is often asymptomatic and heals spontaneously within 7–10 days. Skeletal or muscular <u>birth</u> injuries may cause <u>torticollis</u>, a unilateral contraction of the <u>sternocleidomastoid muscle</u> with a resulting head tilt. Other <u>birth</u> injuries include nerve damage, such as <u>brachial plexus injury</u> and <u>facial nerve palsy</u>, which may cause temporary <u>muscle weakness</u> or <u>paralysis</u>. The prognosis of <u>birth</u> traumas is usually favorable, with most injuries resolving spontaneously within weeks to months.

---

## Risk factors

* <u>Macrosomia</u> or anatomical abnormalities

* Extremely <u>premature infants</u>; <u>low birth weight</u>

* Abnormal <u>fetal presentation</u>

* <u>Breech presentation</u>

* <u>Shoulder dystocia</u>

* Forceps-assisted delivery or <u>vacuum delivery</u>

* Prolonged or rapid <u>labor</u>

* Small maternal stature

## Neonatal soft tissue injuries

<u>Soft tissue</u> injuries of the scalp in <u>infants</u> are mostly caused by shearing forces during vacuum or <u>forceps delivery</u>.




* Head molding  

* Transient deformation of the head into an elongated shape due to external compression of the fetal head as it passes through the <u>birth canal</u> during <u>labor</u>

* Typically resolves within a few days after the <u>birth</u>

* Caput succedaneum: benign <u>edema</u> of the scalp tissue that extends across the <u>cranial suture lines</u>   

* Firm swelling; pits if gentle pressure is applied

* No treatment required; resolves within hours or days

* Cephalohematoma: subperiosteal <u>hematoma</u> that is limited to <u>cranial suture lines</u>   

* Complications: calcification of the <u>hematoma</u>, secondary infection

* No treatment required; resolves within several weeks or months

* Subgaleal hemorrhage  

* Rupture of the <u>emissary veins</u> and bleeding between the <u>periosteum</u> of the <u>skull</u> and the <u>aponeurosis</u> that may extend across the suture lines

* Associated with a high risk of significant hemorrhage and <u>hemorrhagic shock</u>

## Birth-related clavicle fracture

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: most common <u>fracture</u> during <u>birth</u> (∼ 2% of deliveries)

* Etiology [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ls1wvR0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/os10DR0)

* Mostly occurs during <u>normal labor and delivery</u>

* May be associated with high <u>birth</u> weight (e.g., due to <u>macrosomia</u>)

* Clinical features

* Usually asymptomatic

* Possible pseudoparalysis

* Bone irregularities, <u>crepitus</u>, and tenderness over the <u>clavicle</u> possible on palpation

* Possible <u>brachial plexus</u> palsy

* Diagnostics: : <u>clinical diagnosis</u>; <u>X-ray</u>;  only indicated in cases of gross bone deformation

* Treatment

* Reassurance and promote gentle handling of the arm (e.g., while dressing)

* To avoid discomfort, pin shirt sleeve to the front of the shirt with the arm flexed at 90 degrees

* Consider <u>analgesics</u>

* Follow-up 2 weeks later to confirm proper healing: via clinical findings of a <u>callus</u> formation, and possibly an <u>x-ray</u>

* Usually self-resolves within 2–3 weeks without surgical intervention or long-term complications

## Facial nerve palsy due to birth trauma

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: most common <u>cranial nerve</u> injury during <u>birth</u>

* Pathomechanism

* Injury occurs during <u>forceps-assisted delivery</u> (most common)

* Prolonged <u>birth</u> in which the head is pressed against the maternal sacral promontory

* Clinical features
* <u>Peripheral facial nerve palsy</u> : difficulty feeding, incomplete <u>eye</u> closure, absent <u>nasolabial fold</u>

* Treatment: <u>eye</u> care with artificial tears and ointment

* Prognosis: spontaneous recovery in 90% of cases within several weeks

---

## Neonatal brachial plexus palsy

* Excessive <u>lateral</u> <u>traction</u> on the neck during delivery → injury to the upper trunk of the <u>brachial plexus</u> → <u>Erb palsy</u> (most common <u>iatrogenic</u> <u>brachial plexus injury</u> during delivery)

* Excessive <u>traction</u> on the arm during delivery → injury to the lower trunk of the <u>brachial plexus</u> → <u>Klumpke palsy</u>

* Prognosis: approx. 25% of affected <u>infants</u> experience persistent functional impairment [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/K4XU4y)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Brachial plexus injury</u> is associated with <u>shoulder dystocia</u>, which more commonly leads to <u>Erb palsy</u> than <u>Klumpke palsy</u>.
