## Summary

Distal radius fracture is a common <u>fracture</u> of the arm, occurring most frequently in individuals 10–30 years of age and in those older than 65. The injury can be caused by low-energy trauma (common in women with <u>osteoporosis</u>) or high-energy trauma (e.g., <u>sports injuries</u> or <u>motor vehicle accidents</u>). Clinical features include wrist <u>pain</u> and tenderness, <u>soft tissue</u> swelling, visible deformity, and decreased <u>range of motion</u> at the <u>wrist joint</u>. Diagnosis is confirmed by <u>x-ray</u>. Nondisplaced <u>stable fractures</u> are typically managed with <u>closed reduction</u> and <u>immobilization</u>. <u>Fractures</u> that are open, unstable, comminuted, and/or accompanied by <u>neurovascular injury</u> are usually managed surgically.

## Etiology

* Mechanism of injury [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)

* <u>Fall onto an outstretched hand</u> (most common)  

* <u>Dorsiflexed</u> wrist (a typical protective action used to break one's fall) → extension <u>fracture</u> (<u>Colles fracture</u>)

* <u>Palmar</u>-flexed wrist → <u>flexion</u> <u>fracture</u> (<u>Smith fracture</u>)

* High-energy trauma, e.g., <u>MVC</u>, contact sports

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* <u>Osteoporosis</u>

* See “<u>Etiology of fractures</u>.”

## Classification

This section lists common types of <u>distal</u> <u>radial fractures</u> and their mechanisms of injury. See “<u>Fracture classification</u>” for general principles of <u>fracture</u> description. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Colles fracture  

* Result of a fall on an extended wrist

* The <u>distal</u> fragment is usually radially angulated and dorsally displaced.

* Intraarticular extension is possible.

* Smith fracture  

* Result of a fall onto a flexed wrist or direct injury to the back of the wrist

* The <u>distal</u> fragment is volarly angulated and volarly displaced.

* Intraarticular extension, neurovascular compromise, and/or instability are more likely than in Colles <u>fractures</u>.

* Barton fracture  

* Result of a fall on an extended wrist.

* Intraarticular <u>fracture</u> and <u>dislocation</u>

* The radiocarpal segment is avulsed and dorsally displaced.

* Reverse Barton fracture  

* Result of a fall on a flexed wrist

* An intraarticular <u>fracture</u> and <u>dislocation</u>

* The radiocarpal segment is avulsed and volarly displaced.

* Hutchinson fracture (<u>Chauffeur fracture</u>) 

* Result of a direct blow to the radial portion of the wrist

* The <u>radial styloid</u> is intraarticularly avulsed.

* Die-punch fracture  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ty16ej0)

* Result of an axial or transverse load through the lunate into the radius

* Intraarticular <u>depression</u> <u>fracture</u> of the lunate fossa of the <u>distal</u> radius

## Clinical features

* <u>Pain</u>, tenderness, and <u>soft tissue</u> swelling

* Reduced <u>range of motion</u> at the <u>wrist joint</u>

* Wrist deformities based on the type of <u>fracture</u>

* <u>Colles fracture</u>: dorsally displaced and dorsally angulated <u>fracture</u> (bayonet or dinner fork deformity)

* <u>Smith fracture</u>: garden spade deformity

* See “<u>Fracture signs</u>.”

## Diagnosis

### Clinical evaluation [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)




* <u>Neurovascular exam</u>

* Assess radial and <u>ulnar artery</u> pulses and <u>capillary refill time</u>.

* Evaluate for <u>median nerve injury</u>  , <u>radial nerve injury</u> , and <u>ulnar nerve injury</u>.   [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)

* Repeat the exam after reduction and again after <u>immobilization</u>.

* <u>Skin</u> exam: Evaluate for <u>laceration</u>, tearing, and tenting.





> [!WARNING]
> Repeat the <u>neurovascular exam</u> after reduction and again after <u>immobilization</u> of the <u>radial fracture</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

### Imaging [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uz1puj0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cz1qEj0)




* <u>X-ray</u>: <u>anterior</u>-<u>posterior</u>, <u>lateral</u>, and oblique views of the wrist (including the <u>carpal bones</u>) 

* Assess angulation, rotational deformity, shortening, <u>joint</u> alignment, and comminution.

* Radial inclination: <u>Inclination</u> ≥ 10–15° indicates acceptable <u>fracture reduction</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uz1puj0)

* Normal range: 15–25° [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j-1_Cj0)

* On <u>posterior</u>-<u>anterior</u> view, measure the angle between: 

* A line from the <u>radial styloid</u> to the ulnar border of <u>distal</u> radius

* A line across the articular surface perpendicular to the long axis of the radius

* Volar inclination: <u>Inclination</u> > 20° indicates a potentially <u>unstable fracture</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uz1puj0)

* Normal range: 10–25° [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j-1_Cj0)

* On <u>lateral</u> view, measure the angle between:

* A line along the <u>dorsal</u> to <u>palmar</u> articular surface of radius

* A line across the articular surface perpendicular to the long axis of the radius

* See “<u>Radiographic signs of a fracture</u>.”

* CT wrist: may be required for preoperative planning  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)





> [!WARNING]
> Obtain a second set of <u>x-rays</u> after reduction and <u>immobilization</u> of the <u>fracture</u>.

## Differential diagnoses

See also “<u>Types of distal radius fractures</u>.”




* <u>Ligamentous injury</u>

* <u>Triangular fibrocartilage complex sprain</u>

* <u>Ulnar styloid fracture</u>

* <u>Lunate dislocation</u>

* <u>Perilunate dislocation</u>

* <u>Volar intercalated segment instability</u> (<u>VISI</u>)

### Nonoperative management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




Nondisplaced and <u>stable fractures</u> are typically managed with <u>closed reduction</u> and <u>immobilization</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)




* <u>Closed reduction</u> while applying longitudinal <u>traction</u> through the fingers either manually or using a finger trap

* Initial <u>immobilization</u> in <u>sugar tong splint</u>

* Short arm cast when <u>edema</u> resolves

* Postreduction <u>x-rays</u> and serial exams to evaluate for subsequent displacement

* Cast removal after 6 weeks

* See also “<u>Conservative management of fractures</u>.”





> [!TIP]
> The radius should be realigned to its normal position after <u>fracture reduction</u>.

### Operative management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oz10Fj0)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Iz1Y8j0)




#### Indications [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4y132j0)




Operative fixation in patients ≥ 65 years of age does not improve long-term functional outcomes.  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4y132j0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vz1Auj0)




* Open, significantly displaced, intraarticular, and/or <u>unstable fractures</u>

* <u>Neurovascular injury</u>

* Any of the following post-reduction radiographic signs of instability:

* > 3 mm radial shortening

* ≥ 10° <u>dorsal</u> tilt

* Intraarticular step-off > 2 mm

* Concurrent <u>ulnar fracture</u>

* <u>Fracture-dislocation</u>





#### Common techniques




All procedures require postoperative <u>immobilization</u> of the forearm and wrist.




* <u>Open reduction internal fixation</u>: Fixed-angle <u>volar</u> plates are used for displaced, unstable, and/or involve <u>osteoporotic</u> bone.

* <u>K-wire</u> fixation: typically limited to patients with minimal <u>fracture</u> comminution and healthy bone

* <u>External fixation</u>: typically used in patients with severe <u>soft tissue</u> injury and/or <u>polytrauma</u>
