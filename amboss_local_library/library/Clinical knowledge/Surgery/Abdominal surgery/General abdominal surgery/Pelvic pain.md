## Summary

<u>Pelvic</u> <u>pain</u>, whether chronic or acute, presents a diagnostic challenge because of its diverse etiologies and potential for significant <u>morbidity</u>. Common causes are gastrointestinal (e.g., <u>appendicitis</u>, <u>diverticulitis</u>), genitourinary (e.g., <u>urinary tract infection</u>, <u>renal colic</u>), and musculoskeletal. In female patients, obstetric and gynecologic causes, such as <u>ectopic pregnancy</u> and <u>ovarian torsion</u>, require urgent attention. <u>Testicular torsion</u> must be ruled out in male patients presenting with testicular <u>pain</u>. Assessment begins with a focused history encompassing surgical, sexual, obstetric, and <u>social history</u>, alongside a <u>physical examination</u> that may include a <u>digital rectal examination</u>, <u>pelvic examination</u>, and <u>palpation of the inguinal canal</u>. Basic diagnostics include <u>urinalysis</u>, <u>POCUS</u>, and <u>β-hCG</u> for female patients of reproductive age. Additional investigations, such as CT <u>pelvis</u> and abdomen with IV contrast or <u>STI</u> workup, may be ordered based on the suspected cause. Chronic <u>pelvic</u> <u>pain</u> typically involves consultation with specialists. Initial management prioritizes stabilization of critically ill patients. Subsequent management options depend on the underlying cause and may include surgical intervention, pharmacotherapy, <u>physical therapy</u>, or lifestyle modifications tailored to the patient's needs and preferences.




See “<u>Acute abdomen</u>” for the management of other related symptoms. See also “<u>Dyspareunia in women</u>.”
