## Summary

Acute <u>pancreatitis</u> is an inflammatory condition of the <u>pancreas</u> most commonly caused by <u>gallstones</u> or <u>alcohol</u> use in adults and biliary disease and medications in children. The typical manifestation includes sudden, severe epigastric <u>pain</u> that radiates to the back, <u>nausea and vomiting</u>, and epigastric tenderness on palpation. Elevation of serum <u>lipase</u> or <u>amylase</u> ≥ 3× <u>ULN</u> and/or characteristic findings of acute <u>pancreatitis</u> on <u>cross-sectional imaging</u> (e.g., abdominal <u>ultrasound</u>, <u>contrast-enhanced CT</u> abdomen) confirm the diagnosis. Clinical scores (e.g., <u>Ranson criteria</u>, <u>APACHE II</u>) are used to predict the severity and prognosis of <u>pancreatitis</u>. Initial management is primarily supportive and includes <u>fluid resuscitation</u>, <u>analgesia</u>, <u>antiemetics</u>, and early <u>enteral nutrition</u> as tolerated. The underlying cause should be identified and managed to prevent recurrence (e.g., <u>cholecystectomy</u> for <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>, long-term lipid-lowering therapy for <u>hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis</u>). Localized complications of <u>pancreatitis</u> include <u>necrosis</u> (<u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u>), which may become infected, <u>pancreatic pseudocysts</u>, and <u>walled-off necrosis</u>. Systemic complications include <u>sepsis</u>, <u>ARDS</u>, organ failure, and <u>shock</u>. Complications of <u>pancreatitis</u> are associated with significant <u>morbidity</u> and mortality.

## Etiology

### Most common causes [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R-blCw)




* Biliary pancreatitis (∼ 40% of cases; mostly caused by <u>gallstones</u>)

* <u>Alcohol</u>-induced (∼ 20% of cases)

* <u>Idiopathic</u> (∼ 25% of cases)





### Other causes [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R-blCw)




* Hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis: caused by <u>severe hypertriglyceridemia</u> (> 1,000 mg/dL)

* <u>Hypercalcemia</u>

* Post-<u>ERCP</u>

* Drug-induced pancreatitis

* <u>Steroids</u>

* <u>Azathioprine</u>

* <u>Sulfonamides</u>

* Loop and <u>thiazide diuretics</u>

* <u>Estrogen</u>

* <u>Protease inhibitors</u>

* <u>NRTIs</u>

* <u>Anticonvulsants</u> (e.g., <u>valproate</u>)

* <u>Metronidazole</u>

* <u>Scorpion stings</u>

* Viral infections (e.g., <u>coxsackievirus B</u>, <u>mumps</u>)

* Trauma (especially in children)

* Autoimmune and rheumatological disorders (e.g., Sjögren syndrome)

* <u>Pancreas divisum</u>

* Hereditary (e.g., mutation of <u>PRSS1</u> <u>gene</u>, <u>cystic fibrosis</u>) [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i-bJCw)

* <u>Cholesterol embolism</u>





> [!NOTE]
> I <u>GET</u> SMASHED: Idiopathic, Gallstones, Ethanol, Trauma, Steroids, Mumps, Autoimmune, Scorpion venom, Hypercalcemia and hypertriglyceridemia, ERCP, and Drugs are causes of acute <u>pancreatitis</u>.

## Pathophysiology

### Sequence of events



1. * Intrapancreatic activation of <u>pancreatic enzymes</u>: secondary to <u>pancreatic</u> ductal outflow obstruction (e.g., <u>gallstones</u>, <u>cystic fibrosis</u>) or direct injury to <u>pancreatic acinar cells</u> (e.g., <u>alcohol</u>, drugs)
2. * Increased proteolytic and lipolytic enzyme activity → destruction of <u>pancreatic</u> <u>parenchyma</u>
3. * Attraction of inflammatory cells (<u>neutrophils</u>, <u>macrophages</u>) → release of inflammatory <u>cytokines</u> → <u>pancreatic</u> inflammation (<u>pancreatitis</u>)




### Sequelae of <u>pancreatitis</u>




* <u>Capillary leakage</u>: release of inflammatory <u>cytokines</u> and vascular injury by <u>pancreatic</u> enzymes → <u>vasodilation</u> and increased vascular permeability → shift of fluid from the <u>intravascular space</u> into the <u>interstitial space</u> (<u>third-space fluid loss</u>) → <u>hypotension</u>, <u>tachycardia</u>, warm and flushed <u>skin</u> → <u>distributive shock</u>
* In severe cases, the <u>third spacing</u> of fluid from this inflammatory response can lead to <u>hypovolemic shock</u>.

* <u>Pancreatic</u> <u>necrosis</u>: uncorrected <u>hypotension</u> and <u>third-spacing</u> → decreased end-organ <u>perfusion</u> → <u>multiorgan dysfunction</u> (mainly renal) and <u>pancreatic</u> <u>necrosis</u>

* <u>Hypocalcemia</u>: <u>lipase</u> breaks down peripancreatic and <u>mesenteric</u> fat;  → release of free <u>fatty acids</u> that bind <u>calcium</u> → <u>hypocalcemia</u> (<u>fatty saponification</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/__a5IM)

## Clinical features

### Symptoms




* Constant, severe epigastric <u>pain</u>

* Classically radiating towards the back

* Worse after meals and when <u>supine</u>

* Improves on leaning forwards

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* <u>Fever</u>

* If pulmonary complications are present: <u>chest pain</u>, <u>dyspnea</u>





### Examination findings




* General

* <u>Signs of shock</u>: <u>tachycardia</u>, <u>hypotension</u>, <u>oliguria</u>/<u>anuria</u>

* Possibly <u>jaundice</u> in patients with <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>

* <u>Abdominal examination</u>

* Abdominal tenderness, distention, guarding

* <u>Ileus</u>  with reduced <u>bowel sounds</u> and tympany on percussion

* <u>Ascites</u>

* <u>Skin</u> changes (rare) 

* Cullen sign: periumbilical <u>ecchymosis</u> and discoloration (bluish-red)

* Grey Turner sign: flank <u>ecchymosis</u> with discoloration

* Fox sign: <u>ecchymosis</u> over the <u>inguinal ligament</u>

* <u>Pulmonary examination</u>: signs of <u>pleural effusion</u> and/or <u>ARDS</u> may be present

## Diagnosis

### Diagnostic criteria for acute pancreatitis [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WBYP-r)




Two of the three following criteria should be met for a <u>diagnosis of acute pancreatitis</u> to be made.




* Characteristic abdominal <u>pain</u>

* ↑ Serum <u>pancreatic enzymes</u>: <u>lipase</u> or <u>amylase</u> ≥ 3× <u>ULN</u>

* Characteristic findings of acute <u>pancreatitis</u> on <u>cross-sectional imaging</u> (e.g., abdominal <u>ultrasound</u>, <u>contrast-enhanced CT</u> abdomen)





### Approach [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJYMuJ)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)




* All patients

* Perform <u>laboratory studies</u> to:

* Establish the diagnosis: serum <u>lipase</u> and/or <u>amylase</u> levels

* Determine severity: <u>CBC</u>, <u>BMP</u>, <u>ABG</u>, <u>LDH</u>, <u>inflammatory markers</u>, serum <u>calcium</u>

* Evaluate for the underlying etiology: <u>liver chemistries</u>, serum or plasma <u>triglyceride</u> levels

* Obtain <u>ultrasound</u> abdomen.

* Diagnostic uncertainty: Perform <u>contrast-enhanced CT</u> (<u>CECT</u>) abdomen.

* Confirmed diagnosis

* Perform further diagnostics as needed to determine the etiology (e.g., <u>MRCP</u> for suspected <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>).

* Calculate severity scores of acute <u>pancreatitis</u> to estimate severity and prognosis.

* In patients with <u>severe pancreatitis</u>, consider <u>CECT</u> abdomen 5–7 days after the onset of symptoms to assess for <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u>.





> [!TIP]
> Simultaneously conduct tests to establish the diagnosis, assess severity, and rule out <u>differential diagnoses of acute abdominal pain</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> Acute <u>pancreatitis</u> is a <u>medical emergency</u>; begin <u>fluid resuscitation</u> as soon as it is suspected.

### Imaging




#### <u>Ultrasound</u> abdomen [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fTckJb0)




* Indications: first-line imaging modality for all patients

* Supportive findings 

* <u>Features of acute pancreatitis</u> (visible in 20% of cases) [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* Enlarged <u>hypoechoic</u> <u>pancreas</u> (<u>pancreatic</u> <u>edema</u>)

* Peripancreatic fluid and/or <u>ascites</u>

* Features of <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>

* <u>Cholelithiasis</u>  and/or <u>gallbladder</u> sludge  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* Dilated biliary tree

* Evidence of complications: <u>pancreatic pseudocysts</u>, <u>walled-off necrosis</u> (typically > 4 weeks from symptom onset)  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

#### CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u> with IV contrast  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)




* Indications

* Diagnostic uncertainty (e.g., typical clinical features in a patient with moderately elevated <u>pancreatic enzymes</u>)

* Acute deterioration or lack of improvement within 48–72 hours of admission

* <u>Severe pancreatitis</u> : optimally performed > 5–7 days after symptom onset  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* To evaluate for underlying etiology if routine diagnostic studies are negative [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)

* Findings

* <u>Features of acute pancreatitis</u>

* Enlargement of the <u>pancreatic</u> <u>parenchyma</u> with <u>edema</u>

* Indistinct <u>pancreatic</u> margins with surrounding <u>fat stranding</u>

* Peripancreatic free fluid

* Evidence of complications

* <u>Necrotizing pancreatitis</u>: nonenhancing areas of <u>pancreatic</u> <u>parenchyma</u>

* Acute <u>necrotic</u> collections: ill-defined, heterogeneous appearance with varying densities

* <u>Walled-off necrosis</u>: an encapsulated collection of <u>necrotic</u> material, usually occurring > 4 weeks after the onset of <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u>

* Infection: air within the <u>pancreatic</u> or peripancreatic tissue or fluid collections

## Severity grading and prognostic scores

There are several scores used to assess the severity and prognosis of acute <u>pancreatitis</u>. The most commonly used and validated scores are described here.




### Revised Atlanta grades of severity [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WBYP-r)




The <u>revised Atlanta grades of severity</u> classify <u>pancreatitis</u> as mild, moderately severe, or severe, depending on the presence of organ failure. Organ failure can be determined using the <u>modified Marshall scoring system for organ dysfunction</u>.




* Mild acute pancreatitis: no organ failure and no local or systemic complications

* Moderately severe acute pancreatitis: transient organ failure (< 48 hours) and/or local or systemic complications

* Severe acute pancreatitis: persistent organ failure (> 48 hours)





> [!WARNING]
> Patients with organ failure at presentation or within the first 24 hours of admission should be classified as having <u>severe pancreatitis</u>. If organ failure resolves within 48 hours, patients can be reclassified as having <u>moderately severe acute pancreatitis</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WBYP-r)




![Modified Marshall Score](../../../../../media/wg9kgzk40i5f0eb9oq2n5ukl.jpg)

Modified Marshall Score




### CT severity index [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJYMuJ)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3AYSj7)[[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sfctnb0)




The CT severity index for acute pancreatitis (<u>CTSI</u>) and modified CT severity index (<u>MCTSI</u>) can be used to estimate the severity, mortality, and <u>morbidity</u> of acute <u>pancreatitis</u> based on the extent of <u>pancreatic</u> inflammation and <u>necrosis</u> on a CT abdomen performed ideally > 5–7 days (or at least 72 hours) after symptom onset.

## Treatment

The initial management is identical for all <u>etiologies of acute pancreatitis</u> and should be administered without delay. For management in pregnant individuals and children, see “Special patient groups.”




### Acute stabilization [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)




* <u>ABCDE survey</u>

* Hemodynamic and <u>respiratory support</u>

* Maintain <u>NPO</u> status until potential causes of <u>acute abdomen</u> that require emergency <u>surgery</u> have been ruled out.





### Goal-directed IV fluid therapy for acute pancreatitis [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)




#### Fluids and infusion rate [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)




* <u>Crystalloids</u> such as <u>lactated Ringer's solution</u> (<u>LR</u>) are preferred. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)

* Hemodynamically stable patients [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)

* Moderately aggressive <u>IV fluid therapy</u> in the first 24–48 hours 

* <u>Normovolemic</u> patients: <u>LR</u> 1.5 mL/kg/hour [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mddVqK0)

* <u>Hypovolemic</u> patients: <u>LR</u> 10 mL/kg bolus [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[36]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mddVqK0)

* Exercise caution in patients with renal or cardiovascular disease.

* <u>Hemodynamically unstable</u> patients [[37]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/APbRhF)

* Administer rapid fluid bolus (e.g., <u>LR</u> 500–1000 mL IV bolus over 10–30 minutes).

* Repeat as needed based on response.

* See also “<u>Fluid resuscitation</u>” for further detail.





#### Monitoring [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJYMuJ)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/K2cUib0)




* Monitor <u>vitals</u>, <u>oxygen saturation</u>, and urine output every 1–2 hours during the initial period of <u>IV fluid therapy</u>.

* Obtain <u>laboratory studies</u> (<u>CBC</u>, <u>BMP</u>, HCT) every 6–12 hours to monitor adequacy of <u>fluid resuscitation</u> and tissue <u>perfusion</u>.

* Perform serial <u>physical examination</u> every 4–6 hours to assess for <u>abdominal compartment syndrome</u>.





#### Fluid therapy goals in acute pancreatitis [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)




* <u>Heart rate</u> < 120 bpm, <u>MAP</u> 65–85 mm Hg

* Urine output > 0.5–1 mL/kg/hour

* <u>Central venous pressure</u> 8–12 mm Hg, <u>central venous oxygen saturation</u> ≥ 70% [[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dTcopb0)

* <u>Hct</u> 35–44%

* Reduction in <u>BUN</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)





> [!TIP]
> Intravenous <u>fluid resuscitation</u> in the first 12–24 hours has the greatest impact on the <u>clinical outcome</u> of patients with acute <u>pancreatitis</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)




### Supportive therapy




* <u>Analgesics</u>: based on the <u>WHO analgesic ladder</u> [[39]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/odd0IK0)

* NSAIDS (e.g., <u>ketorolac</u> DOSAGE, <u>diclofenac</u> DOSAGE, <u>ibuprofen</u> DOSAGE) [[40]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4wc3Qe0)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VTcGpb0)

* <u>Opioids</u>;  (e.g., <u>hydromorphone</u> DOSAGE, <u>morphine</u> DOSAGE) [[42]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/roYfWJ)

* Consider <u>patient-controlled analgesia</u> for management of severe <u>pain</u>. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)[[41]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VTcGpb0)

* <u>Antiemetics</u> (e.g., <u>ondansetron</u> DOSAGE, <u>metoclopramide</u> DOSAGE)

* <u>Electrolyte repletion</u>





> [!WARNING]
> In concurrent <u>acute kidney injury</u>, avoid <u>NSAIDs</u> and use <u>opioids</u> with caution because of the risk of accumulation. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)




> [!WARNING]
> Prophylactic <u>antibiotics</u> are not recommended, and should only be used in patients with evidence of infected <u>necrosis</u>. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)




### Consults




* <u>Multidisciplinary care</u> is ideal.

* Urgent gastroenterology, <u>surgery</u>, and/or interventional radiology for <u>cholangitis</u>, <u>choledocholithiasis</u>, or localized complications





### Disposition [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)




* Hospital admission is usually required.

* Consider <u>ICU</u> admission in the following cases:

* Organ dysfunction or failure

* Ongoing <u>SIRS</u> or <u>fluid resuscitation</u> requirements

* Significant <u>electrolyte</u> imbalances

* Older age or high-risk comorbidities

* <u>Severe pancreatitis</u> on severity scores

* Refer to a specialist center if the need for surgical or interventional procedures is anticipated.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)





### Nutrition




* Early oral feeding: : low-fat, solid diet as soon as tolerated, ideally within 24 hours  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[43]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j4c_Qc0)

* <u>Enteral tube</u> (nasogastric or nasojejunal): preferred over <u>parenteral nutrition</u> if patients cannot tolerate oral intake  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[44]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/UTcbJb0)

* <u>Parenteral nutrition</u> (total or partial): only in patients who cannot tolerate enteral feeds (e.g., those with persistent <u>paralytic ileus</u>)  [[45]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZzaZrM)





> [!TIP]
> Bowel rest is no longer routinely recommended. <u>Enteral nutrition</u>, via oral route or <u>enteral tube</u>, should be initiated as early as tolerated. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)




### Management of the underlying cause




#### <u>Biliary pancreatitis</u>




* Therapeutic <u>ERCP</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[38]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dTcopb0)

* Indication: <u>biliary pancreatitis</u> associated with <u>cholangitis</u> or persistent <u>CBD</u> obstruction

* Timing: Urgent therapeutic <u>ERCP</u> (within < 24 hours) is indicated if there is concurrent <u>cholangitis</u>.

* Procedure: sphincterotomy and stone removal; see “<u>Treatment of choledocholithiasis</u>”

* Complications: aggravation of <u>pancreatitis</u>, perforation, hemorrhage [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* <u>Cholecystectomy</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[46]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2TcTJb0)

* Indications

* All patients with <u>biliary pancreatitis</u> to prevent recurrence

* Consider in patients with a recurrent episode of acute <u>pancreatitis</u> of unclear etiology [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)

* Timing: recommended during the initial admission for patients with mild <u>biliary pancreatitis</u>





> [!TIP]
> Urgent <u>ERCP</u> is not indicated in acute <u>biliary pancreatitis</u> unless <u>acute cholangitis</u> is present. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)




#### <u>Hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis</u> [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3AYSj7)




* Initiate measures to rapidly decrease <u>triglyceride</u> levels alongside <u>fluid resuscitation</u> and <u>analgesia</u>.

* <u>Insulin therapy</u> DOSAGE; monitor blood <u>glucose</u> levels.

* <u>Plasmapheresis</u> and <u>hemofiltration</u>

* Evaluate for <u>secondary causes of hypertriglyceridemia</u> ; consider screening for <u>familial hypertriglyceridemia</u> if none are present.

* Long-term management

* Initiate long-term lipid-lowering therapy e.g., with <u>fibrates</u>, as soon as tolerated to prevent recurrences.

* Dietary and lifestyle modifications

* See “Treatment of <u>hypertriglyceridemia</u> in adults” for details.





#### <u>Hypercalcemia</u>-induced <u>pancreatitis</u> [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3TcSqb0)




* Initial treatment: See “<u>Treatment of hypercalcemia</u>.”

* Definitive management: Investigate for <u>primary hyperparathyroidism</u>; if this is the underlying cause, perform <u>parathyroidectomy</u>.





#### <u>Alcohol</u>-induced <u>pancreatitis</u>




* Check <u>magnesium</u> and <u>phosphorus</u> levels and replete as needed.  [[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MJYMuJ)[[47]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/34cSQc0)

* <u>Vitamin</u> supplementation (<u>thiamine</u>, <u>pyridoxine</u>)

* See “<u>Treatment of alcohol use disorder</u>” for details.

* Provide <u>counseling on alcohol use disorder</u> before discharge. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qoYC1J)





> [!NOTE]
> "PANCREAS": <u>Perfusion</u> (<u>fluid replacement</u>), <u>Analgesia</u>, Nutrition, Clinical (observation), Radiology (imaging), <u>ERCP</u>, <u>Antibiotics</u> (if indicated), <u>Surgery</u> (surgical intervention, if necessary)

## Special patient groups

### Acute pancreatitis during pregnancy [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d6coPW0)




#### <u>Epidemiology</u>




* <u>Incidence</u> is 1:1000–1:10,000 <u>pregnancies</u>

* More commonly affects <u>multiparous</u> individuals (75%)

* The majority of cases occur in the <u>third trimester</u> (50%), followed by the early <u>postpartum period</u> (38%), and the first and <u>second trimester</u> (12%)





#### Etiology




* Most commonly <u>gallstones</u>, <u>heavy alcohol use</u>, and <u>familial hypertriglyceridemia</u>

* Physiologic changes during <u>pregnancy</u> such as altered <u>progesterone</u> and <u>estrogen</u> levels increase the risk of <u>choledocholithiasis</u>.

* ↑ <u>Progesterone</u> → ↑ pressure on the <u>sphincter of Oddi</u> → <u>bile</u> stasis

* <u>Estrogen</u> alters the composition of <u>bile</u>, making it more lithogenic.





#### Clinical features




Similar to <u>clinical features of acute pancreatitis</u> in nonpregnant individuals




#### Diagnostics




* <u>CBC</u>

* May show physiologic alterations due to <u>pregnancy</u> (e.g., <u>leukocytosis</u>, increased serum <u>amylase</u> and <u>lipase</u>)

* If <u>amylase</u> and/or <u>lipase</u> are > 3 times greater than normal, acute <u>pancreatitis</u> is likely  [[48]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d6coPW0)

* Imaging: abdominal <u>ultrasound</u> or <u>MRI</u> are preferred (e.g., to identify <u>choledocholithiasis</u> or <u>complications of acute pancreatitis</u> such as hemorrhage, <u>edema</u>, or <u>pseudocysts</u>)





#### Treatment




* Identical to that for nonpregnant individuals (see “<u>Treatment of acute pancreatitis</u>.”)

* See “<u>Overview of analgesics to avoid during pregnancy</u>” for further details.





#### Complications




* See “<u>Complications of acute pancreatitis</u>.”

* <u>Pregnancy</u>-related complications: increased risk of <u>preterm labor</u>, <u>premature birth</u>, and/or fetal <u>death</u>





### Acute pancreatitis in children [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C8dqK70)[[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)




#### <u>Epidemiology</u>




<u>Incidence</u> is 3–13 cases per 100,000 people per year. [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)




#### Etiology [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)




* <u>Biliary pancreatitis</u> (most common cause)

* Medications (e.g., <u>valproic acid</u>, <u>mesalamine</u>, <u>glucocorticoids</u>)

* Systemic disease (e.g., metabolic disorders, <u>hemolytic uremic syndrome</u>, <u>Kawasaki disease</u>)

* Underlying genetic conditions (e.g., <u>cystic fibrosis</u>)

* Anatomical anomalies of the <u>pancreas</u> (e.g., <u>pancreas divisum</u>, <u>annular pancreas</u>)

* Trauma and/or <u>child maltreatment</u>

* Infection (e.g., <u>mumps</u>, <u>influenza</u>, herpes <u>viruses</u>, <u>hepatitis</u>)

* Critical illness (e.g., <u>sepsis</u>)

* <u>Autoimmune pancreatitis</u>





> [!TIP]
> Approximately 20% of children have > 1 underlying cause. [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)




#### Clinical features [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)




* Similar to <u>clinical features of acute pancreatitis</u> in adults

* Symptoms common in <u>infants</u> and young children include:

* Generalized abdominal <u>pain</u>

* Irritability





#### Diagnostics




* Similar to <u>diagnostics for acute pancreatitis</u> in adults [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C8dqK70)

* Use adult <u>diagnostic criteria for acute pancreatitis</u>.

* Serum <u>lipase</u> is more reliable than <u>amylase</u> in <u>infants</u>. [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)

* Abdominal <u>ultrasound</u> is the preferred initial imaging modality. [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)

* Studies considered by a specialist (e.g., gastroenterology) may include:

* Genetic studies  [[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C8dqK70)

* <u>Diagnostics for cystic fibrosis</u>

* Stool studies





#### Management of pediatric pancreatitis [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)[[50]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/C8dqK70)




* <u>Treatment of acute pancreatitis</u> in children is similar to adults with the following changes:

* Use pediatric parameters for <u>fluid resuscitation</u> (e.g., <u>lactated Ringer's solution</u> or <u>normal saline</u>).  [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)

* Bolus: 10–20 mL/kg once in <u>hemodynamically unstable</u> patients [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)

* Maintenance rate: 1.5–2 times the typical weight-based maintenance rate for 24–48 hours [[49]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/D8d1K70)

* <u>Pain management</u> according to the <u>WHO analgesic ladder</u> may include both of the following:

* <u>Nonopioid oral analgesia in children</u> (e.g., <u>acetaminophen</u>, <u>NSAIDs</u>)

* <u>Opioids for acute pain</u>

* Consult a specialist (e.g., gastroenterology, <u>surgery</u>) for:

* Clinical deterioration

* Suspected complications

* Consideration of invasive procedure

* Recurrent acute <u>pancreatitis</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Up to 40% of children with acute <u>pancreatitis</u> eventually progress to <u>chronic pancreatitis</u>. [[51]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/w8dhK70)

## Complications

### Necrotizing pancreatitis [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)




* Definition: <u>necrosis</u> of <u>pancreatic</u> and peripancreatic tissue

* Clinical features: <u>fever</u>, persistent <u>tachycardia</u>, or insufficient symptomatic improvement over several days

* Diagnostics: nonenhancing areas of <u>pancreatic</u> <u>parenchyma</u> on <u>CECT</u> abdomen  [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* Treatment [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* Sterile <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u> can usually be <u>managed conservatively</u>. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)

* Encourage <u>enteral nutrition</u> if feasible.

* Provide supplemental <u>nutritional support</u> as needed.

Pancreatic necrosis




### Infected <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)




* Definition: bacterial <u>superinfection</u> of <u>necrotic</u> <u>pancreatic</u> <u>parenchyma</u>

* Clinical features: similar to those of <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u>

* Diagnostics 

* <u>Laboratory studies</u>: persistent or worsening <u>leukocytosis</u>, <u>bacteremia</u>, increasing <u>inflammatory markers</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* <u>CECT</u> abdomen: gas within the <u>pancreas</u> and/or peripancreatic tissue or fluid collections [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WBYP-r)

* <u>Fine-needle aspiration</u> of <u>necrotic</u> areas: not routinely recommended  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/T_a6nM)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aAYQR7)

* Treatment [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* <u>Supportive care</u>: <u>fluid therapy</u>, <u>analgesics</u>, <u>nutritional support</u>

* Broad-spectrum <u>empiric antibiotics</u> with good tissue penetration (e.g., <u>carbapenems</u> DOSAGE, <u>quinolones</u>, third- or higher-generation <u>cephalosporins</u>, <u>metronidazole</u>) for 2–4 weeks [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* Drainage of infected material if there is clinical deterioration or persistence of symptoms despite <u>antibiotic therapy</u>

* Operative <u>pancreatic</u> <u>debridement</u> (necrosectomy) should ideally be performed at least 2–4 weeks after initial presentation.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* Minimally invasive procedures (e.g., <u>image-guided percutaneous drainage</u>) can be performed in the first 2 weeks in seriously ill patients.

* Prognosis: high <u>mortality rate</u> (30%) [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)





### Walled-off necrosis




* Definition

* An encapsulated collection of sterile <u>necrotic</u> material, usually occurring > 4 weeks after the onset of <u>necrotizing pancreatitis</u> [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WBYP-r)

* Previously known as <u>pancreatic abscess</u>

* Diagnostics: CT abdomen with IV contrast showing an encapsulated heterogeneous collection containing fluid and debris [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SAYyQ7)

* Treatment:  (of symptomatic <u>walled-off necrosis</u>): percutaneous drainage or <u>transmural</u> endoscopic necrosectomy [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/J2csib0)

* <u>Pancreatic pseudocyst</u>

* <u>Splenic vein thrombosis</u>

* <u>Pancreatic</u> hemorrhage (blood vessel <u>erosion</u> with bleeding)

* <u>Shock</u>, <u>SIRS</u>, <u>sepsis</u>, <u>DIC</u>

## Prognosis

* Mortality

* In patients without organ failure: < 1%

* In patients with organ failure: ∼ 30%

* Higher mortality in patients with <u>biliary pancreatitis</u> than in patients with <u>alcohol</u>-induced <u>pancreatitis</u>

* <u>Risk factors</u> for severe disease: See “<u>Severity scores for acute pancreatitis</u>.”  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fddk6K0)[[56]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HDYKfr)
