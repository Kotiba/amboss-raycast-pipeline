## Summary

Dyspepsia is a common condition, defined as predominantly epigastric <u>pain</u> lasting at least one month, and is often attributed to conditions affecting the <u>stomach</u>. <u>Heartburn</u> (or <u>pyrosis</u>) is used to describe predominantly esophageal symptoms. Although <u>heartburn</u> and dyspepsia have distinct definitions, the clinical features overlap and may be indistinguishable. Common <u>causes of dyspepsia</u> include <u>Helicobacter pylori infection</u>, <u>gastritis</u>, <u>esophagitis</u>, <u>gastroesophageal reflux</u> (<u>GERD</u>), and <u>peptic ulcer disease</u> (<u>PUD</u>). A thorough <u>medical history</u>, <u>physical examination</u>, screening for <u>risk factors</u> for common etiologies, and assessment of <u>red flag features for dyspepsia</u> is imperative to guide initial management. Patients ≥ 60 years of age with/without <u>red flag features</u> should undergo an <u>EGD</u> to rule out <u>neoplasia</u> before initiating empirical pharmacotherapy. All patients with typical dyspepsia should be tested for <u>H. pylori infection</u> and, if detected, eradication therapy should be initiated. Patients < 60 years of age with typical <u>heartburn</u> and no major <u>red flag features</u> may be initiated on empirical therapy with <u>acid suppression medications</u>, such as <u>proton pump inhibitors</u> (<u>PPIs</u>). Inadequate response to empirical therapy warrants further diagnostics and management. Patients with atypical features should be assessed for other possible etiologies of dyspepsia, such as <u>symptomatic cholelithiasis</u>, <u>chronic pancreatitis</u>, and <u>stable angina</u>. Patients in whom no organic cause can be identified likely have <u>functional dyspepsia</u> (accounts for ∼ 70% of all cases of dyspepsia), the likelihood of which should be assessed based on the <u>Rome IV criteria for functional dyspepsia</u>.

## Approach

Obtain a thorough <u>medical history</u> and <u>physical examination</u>, screen for <u>risk factors</u> for common etiologies (e.g., shared <u>risk factors for PUD, GERD, and gastritis</u>), and evaluate for <u>red flag features</u> in all patients.




### Red flag features of dyspepsia




* <u>Family history</u> of gastrointestinal cancer

* <u>Immunosuppression</u>

* <u>Clinically significant unintentional weight loss</u>

* <u>Anorexia</u>

* <u>Dysphagia</u>, <u>odynophagia</u>

* Early satiety

* Persistent <u>vomiting</u>

* Noncardiac <u>chest pain</u> (specific for patients presenting with <u>heartburn</u>)

* Suspicion of <u>gastrointestinal bleeding</u> (e.g., unexplained <u>iron deficiency anemia</u>, <u>melena</u>)





### Patients ≥ 60 years of age [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MuYM7I)




The following is applicable to patients ≥ 60 years of age with or without <u>red flag features for dyspepsia</u>.




* Refer for <u>EGD</u> to exclude <u>neoplasia</u>.

* Test for the presence of <u>H. pylori</u> during the endoscopy using <u>biopsies</u> and/or a <u>rapid urease test</u>.

* If a clear etiology is identified using <u>EGD</u> (e.g., visualization of <u>peptic ulcer</u>, <u>gastritis</u>), manage accordingly.

* If the etiology remains unclear after <u>EGD</u>, consider further testing based on clinical suspicion.





### Patients < 60 years of age [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MuYM7I)




* <u>Red flags of dyspepsia</u> present: Consider <u>EGD</u> on a case-by-case basis.

* <u>Red flags of dyspepsia</u> absent

* Typical dyspepsia; : Perform noninvasive <u>tests for H. pylori</u> infection, e.g., <u>urea breath test</u>

* Positive: <u>H. pylori eradication therapy</u>

* Negative: trial of acid suppression with <u>proton pump inhibitors</u> (<u>PPIs</u>)

* Typical <u>heartburn</u> : trial of acid suppression with <u>PPIs</u> (empirical therapy) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5K0iSS)





> [!TIP]
> Patients with typical features of <u>GERD</u> and no features of concomitant or past <u>PUD</u> do not require routine <u>testing for H. pylori</u> infection. If <u>H. pylori infection</u> is detected in a patient with <u>GERD</u>, <u>H. pylori eradication therapy</u> should be initiated. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5K0iSS)

## Treatment

### Approach [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/MuYM7I)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5K0iSS)




* Typical <u>heartburn</u> (<u>GERD</u>) [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/4xY39r)

* Trial of <u>acid suppression therapy</u> with <u>PPIs</u> for 8 weeks  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/5K0iSS)

* See “<u>Management of GERD</u>” for details on further management based on response to empirical therapy.

* Typical dyspepsia and negative test for <u>H. pylori</u> infection (<u>PUD</u>, <u>gastritis</u>) [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LOYwG6)

* Trial of <u>acid suppression therapy</u> with <u>PPIs</u> for 8 weeks

* See “<u>Therapeutic approach to PUD</u>” and “<u>Gastritis subtypes</u>” for further management.

* Positive test for <u>H. pylori</u> infection

* <u>H. pylori eradication therapy</u>

* <u>H. pylori eradication confirmation</u> 4–6 weeks after completion of therapy.

* Other etiologies
* See relevant articles for details on the management of conditions diagnosed on <u>EGD</u> and further diagnostics.

* All patients

* Consider <u>antacids</u> for rapid symptomatic relief as needed.

* Implement complementary nonpharmacological measures and lifestyle changes.

### Nonpharmacological recommendations  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/-aXDM9)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OTcIIb0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zaXrM9)




* Dietary recommendations

* Reduce portion size.

* Avoid eating at least 3 hours before lying down (e.g., before going to <u>sleep</u>). [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)

* Avoid foods and beverages that appear to trigger symptoms.  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pO0LtT)

* Physical recommendations

* Patients with <u>obesity</u> should be encouraged to lose weight and should also receive advice on the best approach. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)

* Elevate the head-end of the bed (10–20 cm) for patients who experience symptoms while sleeping. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)

* Reduce or avoid triggers

* Reduce or avoid <u>stress</u>.

* Avoid <u>nicotine</u>, <u>alcohol</u>, and <u>caffeine</u> (especially if they appear to trigger symptoms).

* If possible, avoid medications that may worsen symptoms (e.g., <u>NSAIDs</u>, <u>aspirin</u>, corticoids, <u>calcium channel blockers</u>, <u>diazepam</u>)  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qO0CtT)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/mO0VGT)
