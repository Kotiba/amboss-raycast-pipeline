# Testicular tumors

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Surgery > Urologic surgery > Testicular tumors*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ii0YGf)

---

## Summary

Testicular tumors are the most common solid <u>malignancy</u> in young men. Patients often present with a testicular mass and may report scrotal <u>pain</u> or discomfort. Diagnosis is based on scrotal <u>ultrasound</u> findings and initial serum <u>tumor markers</u>. Patients are referred to urology for a <u>radical inguinal orchiectomy</u>. Clinical staging is determined by <u>tumor</u> type and staging diagnostics (e.g., imaging, postorchiectomy serum <u>tumor markers</u>). Further management may include <u>active surveillance</u>, <u>chemotherapy</u>, <u>radiation therapy</u>, and/or <u>retroperitoneal lymph node dissection</u>. The overall prognosis of testicular tumors is excellent; cure is often possible even in advanced, <u>metastatic</u> stages.

---

## Epidemiology

* Most common solid <u>malignant tumor</u> in young men [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hOdcHJ0)

* <u>Nonseminoma</u> tumors are most common in men aged < 30 years, while <u>seminomas</u> are most common in men aged ≥ 30 years. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/HldKyJ0)




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

## Etiology

<u>Risk factors</u> for testicular tumors include the following: [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sldtyJ0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)




* <u>Cryptorchidism</u> (increased risk for <u>germ cell tumors</u>)

* <u>Contralateral</u> testicular cancer

* Germ cell <u>neoplasia</u> in situ (GCNIS)

* <u>Family history</u> of testicular cancer

* <u>Klinefelter syndrome</u> [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/GldByJ0)

* <u>Infertility</u>

* <u>Hypospadias</u>

> [!TIP]
> <u>hCG</u> is always elevated in <u>choriocarcinoma</u> and sometimes elevated in <u>seminoma</u>. <u>AFP</u> is always elevated in <u>yolk sac tumors</u>. Both <u>AFP</u> and <u>hCG</u> may be elevated in mixed <u>germ cell tumors</u>.




> [!TIP]
> Testicular tumors <u>metastasize</u> early into the <u>retroperitoneum</u> via <u>the lymphatic system</u> (drain to the para-aortic <u>lymph nodes</u> first), with the exception of early hematogenous metastasizing <u>choriocarcinomas</u>.

## Clinical features

### Symptoms [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)




* General

* Typically painless

* Scrotal discomfort

* Scrotal heaviness and/or swelling

* Dull lower abdominal <u>pain</u>

* Endocrine [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vmdAhq0)

* <u>Androgen excess</u>

* Prominent external genitalia, pubic <u>hair</u> growth, accelerated skeletal and muscle development, and mature masculine voice

* May cause <u>precocious puberty</u> in boys

* <u>Estrogen</u> excess: <u>gynecomastia</u>, <u>breast</u> tenderness, and gonadal underdevelopment

* <u>hCG</u> excess: <u>gynecomastia</u>, <u>signs of hyperthyroidism</u>  [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/OYaI6Q)

* <u>Metastatic</u> disease

* <u>Bone metastases</u>: <u>lower back pain</u>, bone <u>pain</u>

* <u>Pulmonary metastases</u>: <u>cough</u>, <u>shortness of breath</u>, <u>hemoptysis</u>





### <u>Physical examination</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)




* Palpable testicular mass (e.g., hard, firm, or fixed areas)

* Negative <u>transillumination</u> test

* Inguinal and/or supraclavicular <u>lymphadenopathy</u>

## Subtypes and variants

### Extragonadal germ cell tumors [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ymddRq0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AmdRRq0)




* Definition: primary <u>germ cell tumors</u> that arise outside of the <u>gonads</u>, anywhere along the body's midline from the <u>pineal gland</u> to the <u>coccyx</u>

* <u>Epidemiology</u>

* 1–5% of all <u>germ cell tumors</u>

* Nonseminomas are more common than <u>seminomas</u>

* Mostly affects young men

* Location

* Midline organs

* <u>Mediastinal</u> > <u>retroperitoneal</u> > intracranial (<u>pineal gland</u> and suprasellar region)

* Sacrococcygeal teratomas

* Most common in <u>infancy</u> or early childhood [[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/JcbsWs)

* Manifest as a mass near the <u>caudal</u> end, near the <u>rectum</u>

* May cause obstruction, <u>weakness</u>, <u>pain</u>, and/or <u>paralysis</u>

* Clinical features: varies based on <u>tumor</u> location and size, e.g.,

* <u>Mediastinal</u>: <u>chest pain</u>, <u>dyspnea</u> (on exertion), <u>cough</u>

* <u>Retroperitoneal</u>: abdominal <u>pain</u>, <u>back pain</u>, weight loss

* Intracranial: <u>headache</u>, <u>nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>, <u>ataxia</u>, change in <u>vision</u>

* Diagnosis

* Varies based on presenting symptoms, e.g.,

* <u>Mediastinal</u>: CT chest

* <u>Retroperitoneal</u>: CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u>

* Intracranial: <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u>

* Scrotal <u>ultrasound</u>: to rule out primary gonadal <u>tumor</u>

* Serum <u>tumor markers</u>: <u>AFP</u>, <u>hCG</u>, <u>lactate dehydrogenase</u> (<u>LDH</u>)

* <u>Tumor</u> <u>biopsy</u> (confirmatory)

* Treatment: varies based on <u>tumor</u> location, size, and <u>histopathology</u>, options include,

* <u>Chemotherapy</u>

* <u>Radiation therapy</u>

* Surgical resection

* Prognosis: The 5-year survival rate is ∼ 90% for <u>seminomas</u> and 45–60% for nonseminomas. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AmdRRq0)

## Staging

Staging of testicular tumors is based on the American <u>Joint</u> Committee on Cancer (AJCC) groups, which combines <u>TNM stage</u> and postorchiectomy serum <u>tumor marker</u> levels.




| <br>Simplified AJCC classification of testicular cancer [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1Yc2La0) |  |
| --- | --- |
| Stage | Description |
| Stage I | <br>* Limited to the <u>testicles</u> without <u>lymph node</u> involvement<br><br> |
| Stage II | <br>* Regional (i.e., <u>retroperitoneal</u>) <u>lymph node</u> involvement<br><br>* Stage IIa: affected <u>lymph nodes</u> ≤ 2 cm<br><br>* Stage IIb: affected <u>lymph nodes</u> > 2 cm and ≤ 5 cm<br><br>* Stage IIc: affected <u>lymph nodes</u> > 5 cm<br><br> |
| Stage III | <br><br>* Distant <u>metastasis</u> or nonregional <u>lymph node</u> involvement<br><br>* OR regional <u>lymph node</u> involvement with moderately to highly elevated postorchiectomy <u>tumor markers</u><br><br><br> |

## Approach

* Suspect testicular cancer based on history, clinical features, and <u>risk factors</u>.

* Obtain a scrotal <u>ultrasound</u> to assess testicular mass and narrow the differential diagnosis.

* If <u>malignancy</u> is suspected on <u>ultrasound</u>, obtain baseline serum <u>tumor markers</u>.

* Consider <u>sperm</u> <u>cryopreservation</u>.

* Refer to urology for <u>radical inguinal orchiectomy</u> and histopathological confirmation.

* Obtain staging diagnostics, including postorchiectomy serum <u>tumor markers</u>.

* Classify clinical stage based on AJCC criteria (see “Staging”).

* Tailor <u>postsurgical management of testicular cancer</u> to clinical stage.

* Monitor for recurrence over 5 years.

## Diagnosis

### Scrotal <u>ultrasound</u> [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/i6dJkI0)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)




* Indication: palpable <u>scrotal abnormalities</u>

* Techniques

* Conventional <u>ultrasound</u>

* <u>Duplex ultrasound</u>

* <u>Contrast-enhanced ultrasound</u>

* Findings

* <u>Hypoechoic</u> or inhomogeneous areas are suspicious for <u>malignancy</u>.

* <u>Ultrasound</u> cannot definitively differentiate between benign and malignant lesions.





### Serum <u>tumor markers</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)




* Indications

* Suspected testicular cancer

* Postorchiectomy staging

* Surveillance

* <u>Laboratory studies</u>: <u>AFP</u>, <u>hCG</u>, <u>LDH</u>

* Findings: vary by <u>tumor</u> type (See “<u>Overview of testicular tumors</u>.”)





### Staging diagnostics [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)




Staging diagnostics are obtained following <u>radical inguinal orchiectomy</u> and histopathological confirmation of testicular cancer. See “Staging” for interpretation.




* Postorchiectomy serum <u>tumor markers</u>

* CT abdomen and <u>pelvis</u> with IV contrast

* <u>CXR</u> or CT chest

* Consider <u>MRI</u> <u>brain</u> and/or <u>bone scan</u> based on concern for <u>metastases</u>.





> [!NOTE]
> The regional <u>lymph nodes</u> for the <u>testes</u> are located in the <u>retroperitoneum</u>.

## Treatment

### Radical inguinal orchiectomy [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)




* Removal of the <u>testicle</u> and <u>spermatic cord</u> (up to the <u>internal inguinal ring</u>)

* Provides therapeutic removal of local <u>tumor</u>

* Allows histopathologic diagnosis of <u>tumor</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Testis</u>-sparing <u>surgery</u> is not routinely recommended but may be considered in certain situations (e.g., equivocal <u>ultrasound</u> and negative serum <u>tumor markers</u>). [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)




> [!TIP]
> If a testicular <u>tumor</u> is suspected, the <u>testis</u> should be removed and sent to pathology. Transscrotal <u>biopsy</u> should not be conducted because of the risk of <u>tumor</u> seeding.




### Postsurgical management of testicular cancer




Postsurgical therapy is based on <u>histopathology</u>, clinical staging group (see “Staging”), and prognosis.




#### <u>Seminoma</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ApdRHI0)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1s02Fh)




* Stage I

* <u>Active surveillance</u>  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)

* Consider <u>chemotherapy</u> with <u>carboplatin</u> or <u>radiation therapy</u> in select patients (e.g., <u>rete testis</u> infiltration or <u>tumor</u> size ≥ 4 cm).

* Stage II

* <u>Radiation therapy</u>

* OR <u>chemotherapy</u>, e.g.:

* <u>Bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin</u> (<u>BEP</u>)

* <u>Etoposide</u> and <u>cisplatin</u> (EP)

* OR retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (<u>RPLND</u>)

* Stage III

* <u>Chemotherapy</u>: <u>BEP</u> or EP depending on prognosis

* Evaluation of any residual disease that may require resection





#### <u>Nonseminoma</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xpdE7I0)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ApdRHI0)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1s02Fh)




* Stage I: selected strategy should balance relapse risk (e.g., lymphovascular invasion status), treatment toxicity, patient preference, and surveillance adherence

* <u>Active surveillance</u>

* OR <u>RPLND</u>

* OR <u>chemotherapy</u> (e.g., <u>BEP</u>)

* Stage II: based on postorchiectomy <u>tumor markers</u>

* Normal: <u>RPLND</u> and/or <u>chemotherapy</u> (e.g., <u>BEP</u>, EP)

* Elevated: <u>chemotherapy</u> (e.g., <u>BEP</u>, EP)

* Stage III

* <u>Chemotherapy</u>: <u>BEP</u> or EP depending on prognosis

* Evaluation of any residual disease that may require resection

## Prognosis

* The overall prognosis of testicular tumors is excellent, with a high cure rate and 5-year survival rates of > 95%. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Emd8hq0)

* Even in advanced, <u>metastatic</u> stages, testicular tumors are often curable.
