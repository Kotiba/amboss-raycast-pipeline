# Retroperitoneal fibrosis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Surgery > Urologic surgery > Retroperitoneal fibrosis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9h0NTf)

---

## Summary

<u>Retroperitoneal</u> <u>fibrosis</u> (<u>RPF</u>, Ormond's disease) is a rare disease of unknown etiology, characterized by inflammation and <u>fibrosis</u> of the <u>retroperitoneum</u> resulting in compression and encasement of the <u>ureter</u>, and/or the <u>retroperitoneal</u> <u>blood vessels</u>. <u>RPF</u> may be primary/<u>idiopathic</u> (most common) or secondary (e.g., drug-induced, inflammatory, <u>iatrogenic</u>). Patients often present with nonspecific symptoms (e.g., <u>fever</u>, <u>malaise</u>, weight loss, flank <u>pain</u>, etc.). Bilateral <u>ureteral obstruction</u>, with subsequent <u>hydronephrosis</u> and <u>obstructive nephropathy</u>, is common. Diagnosis is often suspected in patients who present with bilateral <u>hydronephrosis</u> of unknown etiology. Contrast CT is the diagnostic test of choice and reveals a <u>retroperitoneal</u> mass encasing and obstructing the <u>ureters</u> and/or the aorta and <u>IVC</u>. Diagnosis is confirmed on CT-guided <u>biopsy</u> of the mass. High-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u> are the mainstay of treatment of primary <u>RPF</u>. Secondary <u>RPF</u> is managed by treating the underlying cause (stopping the offending drug, treating the infection, etc.). Symptomatic/severe obstruction of the <u>retroperitoneal</u> structures require treatment (ureteric <u>stenting</u>, ureterolysis, arterial <u>stenting</u>, etc.). Prognosis of non-<u>malignancy</u>-induced <u>RPF</u> is good, but recurrence rates are high (70%).

## Etiology

### Primary/<u>idiopathic</u> <u>retroperitoneal</u> <u>fibrosis</u>




* Immune reaction to <u>antigens</u> within aortic <u>atherosclerotic plaques</u>

* Systemic autoimmune disease: <u>RPF</u> may be a systemic autoimmune disease of large <u>arteries</u> → periaortic inflammation → inflammation and <u>fibrosis</u> in the periaortic region

* <u>IgG4-related disease</u> (<u>immunoglobulin</u> G4 related disease): characterized by an infiltration of various organs by IgG4-bearing <u>plasma cells</u> which cause inflammation and <u>fibrosis</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)





### Secondary <u>retroperitoneal</u> <u>fibrosis</u>




* Drugs: <u>ergot</u> alkaloids (methysergide, <u>ergotamine</u>)  ; <u>Dopamine agonists</u> (pergolide, <u>methyldopa</u>), <u>β-blockers</u>, <u>analgesics</u> (<u>phenacetin</u>), <u>hydralazine</u>, etc. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)[[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Jl0sBT)

* <u>Biological agents</u>: <u>infliximab</u>, <u>etanercept</u>, etc.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)

* Malignancies: primary <u>retroperitoneal</u> malignancies , <u>Retroperitoneal</u> <u>metastases</u> , <u>neuroendocrine tumors</u> (<u>NETs</u>)

* Infections: <u>mycobacterium tuberculosis</u>, <u>actinomycosis</u>, <u>histoplasmosis</u>

* <u>Iatrogenic</u>: <u>surgery</u> or <u>radiation therapy</u> to the <u>retroperitoneum</u>

* Trauma: <u>retroperitoneal hemorrhage</u>

* Tobacco use

* Exposure to <u>asbestos</u>





> [!TIP]
> Malignancies and exposure to methysergide are the most common causes of secondary <u>RPF</u>.

## Clinical features

* <u>Pain</u> in the lower back/flanks (most common symptom)

* <u>Constitutional symptoms</u>: <u>fever</u>, <u>anorexia</u>, weight loss, <u>nausea</u>, etc.[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)

* Specific symptoms 

* <u>Ureters</u> → <u>upper urinary tract obstruction</u> →  <u>hydronephrosis</u> and features of <u>chronic renal failure</u> (<u>obstructive nephropathy</u>: <u>uremia</u>, <u>hypertension</u>, etc.) [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ql0CBT)

* Infrarenal aorta/<u>iliac arteries</u> → <u>chronic mesenteric ischemia</u>, lower limb and gluteal <u>claudication</u> <u>pain</u>, etc.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Il0YyT)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Gl0ByT)

* <u>Inferior vena cava</u>/iliac <u>veins</u> → <u>deep vein thrombosis</u>, <u>renal vein thrombosis</u>

* Gonadal vessels → hydrocoele, varicocoele, testicular <u>pain</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7l04yT)

* Lymphatic channels → <u>lymphedema</u>





> [!TIP]
> In <u>RPF</u>, the <u>ureters</u> are the earliest and most commonly involved structures, and <u>ureteral obstruction</u> is often bilateral. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ql0CBT)

## Diagnosis

* Laboratory tests [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)

* Raised <u>inflammatory markers</u>: elevated <u>CRP</u> and <u>ESR</u>

* Renal parameters: <u>blood urea nitrogen</u>, serum <u>creatinine</u>, and serum <u>electrolyte</u> levels

* <u>Autoantibodies</u>: e.g., <u>ANA</u> (<u>antinuclear antibody</u>), <u>ANCA</u> (antineutrophilic <u>cytoplasmic</u> <u>antibody</u>)  [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/sl0tyT)

* Imaging

* Contrast-enhanced <u>CT scan</u>

* Investigation of choice to diagnose <u>RPF</u>

* Findings: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ql0CBT)

* Para-aortic mass extending from the <u>renal arteries</u> to the common <u>iliac arteries</u>.

* Encasement and/or compression of the <u>ureters</u>, <u>IVC</u>, and/or aorta  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Il0YyT)

* May identify malignancies

* CT-guided <u>biopsy</u> of the mass

* <u>MRI</u> and <u>MRA</u>:  useful in patients in whom contrast administration is contraindicated

* <u>Intravenous urography</u> and <u>retrograde pyelography</u>  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ql0CBT)

* Renal <u>ultrasonography</u>: useful in assessing response to therapy

* <u>Biopsy</u>: (<u>confirmatory test</u>)

* CT-guided/<u>laparoscopic</u> <u>biopsy</u> of the <u>retroperitoneal</u> mass

* Early stage: hypervascular tissue with perivascular <u>lymphocytic</u> infiltrate and lipid-laden <u>macrophages</u>

* Late stage: avascular fibrous tissue which is devoid of cells

## Treatment

### Medical therapy [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/pl0LBT)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/7l04yT)




* Primary <u>RPF</u>

* Oral high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u>: first line therapy with high dose <u>prednisone</u> (1 mg/kg/day for the first month)

* <u>Tamoxifen</u>: indicated as monotherapy in patients with contraindications to <u>glucocorticoid</u> therapy

* <u>Immunosuppressants</u> : indicated in <u>glucocorticoid</u>-resistant disease

* Secondary <u>RPF</u>

* Treatment of the underlying etiology: e.g., discontinue the causative drug, treat chronic infections, treatment of <u>lymphoma</u>, etc.

* Oral high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u>: Indicated in symptomatic/severe drug-induced cases of secondary <u>RPF</u>





### Decompression of obstructed <u>retroperitoneal</u> structures [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Hl0KyT)




* <u>Kidneys</u> and <u>ureters</u>:  (see “Treatment” of “<u>Upper urinary tract obstruction</u>”)

* <u>Conservative therapy</u>

* Patients with mild <u>hydronephrosis</u> and normal renal parameters: respond well to medical therapy alone

* Mild <u>hydronephrosis</u> with abnormal renal parameters: <u>cystoscopy</u>-guided ureteric <u>stenting</u>

* <u>Obstructive nephropathy</u>: urgent decompression with <u>percutaneous nephrostomy</u>, followed by <u>surgery</u>

* Surgical decompression: open/<u>laparoscopic</u> ureterolysis (release of the <u>ureter</u> from <u>fibrotic</u> tissue)

* Aorta or <u>iliac arteries</u>: see “<u>Revascularization</u>” in “<u>Peripheral arterial disease</u>.”

* <u>IVC</u> or iliac <u>veins</u>: see “Treatment” in “<u>Deep vein thrombosis</u>.”
