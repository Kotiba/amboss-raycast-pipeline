# Carcinoma of the penis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Surgery > Urologic surgery > Carcinoma of the penis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ri0fGf)

---

## Summary

Primary <u>carcinoma</u> of the <u>penis</u> is a rare <u>tumor</u> disease that mainly occurs in older men. It is associated with several <u>risk factors</u> including (untreated) <u>phimosis</u>, infection with <u>human papillomavirus</u>, and smoking. Patients present with a lump or an ulcerative lesion of the <u>penis</u>. Palpation of the groin and <u>physical examination</u> may reveal enlarged <u>inguinal lymph nodes</u>. <u>Excisional biopsy</u> is performed for histopathological confirmation of the <u>tumor</u> while <u>MRI</u> of the <u>penis</u> and CT of the <u>pelvis</u> are part of the <u>tumor staging</u>. The primary treatment is usually surgical and depends on the extent of the local and regional disease; options include local excision and partial or radical penectomy. Regional <u>lymph node dissection</u> may be necessary if the inguinopelvic nodes are involved. <u>Metastatic</u> disease is rare at inital presentation, but <u>chemotherapy</u> may be a palliative option. The overall prognosis is determined by the presence and extent of regional <u>lymph node</u> involvement.

## Epidemiology

* Rare type of <u>malignancy</u> in industrialized countries

* More common in low-income regions with poor hygienic conditions, esp. in Africa, South America, and Asia

* Peak <u>incidence</u>: 60 years of age [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nYa7pQ)




Epidemiological data refers to the US, unless otherwise specified.

---

## Etiology

<u>Chronic inflammation</u> of the <u>penis</u>




* <u>Risk factors</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/is0Juh) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nYa7pQ)

* <u>Phimosis</u>, particularly if left untreated

* Tobacco use

* Infections

* <u>Human papillomavirus</u>;  (<u>HPV</u>) infection, especially type <u>HPV</u>-16 and <u>HPV</u>-18

* <u>HIV</u>

* <u>Precancerous skin lesions</u> (see “Differential diagnoses” below)

* Protective factor: <u>circumcision</u> before adulthood

---

## Clinical features

* Palpable painless lump or ulcerative lesion of the <u>penis</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ss0y8h)

* The glans and the <u>foreskin</u> are the most common locations.

* A concurrent <u>phimosis</u> may conceal the lesion

* Surface of the lesion may be <u>exophytic</u>, flat, or ulcerated

* Chronic penile <u>rash</u> or subtle burning sensation

* Voiding and sexual function typically remain unimpaired

* Swollen <u>inguinal lymph nodes</u>  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/is0Juh)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Rs0luh)

---

## Diagnosis

* <u>Excisional biopsy</u> (<u>gold standard</u>) 
* ∼ 95% of all penile cancers are <u>squamous cell carcinoma</u> (<u>SCC</u>): atypical squamous cells with varying degrees of <u>mitotic</u> activity [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/hs0cuh)

* <u>Tumor staging</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ss0y8h)

* <u>Fine needle aspiration</u>, <u>biopsy</u>  or dissection of <u>inguinal lymph nodes</u>

* Imaging studies

* <u>Ultrasonography</u> and/or <u>MRI</u> of the <u>penis</u> and <u>pelvis</u>

* <u>Pelvic</u> CT  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/is0Juh)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Rs0luh)

## Differential diagnoses

* <u>Benign tumors</u> or lesions [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Ss0y8h)

* <u>Condylomata acuminata</u>

* <u>Buschke-Löwenstein</u> <u>tumor</u>

* <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u>

* <u>Precancerous skin lesions</u> or <u>carcinoma in situ</u> lesions

* <u>Bowen disease</u> :

* <u>Erythroplasia of Queyrat</u>

* <u>Bowenoid papulosis</u>

* <u>Leukoplakia</u>

* <u>Metastases</u>  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Rs0luh)




The differential diagnoses listed here are not exhaustive.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the stage of the disease and the risk of recurrence, but the initial approach is typically surgical.  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Rs0luh)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Qs0uuh)




* Small (< 3.0 cm) primary tumors 

* Limited local excision with or without <u>circumcision</u>

* <u>Laser ablation</u>

* <u>Radiation therapy</u>

* Invasive or bulky primary tumors

* Partial penectomy: if penile length is adequate (e.g., for voiding, <u>sexual activity</u>) after excision with a 2 cm <u>surgical margin</u>

* Total penectomy and perineal urethrostomy

* Regional <u>ipsilateral</u> <u>lymph node dissection</u> in patients with positive <u>lymph nodes</u> often followed by <u>adjuvant chemotherapy</u>

* Recurrent or <u>metastatic</u> disease: penectomy or <u>palliative chemotherapy</u>
