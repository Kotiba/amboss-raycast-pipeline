## Summary

Dermatology is the branch of medicine concerned with the <u>skin</u>, <u>hair</u>, and <u>nails</u> as well as the conditions associated with them. Basic knowledge of dermatology is essential for every physician, as approximately 50% of <u>skin</u>-related consultations are initially assessed by non-dermatologists. In the United States, the most common conditions seen by dermatologists include <u>acne</u>, <u>actinic keratoses</u>, <u>non-melanoma skin cancers</u>, <u>benign tumors</u>, and <u>contact dermatitis</u>. <u>Skin</u> lesions may be primary or secondary. Primary lesions (e.g., <u>macules</u> and <u>papules</u>) appear as a direct result of a disease process. Secondary lesions (e.g., scales and <u>ulcers</u>) may develop from primary lesions or result from external trauma (e.g., infections, scratching). Dermatological conditions can often be diagnosed based on <u>patient history</u> and <u>physical examination</u> but confirming the diagnosis may require laboratory testing or <u>biopsy</u>. <u>Dermatological diseases</u> are managed with medication (topical and systemic) and procedures such as <u>surgery</u>, <u>cryoablation</u>, <u>radiotherapy</u>, or <u>phototherapy</u>. <u>Conservative treatment</u> with topical agents is the treatment of choice for most dermatological conditions, with systemic medication and/or <u>surgery</u> being employed only if necessary. The <u>prevalence</u>, manifestations, and treatment of <u>dermatological conditions in skin of color</u> may vary depending on <u>skin</u> tone and genetic inheritance.




See also “<u>Rash</u>” for a clinical approach to various rashes (e.g., <u>maculopapular rash</u>, <u>vesiculobullous rash</u>, <u>petechial</u> <u>rash</u>, <u>diffuse erythematous rash</u>) and related <u>skin</u> lesions (e.g., <u>annular skin lesions</u>).

## Physical examination

### Overview




* Complete <u>skin</u> assessment: Examine the <u>skin</u> (including the hands, mouth, and scalp) and <u>nails</u> for signs of dermatological conditions.

* Examination techniques

* Inspection

* Palpation: evaluation of consistency (e.g., softness, firmness)

* Typical <u>skin</u> tests, as indicated:

* <u>Nikolsky sign</u>

* <u>Auspitz sign</u>

* <u>Dermatoscope</u> inspection, as indicated

* Confirmation of diagnosis: may require <u>biopsy</u> for histopathological examination (see “Dermapathology” below) and/or <u>laboratory studies</u>





> [!TIP]
> The hands, mouth, scalp, and <u>nails</u> should not be overlooked during a dermatological examination.




### <u>Skin examination</u>




* Determine the type of lesion: See <u>primary skin lesions</u>, <u>secondary skin lesions</u>, and <u>complex skin lesions</u> below.

* Record lesion characteristics

* Location

* Number (single/multiple)

* Size

* Color: e.g., pink discoloration

* Texture: e.g., <u>atrophic</u>, calloused, crusty, <u>verrucous</u>

* Shape: e.g., round, oval, annular

* Distribution

* Symmetric/asymmetric

* Unilateral/bilateral

* Diffuse/grouped

* Secondary changes (e.g., as a result of scratching)

### Provocation tests



* Nikolsky sign

* Slight mechanical pressure (by rubbing) causes the upper <u>epidermal</u> layer to slip away from the lower layer due to impaired adhesion between <u>epidermal</u> cells, leading to separation and <u>blistering</u>.

* The test is positive on intact or only mildly <u>erythematous</u> <u>skin</u>.

* May be present in <u>pemphigus vulgaris</u>, <u>toxic epidermal necrolysis</u>, <u>staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome</u>, and <u>Stevens-Johnson syndrome</u>

* Not present in <u>bullous pemphigoid</u>

### Tzanck test




* Microscopic examination of scrapings from the base of a lesion to look for Tzanck cells

* Tzanck cells (<u>multinucleated giant cells</u>) are present in:

* <u>Pemphigus vulgaris</u>

* <u>Herpes simplex</u> type 1 (<u>HSV-1</u>) infection

* <u>Varicella zoster virus</u> infection (<u>chickenpox</u> or <u>shingles</u>)

* <u>Cytomegalovirus</u>

* No detection of Tzanck cells in:

* <u>Bullous pemphigoid</u>

* <u>Dermatitis herpetiformis</u>

## Overview of treatment

### Treatment




* Topical medications

* First choice of treatment for most conditions; often preferred for treating dermatological conditions because they cause fewer systemic side effects

* Examples: <u>antibiotics</u>, <u>antifungals</u>, <u>corticosteroids</u>, cytotoxic agents, antipruritics, <u>retinoids</u>

* Systemic medications

* Procedures

* <u>Surgery</u>

* Cryoablation: application of cold (e.g., using liquid nitrogen) to destroy abnormal tissue, malignant or premalignant <u>skin</u> lesions.

* Other

* <u>Radiotherapy</u>

* <u>Phototherapy</u>

* Creams

* Ointments

* Lotions, foams, and gels

### Topical corticosteroids (TCS)




* Dermatoses responsive to <u>TCS</u> include: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LnWwFN0)

* <u>Eczematous</u> conditions

* <u>Atopic dermatitis</u> (AD)

* <u>Dyshidrotic eczema</u>

* <u>Nummular dermatitis</u>

* <u>Seborrheic dermatitis</u>

* <u>Contact dermatitis</u>

* Autoimmune conditions

* <u>Psoriasis</u>

* <u>Alopecia areata</u>

* <u>Vitiligo</u>

* <u>Discoid lupus erythematosus</u>

* <u>Lichen planus</u>

* <u>Lichen sclerosus</u>

* Other inflammatory conditions

* <u>Labial adhesions</u>

* <u>Phimosis</u>

* Other

* <u>Insect bites</u>

* Drug eruptions

* General principles of TCS

* Choose a <u>TCS</u> based on the patient's disease, severity, affected body areas, and lesion thickness.

* Reassess response frequently.

* Use the lowest effective <u>potency</u> for the shortest amount of time.

* Avoid prolonged use (e.g., > 3 weeks for class 1 <u>TCS</u>) to decrease adverse effects. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LnWwFN0)

* The following populations are at increased risk of adverse effects from <u>TCS</u>: [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LnWwFN0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/WqdPxI0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Cndqvq0)

* Individuals with occlusive coverings (e.g., wet wrap dressings, diapers)

* Young children

* Adverse effects of TCS

* <u>Skin</u> <u>atrophy</u>

* <u>Telangiectasias</u>

* <u>Striae</u> [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/oJd0EI0)

* <u>Steroid acne</u>

* See also “<u>Adverse effects of corticosteroid therapy</u>.”

### Phototherapy [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/YMcnM10)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8S1OYg0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/XO19IS0)




<u>Ultraviolet light</u> is an effective treatment option for dermatological conditions because of its antiproliferative (e.g., slowing of <u>keratinization</u>) and antiinflammatory effects (e.g., inducing <u>apoptosis</u> of pathogenic <u>T cells</u>).




* UVA therapy 

* Indications: <u>atopic dermatitis</u>, <u>scleroderma</u>, chronic <u>pruritus</u>, prurigo simplex [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cO1arS0)

* Adverse effects

* <u>Skin</u> <u>aging</u>

* ↑ Cancer risk

* UVB therapy 

* Indications: <u>psoriasis</u>, <u>vitiligo</u>, chronic <u>pruritus</u>, <u>atopic dermatitis</u> [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cO1arS0)

* Adverse effects

* <u>Acceleration</u> of photodamage (e.g., <u>sunburn</u>)

* ↑ Cancer risk

* PUVA therapy:  (psoralen plus UVA): topical or oral psoralen → ↑ <u>skin</u> <u>photosensitivity</u> → ↑ antiproliferative and antiinflammatory effects of UVA light

* Indications: <u>psoriasis</u>, <u>atopic dermatitis</u>, <u>vitiligo</u>, <u>mycosis fungoides</u>

* Adverse effects

* <u>Nausea</u>, <u>vomiting</u>

* ↑ Risk of <u>squamous cell carcinoma</u>
