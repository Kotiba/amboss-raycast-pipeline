# Skin and soft tissue infections

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Dermatology > Infectious diseases > Bacterial infections > Skin and soft tissue infections*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qh0CUf)

---

## Summary

<u>Skin</u> and <u>soft tissue</u> infections (SSTIs) are a group of heterogeneous conditions affecting the <u>epidermis</u>, <u>dermis</u>, <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>, or <u>superficial fascia</u>. Uncomplicated infections are most commonly caused by gram-positive pathogens (<u>Streptococcus</u>, <u>Staphylococcus</u>) that infiltrate the <u>skin</u> after minor injuries (e.g., scratches, <u>insect bites</u>). Complicated infections have a higher tendency to be polymicrobial. SSTIs primarily manifest with painful, warm, <u>erythematous</u> <u>skin</u> lesions and may also lead to <u>purulent</u> fluid collections and/or <u>necrosis</u> of the affected tissue. Systemic symptoms like <u>fever</u> are usually a sign of more severe infections. <u>Risk factors</u> for developing SSTIs (or more severe forms of SSTIs) include <u>diabetes mellitus</u>, <u>immunodeficiency</u>, and chronic <u>edema</u>. Diagnosis is mostly clinical, but some patients may require imaging or <u>laboratory studies</u>. <u>Purulent</u> infections, such as <u>abscesses</u>, are primarily treated with <u>incision and drainage</u>, while nonpurulent infections (e.g., <u>erysipelas</u>, <u>cellulitis</u>) require <u>antibiotic therapy</u>. <u>Necrotizing soft tissue infections</u> (<u>NSTIs</u>) have a high <u>mortality rate</u>; they are a surgical emergency and require immediate <u>wound debridement</u>.

> Tissue involvement of SSTI (from <u>superficial</u> to deep): <u>impetigo</u> (<u>superficial</u> <u>epidermis</u>), <u>erysipelas</u> (<u>superficial</u> <u>dermis</u> and lymphatic vessels), <u>cellulitis</u> (deep <u>dermis</u> and <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>), <u>necrotizing fasciitis</u> (<u>subcutaneous tissue</u> including <u>superficial</u> and deep <u>fascia</u>)




### Cardinal signs of inflammation




* Rubor = redness

* <u>Calor</u> = heat

* <u>Tumor</u> = swelling/<u>edema</u>

* Dolor = <u>pain</u>

* <u>Functio laesa</u> = loss of function





### Risk factors for skin and soft tissue infections [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)




* Local factors

* Chronic <u>lymphedema</u>

* Local <u>skin</u> defects (e.g., <u>tinea pedis</u>)

* Circulatory disorders: arteriovenous insufficiency, chronic <u>edema</u>, stasis

* <u>Peripheral neuropathy</u>, <u>paresis</u>

* Systemic factors

* <u>Diabetes mellitus</u>

* <u>Immunodeficiency</u> (e.g., <u>HIV infection</u>, <u>asplenia</u>, <u>chemotherapy</u>)

* <u>Chronic kidney disease</u> (leading to, e.g., chronic <u>edema</u>, dialysis)

* <u>Obesity</u>, poor <u>nutritional status</u>

* Drug or <u>alcohol use disorder</u>

* Older and younger age

* Increased exposure to pathogens

* <u>Nosocomial</u> pathogens (e.g., prolonged hospitalization, <u>surgery</u>)

* Water exposure (e.g., sea water, hot tubs)

* IV drug use, long-term <u>intravascular</u> devices  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9HbNsE)

* Trauma (e.g., <u>open wounds</u>, exposed <u>fractures</u>)





### Complications




* Local spread of infection

* Systemic involvement with <u>fever</u> and possible <u>sepsis</u> (see “<u>Sepsis</u>” for details on the management of severe infections)

* Spread of infection to distant sites (see “<u>Staphylococcal infections</u>”)

## Nonpurulent SSTIs

### Definitions [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Erysipelas: <u>superficial</u> <u>skin</u> infection involving the upper <u>dermis</u>

* Cellulitis: local infection of the deep <u>dermis</u> and <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>





### Clinical features [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Local signs: <u>erythema</u>, <u>edema</u>, warmth, tenderness

* Specific to <u>erysipelas</u>: raised, sharply demarcated lesion

* Specific to <u>cellulitis</u>: poorly defined lesion with induration

* Cutaneous lymphatic <u>edema</u> (historically referred to as “<u>peau d'orange</u>”)

* Common locations: lower limbs, face

* Possible additional features

* Lymphangitis: red streaks radiating from the <u>skin</u> lesion and following the direction of the lymphatic vessels

* <u>Lymphadenitis</u>: swollen, tender, regional <u>lymph nodes</u>

* <u>Bullae</u>

* <u>Purulent</u> <u>exudate</u>

* Systemic symptoms (in moderate/severe infections): <u>fever</u>, chills, confusion, <u>nausea</u>, <u>headache</u>, muscle and <u>joint</u> <u>pain</u>





> [!TIP]
> Bilateral <u>cellulitis</u> is exceedingly rare. Patients presenting with bilateral leg <u>erythema</u> should also be evaluated for alternative diagnoses, including <u>stasis dermatitis</u> and <u>lymphedema</u>. [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/y8cd6V0)




### Pathophysiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Entry is commonly via a minor <u>skin</u> injury ; <u>erysipelas</u> can consequently spread via <u>superficial</u> lymphatic vessels.

* May also be secondary to a systemic infection





> [!TIP]
> In both <u>erysipelas</u> and <u>cellulitis</u>, the most common point of entry for the <u>pathogen</u> is a small <u>skin</u> lesion (e.g., interdigital <u>tinea pedis</u>).




### Etiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* <u>Beta-hemolytic</u> <u>streptococci</u>: mostly <u>group A Streptococcus</u> (<u>S. pyogenes</u>)

* Less common pathogens for <u>cellulitis</u>

* <u>S. aureus</u>

* <u>Pasteurella multocida</u> (gram-negative, encapsulated coccobacillus): secondary to dog and <u>cat bites</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>GAS</u> is the most common cause of <u>nonpurulent skin and soft tissue infections</u> (i.e., <u>erysipelas</u>, <u>cellulitis</u>).




### Diagnostics [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Clbq_F)




Diagnosis is usually clinical. In patients with systemic symptoms, <u>laboratory studies</u>, cultures, and imaging may be indicated to assess severity and tailor treatment.




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: possible <u>leukocytosis</u>

* <u>BMP</u>: signs of underlying conditions that are <u>risk factors</u> for infections (e.g., <u>diabetes mellitus</u>, <u>chronic kidney disease</u>)

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u>;  (e.g., <u>CRP</u>, <u>procalcitonin</u>): may be elevated

* Imaging

* <u>Soft tissue</u> <u>ultrasound</u>

* Indication: consider if there is suspicion for <u>skin abscess</u> and/or foreign body (see “<u>Purulent SSTI</u>” section)

* Findings: <u>hypoechoic</u> strands (signs of <u>edema</u>) between <u>subcutaneous fat</u> tissue  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* CT/<u>MRI</u> with or without IV contrast: consider if there is concern for complications (e.g., <u>pyomyositis</u>, <u>osteomyelitis</u>)  [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Clbq_F)

* Microbiology: <u>Gram stain</u> and cultures are not routinely recommended. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)
* Consider <u>skin biopsy</u>, <u>aspirate</u>, and/or <u>blood cultures</u> in patients with the following: [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)

* <u>Malignancy</u> or patients receiving <u>chemotherapy</u>

* <u>Neutropenia</u>

* Severe <u>immunodeficiency</u>

* <u>Immersion injuries</u>

* <u>Animal bites</u>





### Treatment of nonpurulent SSTIs [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_mb5R8)




#### General principles




* <u>Empiric antibiotic therapy</u> active against <u>streptococci</u> and <u>S. aureus</u> is the mainstay of treatment for <u>nonpurulent SSTIs</u>.

* Treatment should also include the control of any predisposing factors (e.g., <u>edema</u>, <u>fungal infections</u>).

* The majority of patients with <u>cellulitis</u> or <u>erysipelas</u> are clinically stable and can be managed as outpatients.

* Patients with systemic symptoms often require hospitalization and parenteral <u>antibiotic therapy</u>.

* See “<u>Sepsis</u>” for further details on the management of severe infections.





#### <u>Antibiotic therapy</u>




* Systemic <u>antibiotic therapy</u> is usually required.

* Empiric regimens should usually cover <u>Streptococcus</u> spp. and <u>S. aureus</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for nonpurulent SSTIs</u>” for detailed recommendations).

* Consider inpatient management with parenteral <u>antibiotics</u> for patients with any of the following: 

* Systemic symptoms

* Suspicion for deeper infection

* <u>Immunocompromise</u>

* Failure of outpatient treatment. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)

* Consider coverage for <u>MRSA</u> in the following cases:

* <u>Penetrating trauma</u>

* Evidence of <u>MRSA</u> infection or <u>colonization</u> elsewhere

* Injection drug use

* <u>Purulent</u> drainage

* <u>SIRS</u>





> [!TIP]
> <u>Antibiotics</u> should be targeted against gram-positive pathogens and provide broad-spectrum coverage in severe cases. [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




#### <u>Supportive care</u>




* Elevation of the affected limbs

* Rest and <u>acute pain management</u> as needed





#### Acute management checklist for nonpurulent SSTI




* Stabilize the patient as needed.

* Consider indications for <u>laboratory studies</u> and imaging.

* Start <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> according to recommendations for severity and <u>risk factors</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for nonpurulent SSTIs</u>”).

* Provide <u>supportive care</u> (limb elevation, <u>pain management</u>).

* Identify and treat the underlying cause, if present (e.g., <u>tinea pedis</u>, poorly controlled <u>diabetes</u>).

* Consider indications for admission/inpatient management.





### Subtypes and variants




#### Perianal streptococcal dermatitis [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fIdkXr0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Dd5SH0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Bedza60)




* Definition: : an infection of the perianal <u>skin</u> with group A <u>Streptococcus</u> characterized by <u>pain</u>, <u>pruritus</u>, and <u>rash</u>

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: Most common in children between 6 months and 10 years of age

* Etiology: group A <u>Streptococcus</u> infection

* Clinical features

* <u>Signs of systemic infection</u> are rare

* <u>Pruritus</u>, rectal <u>pain</u>, blood-streaked stools

* Sharply demarcated, <u>erythematous</u> perianal <u>rash</u> with pseudoexudate, crusting, and/or <u>superficial</u> <u>anal fissures</u>/<u>cracks</u>

* Diagnosis: Rapid strep and culture of the perianal region

* Differential diagnosis

* See “<u>Overview of diaper dermatitis</u>.”

* See “<u>Differential diagnoses of diaper dermatitis</u>.”

* Treatment [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Dd5SH0)[[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Bedza60) [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LIdwWr0)

* <u>Amoxicillin</u> DOSAGE for 10 days; preferred in children [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_Dd5SH0)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/LIdwWr0)

* Alternatives: <u>penicillin V</u> and <u>clindamycin</u>; see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for nonpurulent SSTIs</u>” for dosage information





> [!TIP]
> If the <u>rapid strep test</u> of the perianal region is negative, consider treating for perianal <u>staphylococcal infection</u>, as the manifestation is identical. [[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fIdkXr0)




#### Other




* <u>Orbital cellulitis</u>; periorbital (preseptal) <u>cellulitis</u>

* <u>Ludwig angina</u>





### Complications  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Recurrent infections

* <u>Abscess</u>

* Infection of deeper tissues: <u>cellulitis</u>, <u>necrotizing fasciitis</u>, <u>osteomyelitis</u>

* <u>Thrombophlebitis</u>, <u>lymphedema</u>

* Systemic complications (e.g., <u>sepsis</u>, <u>endocarditis</u>, <u>streptococcal toxic shock syndrome</u>, <u>poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis</u>, acute <u>rheumatic fever</u>)

* In <u>orbital cellulitis</u>: blindness, <u>cavernous sinus thrombosis</u>, intracranial <u>abscess</u>

## Purulent SSTIs

### <u>Folliculitis</u>, <u>furuncles</u>, and <u>carbuncles</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)




* Definitions

* Folliculitis: localized inflammation of the <u>hair follicle</u> (or <u>sebaceous glands</u>) that is limited to the <u>epidermis</u>

* Hot tub folliculitis: <u>pseudomonal folliculitis</u> that appears 8–48 hours after exposure to contaminated water; usually a <u>self-limiting</u> condition that does not require <u>antibiotic</u> treatment

* Furuncle: deep <u>folliculitis</u> beyond the <u>dermis</u> with <u>abscess</u> formation in the <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>

* Carbuncle: confluent <u>folliculitis</u> that forms an inflammatory mass; <u>abscess</u> and <u>skin</u> <u>necrosis</u> may be present

* Clinical features

* Tender <u>papules</u> and/or <u>pustules</u>, often pruritic

* Located at the site of <u>hair follicles</u>

* Possible <u>pus</u> drainage from follicular orifices

* Potentially multiple lesions

* Variable locations (e.g., <u>carbuncles</u> most often develop on the back of the neck)

* Differential diagnoses

* <u>Ecthyma</u>

* <u>Acne vulgaris</u>

* <u>Eosinophilic folliculitis</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Facial <u>furuncles</u> can result in severe complications (e.g., <u>periorbital cellulitis</u>, <u>cavernous sinus thrombosis</u>).

### Skin abscess [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Definition: an accumulation of white-yellow pus that contains <u>proteins</u>, <u>leukocytes</u> (especially <u>neutrophils</u>), bacteria, and cellular debris and is located in the <u>dermis</u> and <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>

* Clinical features 

* <u>Cardinal signs of inflammation</u>

* Painful, tender, and <u>fluctuant</u> red <u>nodules</u>

* <u>Pain</u> is usually relieved after rupture or drainage.

* May be surrounded by <u>erythema</u> and induration.

* Differential diagnosis: inflamed <u>epidermoid cyst</u>, <u>carbuncle</u>

* Subtypes and variants

* Recurrent skin abscess: a new <u>abscess</u> at the site of a previous infection

* Complex skin abscess: a <u>skin abscess</u> that requires specialized treatment because of its location (e.g., perianal, perirectal), the pathogens involved (e.g., polymicrobial, resistant pathogens), or patient characteristics (e.g., <u>immunodeficiency</u>)  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)

* Scrotal abscess: a subcutaneous collection of <u>pus</u> in the scrotal region, usually a result of inflamed <u>hair follicles</u> 

* Differential diagnoses: <u>neoplasia</u> of the <u>testis</u>, infected <u>hydrocele</u>, <u>genital warts</u>

* May progress to <u>fournier gangrene</u>.





> [!TIP]
> In both <u>scrotal abscess</u> and <u>epididymitis</u>, the <u>classic signs of inflammation</u> are prominent and help to confirm the diagnosis.

### Etiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)




* <u>S. aureus</u>: most common <u>pathogen</u> for any form of <u>purulent SSTI</u>; <u>MRSA</u> is frequently identified.  [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KrbURE)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h7bclE)

* <u>Streptococcus</u> spp. [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/8qbOZE)

* <u>P. aeruginosa</u> (e.g., <u>hot tub folliculitis</u>)

* Polymicrobial (e.g., <u>anaerobes</u> or gram-negative bacteria, especially in complex <u>abscesses</u>)

* Noninfectious causes (e.g., ingrown <u>hairs</u>, friction, trauma, or occlusion)

* Fungi (rare): <u>Candida albicans</u> or Malassezia (particularly <u>M. furfur</u>)





### Diagnostics [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)




Diagnosis is usually clinical. In patients with systemic symptoms, <u>laboratory studies</u>, cultures, and imaging may be indicated to assess severity and tailor treatment.




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: possible <u>leukocytosis</u>

* <u>BMP</u>: signs of underlying conditions that are <u>risk factors</u> for infections (e.g., <u>diabetes mellitus</u>, <u>chronic kidney disease</u>)

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u>;  (e.g., <u>CRP</u>, <u>procalcitonin</u>) may be elevated

* Imaging [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Clbq_F)

* <u>Soft tissue</u> <u>ultrasound</u>

* Indications include:

* Systemic signs of infection (i.e., moderate or severe disease)

* Confirmation of diagnosis prior to <u>incision and drainage</u> of an <u>abscess</u>

* Assessment of size, extent, and depth of the <u>abscess</u>  [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ZIbZYE)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/aIbQYE)

* Suspected foreign object at the site of infection (e.g., needle tip, splinter)

* Suspected <u>complex skin abscess</u>

* Possible findings [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* <u>Skin abscess</u>: <u>hypoechoic</u>, heterogeneous fluid collection with <u>posterior enhancement</u>

* Surrounding <u>edema</u> (especially in accompanying <u>cellulitis</u>)

* CT/<u>MRI</u> with or without IV contrast: usually only necessary for complex <u>skin abscesses</u> (e.g., <u>perianal abscess</u>) and the evaluation of complications (e.g., <u>osteomyelitis</u>)

* Microbiology

* <u>Gram stain</u> and cultures are not routinely recommended, even if <u>CA-MRSA</u> is suspected.  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)[[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/KrbURE)

* Indications for <u>abscess</u> and <u>blood cultures</u> include:

* Systemic symptoms, severe infections (see “Severity of SSTI”)

* <u>Immunocompromised</u> patients

* <u>Recurrent skin abscess</u>

* <u>Carbuncles</u>

* Additional evaluation: Consider additional evaluation for underlying conditions in patients with recurrent <u>abscess</u> and/or <u>complex skin abscess</u>. 

* <u>Colonization</u> with resistant pathogens (e.g., <u>MRSA</u>)

* Local causes (e.g., foreign body, <u>pilonidal cyst</u>, <u>hidradenitis suppurativa</u>)

* Systemic predisposing conditions (e.g., <u>neutrophil</u> disorders)

Breast abscess




### Treatment of purulent SSTIs




#### General principles




* <u>Incision and drainage</u> are the mainstay of treatment for <u>purulent SSTIs</u> and are usually sufficient for mild infections.

* Patients with systemic signs of infections require <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u>.

* Outpatient management is appropriate for clinically stable patients.

* Consider inpatient management for patients with systemic symptoms.

* See “<u>Sepsis</u>” for more details on the management of severe infections.





#### Interventional therapy [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* <u>Incision and drainage</u> (I&D)

* Bedside procedure

* Indications: small <u>skin abscesses</u>  <u>carbuncles</u>, large <u>furuncles</u> , inflamed <u>epidermoid cysts</u>

* See “<u>Abscess incision and drainage</u>” for detailed procedural guidance.

* Surgical drainage

* Usually performed in the OR

* Indications can include:

* Recurrent <u>skin abscesses</u>

* Multiple <u>abscesses</u> (may be associated with <u>fistulas</u>)

* Size > 5 cm

* High-risk anatomical location

* Suspected mycotic or <u>mycobacterial</u> <u>abscess</u>

* Comorbidities that increase the risk of complications (e.g., <u>coagulopathy</u>)





![Abscess incision and drainage](../../../../../media/tkkthtssipmc8gp2n18rksxs.jpg)

Abscess incision and drainage

![Guiding incision and drainage with ultrasound](../../../../../media/small_61081364a5cb4.jpg)

Guiding incision and drainage with ultrasound




#### <u>Antibiotic therapy</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Topical <u>antibiotics</u>: Consider in <u>MRSA</u> <u>folliculitis</u> (e.g., <u>mupirocin</u>  DOSAGE). [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kObmsF)

* Systemic antibiotic therapy for purulent SSTIs: Usually not indicated after successful drainage in otherwise healthy individuals.   [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Q7bulE)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cwca3e0)

* Indications: moderate/severe infections, recurrent or complex <u>abscesses</u>, according to <u>risk factors</u> and comorbidities (e.g., <u>diabetes mellitus</u>)

* Regimens often include coverage for <u>MRSA</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for purulent SSTIs</u>” for detailed recommendations).





> [!TIP]
> Mild <u>purulent</u> <u>skin</u> infections usually do not require systemic <u>antibiotic</u> treatment following drainage.




#### Supportive measures




* Warm compresses (in <u>folliculitis</u>, <u>furuncles</u>, and <u>carbuncles</u>)

* Rest and <u>acute pain management</u> as needed

* Keep the affected area clean and dry.  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)

* Consider <u>MRSA</u> decolonization for recurrent <u>abscesses</u>. [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rD0ffR)





#### Acute management checklist for moderate and severe purulent infections




* Stabilize the patient as needed.

* Complete labs and imaging (e.g., <u>soft tissue</u> <u>ultrasound</u> or CT) based on initial results, extension, and location.

* Consider <u>surgery</u> consult if concern for large <u>abscess</u> or severe infection.

* <u>Incision and drainage</u> if indicated; Consider sending fluid samples for Gram stains and cultures.

* Start <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> according to recommendations for severity and <u>risk factors</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for purulent SSTIs</u>”).

* Provide symptomatic treatment (e.g., <u>pain management</u>, warm compresses).

* Consider indications for admission/inpatient management.

## Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs)

### Definitions [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Necrotizing soft tissue infection (<u>NSTI</u>): an aggressive, life-threatening infection involving <u>necrosis</u> of the tissue. <u>Superficial</u> and/or deep tissue may be affected (i.e., necrotizing <u>cellulitis</u>, <u>necrotizing fasciitis</u>, necrotizing <u>myositis</u>).

* Necrotizing fasciitis (most common <u>NSTI</u>): a rapidly progressive infection resulting in extensive <u>necrosis</u> of <u>superficial</u> and deep <u>fascia</u> and overlying <u>subcutaneous fat</u> that can develop into a life-threatening condition within hours

* Fournier gangrene: <u>Necrotizing fasciitis</u> of the external genitalia that can spread rapidly to the <u>anterior abdominal wall</u> and <u>gluteal muscles</u>.

* <u>Clostridial myonecrosis</u>: a rapidly spreading necrotizing infection caused by <u>Clostridium perfringens</u> or <u>Clostridium septicum</u> (see “<u>Gas gangrene</u>” for details)





![Fournier gangrene](../../../../../media/b5ktj59zsx2o6f9ncqjobl51.jpg)

Fournier gangrene




### Etiology [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Aerobic and anaerobic, gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria are frequently isolated.

* Both monomicrobial and polymicrobial causes are common. ;  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uIbpeE)

* Polymicrobial: wide variety;  of aerobic and anaerobic pathogens, often of intra-abdominal or genitourinary origin (<u>E. coli</u>, <u>Bacteroides</u> spp.)

* Monomicrobial: commonly <u>group A Streptococcus</u> (<u>S. pyogenes</u>), <u>Peptostreptococcus</u> spp., <u>S. aureus</u>

* <u>Fournier gangrene</u>: usually mixed infection with <u>facultative pathogens</u> (<u>E. coli</u>, <u>Klebsiella</u>, <u>Enterococcus</u>) and <u>anaerobic bacteria</u>





> [!TIP]
> The only way to definitively establish the causative <u>pathogen</u> is by obtaining a deep tissue culture (i.e., during surgical exploration). Clinical features alone are not reliable enough to distinguish between pathogens.




### Clinical features [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Systemic symptoms: <u>fever</u>, chills, <u>altered mental status</u>

* Cutaneous findings

* Diffuse <u>erythema</u> (often manifests initially as suspected <u>cellulitis</u> that is not responding to initial <u>antibiotic therapy</u>)

* Extreme tenderness and <u>pain out of proportion</u> to the area of <u>erythema</u>

* Significant induration of the <u>subcutaneous tissue</u>

* <u>Crepitus</u>: due to the production of <u>methane</u> and CO<sub>2</sub> by bacteria

* Purple <u>skin</u> discoloration;  (<u>skin</u> <u>necrosis</u>, <u>ecchymosis</u>)

* <u>Bullae</u>

* Loss of sensation in the affected area (<u>paresthesias</u>)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Necrotizing fasciitis</u> first spreads along the <u>fascia</u> before spreading to the <u>superficial</u> cutaneous tissue. Local findings may, therefore, be unremarkable, with patients experiencing a disproportionate level of <u>pain</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> <u>Red flags</u> that suggest necrotizing deep tissue infection include the presence of <u>crepitus</u>, <u>bullous</u> lesions, <u>skin</u> <u>necrosis</u>, <u>pain out of proportion</u> to examination, and signs of systemic toxicity (especially <u>altered mental status</u>).

### Diagnostics [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)




A definitive diagnosis is usually made during the visualization of the tissue during <u>surgery</u>.




> [!WARNING]
> Do not delay surgical consultation and definitive surgical intervention for imaging and <u>laboratory studies</u>.




* <u>Laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: <u>leukocytosis</u>

* <u>BMP</u>: possibly <u>hyperglycemia</u>; renal function may be compromised

* <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (<u>CRP</u>, <u>ESR</u>; , <u>procalcitonin</u>): elevated

* CK: elevated

* Assess organ failure and severity.

* Microbiology

* <u>Blood cultures</u> (2 sets)

* <u>Gram stain</u> and cultures from deep tissue  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)

* Imaging: not routinely indicated and should not delay treatment [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Clbq_F)

* CT/<u>MRI</u> with/without IV contrast ;  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)

* Gas in <u>soft tissue</u>

* Fascial thickening and <u>edema</u>

* Lack of <u>contrast enhancement</u> (confirms <u>necrosis</u>)

* Fluid collections on deep fascial planes

* Intermuscular septal <u>edema</u>

* <u>X-ray</u> [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)

* May detect gas in <u>soft tissue</u>

* The absence of gas does not rule out <u>NSTI</u>.

* <u>Ultrasound</u>: can identify fluid accumulation and diffuse thickening of the deep tissue  [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Superficial</u> wound cultures may not accurately represent the pathogens found in deep tissue and should not be used to guide management.




### Management [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Admit all patients with suspected or confirmed <u>NSTI</u> to hospital for treatment.

* If clinical features suggest <u>NSTI</u>, start immediate surgical and medical treatment. 

* Surgical exploration with <u>debridement</u> (confirms the diagnosis and the mainstay of treatment)

* <u>Broad-spectrum antibiotic</u> therapy

* Consult <u>ICU</u> for admission and aggressive <u>supportive care for sepsis</u>, if present. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)

* Provide <u>immediate hemodynamic support</u>: e.g., <u>fluid resuscitation</u>, <u>vasopressors</u>

* Begin <u>sepsis management</u>.

* Consider <u>respiratory support</u> as needed, e.g., <u>oxygen therapy</u>, <u>mechanical ventilation</u>.

* Consider <u>adjuvant therapy</u> on an individual basis (e.g., <u>hyperbaric oxygen</u>, <u>negative pressure wound therapy</u>, <u>intravenous immunoglobulin</u> therapy).





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Necrotizing soft tissue infections</u> are a surgical emergency. Expedite and prioritize surgical exploration for diagnostic confirmation and <u>debridement</u> as much as possible!




#### Surgical exploration and <u>debridement</u>




* Procedure [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)

* Extensive exploration with <u>surgical debridement</u> (removal of <u>necrotic</u> tissue)

* Obtain deep tissue samples for <u>Gram stain</u>, cultures, and <u>histopathology</u>.

* Tissue with uncertain <u>perfusion</u> may be left for reassessment on a second intervention.

* Reexploration every 12–36 hours until there is no evidence of <u>necrotic</u> tissue

* Supportive findings

* <u>Fascia</u> appears swollen

* Dull gray <u>fascia</u>; areas of <u>necrosis</u> may be visible

* Possible brown <u>exudate</u> (no <u>pus</u>)

* Easy dissection of tissue planes with a blunt instrument or gloved finger





#### <u>Antibiotic therapy</u> [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)




* Start systemic, <u>broad-spectrum antibiotic</u> therapy immediately after <u>blood cultures</u> have been obtained.

* See “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for NSTIs</u>” for detailed recommendations.

* Consider polymicrobial infections (provide coverage against aerobes, <u>anaerobes</u>, <u>MRSA</u>).

* If there is evidence of <u>toxic shock syndrome</u>, include <u>antitoxin active antibiotics</u> (e.g., <u>clindamycin</u>, <u>linezolid</u>). [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/nMb768)





#### Acute management checklist for necrotizing SSTI




* Stabilize the patient as needed (see “<u>Sepsis</u>” for more details on the management of severe infections).

* Consider the need for laboratory and imaging studies.

* Consult <u>surgery</u>, infectious diseases, and intensive care teams immediately.

* Urgent surgical exploration and <u>debridement</u>

* Send deep tissue samples for <u>Gram stain</u> and cultures.

* Aggressive and early <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> (see “<u>Empiric antibiotic therapy for NSTIs</u>”)

* Admit to <u>intensive care unit</u>.

* Reexploration every 12–36 hours





### Complications




* Severe <u>necrosis</u> requiring <u>amputation</u> of the affected limb

* <u>Sepsis</u>

* <u>Disseminated intravascular coagulation</u>

* Organ dysfunction (e.g., <u>acute kidney injury</u>)

* <u>Death</u>: high <u>mortality rate</u>;  even with adequate treatment (> 30%)  [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/uIbpeE)





### Differential diagnoses




* <u>Gas gangrene</u>: caused by <u>C. perfringens</u>

* Severe SSTI without <u>necrosis</u>

### Empiric antibiotic therapy for SSTIs [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Om0Ifg)




Adjust <u>empiric antibiotics</u> based on <u>culture and sensitivity</u> results.




* Choice of <u>empiric antibiotic therapy</u> should include the following factors:

* Severity (see “<u>SSTI severity grading</u>”)

* Presence of <u>purulence</u>

* Local resistance patterns

* Risk for complications

* <u>Risk factors for skin and soft tissue infections</u>

* Variants that may require a more specialized treatment include:

* <u>Bite wounds</u>

* Recurrent or complex <u>skin abscesses</u>

* Orbital and <u>periorbital cellulitis</u>

* Device-associated infections (e.g., dialysis catheters, prosthetic <u>joints</u>)

* Patients at risk for <u>endocarditis</u> (e.g., patients with a <u>prosthetic heart valve</u>)





> [!TIP]
> Individualize <u>antibiotic</u> regimen based on <u>patient presentation</u> and risk profile. Even patients with localized infections may require admission for IV <u>antibiotic</u> therapy.

## Differential diagnoses

### Ecthyma gangrenosum




* Definition: an ulcerative lesion extending into the <u>dermis</u> that develops in the setting of <u>bacteremia</u>

* <u>Pathogen</u>: <u>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</u> (not <u>pathognomonic</u> but is the most common causative organism)

* Pathophysiology

* Classically develops in patients with <u>P. aeruginosa</u> <u>bacteremia</u> who are <u>immunocompromised</u>

* Bacteria invade vasculature, causing <u>septic</u> <u>vasculitis</u> and cutaneous <u>necrosis</u>

* <u>Virulence factors</u> destroy tissue:

* <u>Exotoxin A</u>: inhibits <u>elongation factor 2</u>, impairing <u>protein synthesis</u>

* <u>Elastase</u>: degrades <u>elastin</u> in blood vessel walls

* <u>Phospholipase C</u>: breaks down <u>cell membranes</u>

* <u>Pyocyanin</u>: produces damaging <u>reactive oxygen species</u>

* Clinical features

* Rapid progression (within 12–18 hours) of painless red <u>macules</u> → induration, development of <u>pustules</u>, <u>vesicles</u>, and/or <u>bullae</u> → crusted <u>ulcers</u>

* Can involve <u>skin</u> or <u>mucous membranes</u>; anogenital and axillary areas most commonly involved

* Can be solitary or multiple

* Patients typically also systemically ill (e.g., <u>septic shock</u>)

* Diagnosis: primarily a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>

* Treatment: see treatment of <u>skin</u> and <u>soft tissue</u> infections

### Erysipeloid




* Definition: <u>cellulitis</u> caused by <u>Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae</u>

* <u>Pathogen</u>: Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, a gram-positive, capsulated <u>bacilli</u> that is found in a variety of animals including fish, shellfish, pigs, and birds

* Pathophysiology: infection usually acquired occupationally by contact with infected meat (enters through <u>abrasions</u> in the hand)

* Clinical features: warm, tender, well-demarcated, <u>erythematous</u> <u>plaques</u> (most commonly affects the hands)

* Treatment: <u>penicillin</u>

* Complications: <u>sepsis</u>, <u>endocarditis</u>





### Other




* Dermatological conditions [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/35YSPp)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kIYm1q)

* <u>Acne vulgaris</u>

* <u>Atopic dermatitis</u>

* <u>Hidradenitis suppurativa</u>

* Botryomycosis

* <u>Fungal infections</u> (e.g., <u>sporotrichosis</u>, <u>kerion</u>)

* Parasitic infections (e.g., <u>scabies</u>, <u>myiasis</u>,  larva migrans)
