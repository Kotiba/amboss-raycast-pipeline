# Giant cell arteritis

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Ophthalmology > Retina and vision > Giant cell arteritis*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/1T02p2)

## Summary

<u>Giant cell</u> arteritis (GCA) is a type of autoimmune <u>vasculitis</u> that causes <u>chronic inflammation</u> of large and medium-sized <u>arteries</u>, in particular the carotid <u>arteries</u>, its major branches, and the aorta. GCA is most common in women over the age of 50 and of northern European descent, and approximately 50% of patients also have <u>polymyalgia rheumatica</u>. Patients usually present with <u>constitutional symptoms</u> (e.g., <u>fever</u>, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, and <u>malaise</u>), new-onset <u>headache</u>, a tender, hardened <u>temporal artery</u>, <u>jaw claudication</u>, or <u>amaurosis fugax</u>. If there is strong clinical suspicion for GCA, <u>glucocorticoids</u> should be administered immediately, even prior to diagnostic workup if needed, to reduce the risk of permanent <u>vision loss</u> and cerebral <u>ischemia</u>. <u>Laboratory studies</u> typically show signs of inflammation (e.g., elevated <u>erythrocyte sedimentation rate</u> and <u>CRP</u>), and <u>temporal artery biopsy</u> and/or imaging (e.g., <u>duplex ultrasound</u>) should be performed to confirm the diagnosis of <u>vasculitis</u>. The classic histopathological findings are mononuclear infiltration of the vessel wall and formation of <u>giant cells</u>. Patients should be referred to rheumatology for follow-up because most patients require long-term therapy with <u>glucocorticoids</u> (≥ 2 years).

## Etiology

* Unknown; possible contributing factors include:

* Genetic predisposition (e.g., <u>human leukocyte antigen</u> <u>HLA-DR4</u>) [[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/wKXhi_)

* Viral infections (e.g., <u>parvovirus B19</u>) [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9KXNi_)

* Association with <u>polymyalgia rheumatica</u> (<u>PMR</u>): up to 50% of patients with <u>giant cell</u> arteritis also have <u>PMR</u>. [[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/DKX1i_)

## Pathophysiology

<u>Giant cell</u> arteritis is thought to be due to a cell-mediated <u>immune response</u> to <u>endothelial</u> injury. However, the initiating factors are not fully understood.



1. * Inflammation

* Activation of <u>dendritic cells</u> in the adventitia of blood vessel walls → <u>dendritic cells</u> recruit <u>Th1 cells</u>, <u>CD4+ T cells</u> and <u>monocytes</u>

* <u>Monocytes</u> differentiate into <u>macrophages</u> and <u>giant cells</u>, which produce <u>cytokines</u> (e.g., <u>IL-6</u>, <u>TNF-α</u>) that augment the inflammatory response → focal <u>granulomatous inflammation</u>
2. * Local vascular damage

* <u>Macrophages</u> produce <u>metalloproteinases</u> → destruction of vessel wall structures (e.g., <u>internal elastic lamina</u>)

* Most commonly involves <u>external carotid artery</u> branches (especially <u>temporal artery</u>), as well as the aorta and <u>vertebral arteries</u>
3. * Concentric <u>intimal</u> <u>hyperplasia</u>: <u>macrophages</u> and <u>giant cells</u> produce <u>PDGF</u> and <u>VEGF</u> → stimulate <u>intimal</u> <u>proliferation</u> → reduced blood flow and <u>ischemia</u>

## Clinical features

### Nonspecific symptoms




* <u>Constitutional symptoms</u> (e.g. <u>fever</u>, weight loss, and night sweats)

* <u>Symptoms of anemia</u> (e.g., <u>fatigue and malaise</u>)

* Musculoskeletal <u>pain</u> and morning stiffness in the shoulders and/or neck

* <u>Clinical features of polymyalgia rheumatica</u> (if both diseases are present)





> [!TIP]
> About 50% of patients with <u>giant cell</u> arteritis also have <u>polymyalgia rheumatica</u>.




### Clinical features of arterial inflammation




* Cranial giant cell arteritis: involves the extracranial branches of the <u>common carotid</u>, <u>internal carotid</u>, and <u>external carotid arteries</u> (the <u>temporal artery</u> is the most commonly affected vessel)

* New-onset unilateral (or bilateral) <u>headache</u> 

* Can be <u>pulse</u>-synchronous, throbbing, dull

* Typically located over the temples

* Hardened and tender <u>temporal artery</u>

* Jaw claudication: <u>jaw</u> <u>pain</u> when chewing

* <u>Vision loss</u>: due to inflammation and occlusion of the <u>ophthalmic artery</u> and its branches  

* <u>Scintillating scotoma</u>

* <u>Amaurosis fugax</u> or permanent loss of <u>vision</u> due to <u>anterior ischemic optic neuropathy</u> (<u>AION</u>)

* <u>Diplopia</u>

* Large-vessel giant cell arteritis: less common; involves the aorta and its primary branches  [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/81bOis)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/vOXA8y)

* <u>Angina pectoris</u>, <u>acute coronary syndrome</u>

* Abdominal <u>pain</u>

* Limb <u>claudication</u>

* Asymmetrical pulses, asymmetrical blood pressure

* Vascular <u>bruits</u>, <u>aortic regurgitation</u> <u>murmur</u>

* Features of <u>AAA</u>, <u>TAA</u>, <u>aortic dissection</u>





> [!WARNING]
> Limb or <u>jaw claudication</u>, <u>temporal artery</u> abnormalities (e.g., tenderness, thickening, or loss of pulses), and/or signs of <u>AION</u> in a patient ≥ 50 years of age should prompt further evaluation for GCA. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kwWmQn0)

## Diagnosis

### Approach [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqdf-I0)




* Obtain <u>ESR</u> and <u>CRP</u> levels.

* Perform imaging studies and/or a <u>biopsy</u> to confirm the presence of medium- or <u>large-vessel vasculitis</u>.

* Once <u>vasculitis</u> is established, apply the <u>ACR</u>/<u>EULAR</u> classification criteria to assess for GCA.





> [!WARNING]
> If clinical suspicion for GCA is high, initiate high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u> before the diagnostic workup to minimize the risk of complications such as <u>vision loss</u> or <u>stroke</u>. [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_cb5Us)




### <u>Laboratory studies</u> [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqdf-I0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)[[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/Rkalnk)




* <u>Inflammatory markers</u>: initial laboratory study in suspected GCA

* ↑ <u>ESR</u> (≥ 50 mm/hour): due to <u>rouleaux formation</u> of <u>RBCs</u>  [[13]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/PhYWVK)[[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zcbrUs)

* ↑ <u>CRP</u> (â¥ 10 mg/L)

* Additional <u>laboratory studies</u>

* <u>CBC</u>: normal or mild <u>thrombocytosis</u>, <u>leukocytosis</u>, or normochromic <u>anemia</u>

* <u>Liver chemistries</u>: normal or ↑ <u>ALP</u>, ↑ <u>transaminases</u>, or ↓ <u>albumin</u>

* <u>Coagulation studies</u>: normal or ↑ <u>fibrinogen</u>





> [!TIP]
> Elevated <u>inflammatory markers</u> (e.g., <u>ESR</u>, <u>CRP</u>) and/or <u>thrombocytosis</u> increase the likelihood of GCA. [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/kwWmQn0)




### Confirmatory studies [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




All patients require imaging studies and/or a <u>temporal artery biopsy</u> to confirm the diagnosis of <u>vasculitis</u>.  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqdf-I0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/b01He20)




#### Temporal artery biopsy (<u>gold standard</u>)




* Indications

* Consider in all patients with suspected GCA  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xOXEuy)

* High clinical suspicion for GCA despite inconclusive findings on imaging

* Findings: See “Pathology.”

* Important considerations

* <u>Biopsy</u> samples should be > 1 cm in length.  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)

* <u>Histopathology</u> may be <u>falsely negative</u> due to <u>skip lesions</u>. [[14]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zcbrUs)[[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/41b3Ss)





> [!TIP]
> <u>Temporal artery biopsy</u> with <u>histopathology</u> is the only study that can definitively confirm GCA and should be considered in all patients, especially if GCA cannot be ruled out with imaging. [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xOXEuy)




#### <u>Duplex ultrasound</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h1bcgs)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/31bSgs)[[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R1blgs)




* Indications

* <u>Duplex ultrasound</u> of the <u>temporal arteries</u> (with/without the <u>axillary arteries</u>): first-line imaging technique in suspected GCA  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqdf-I0)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/b01He20)

* <u>Duplex ultrasound</u> of other <u>cranial</u> <u>arteries</u> and extracranial <u>arteries</u>: consider if <u>duplex ultrasound</u> of the temporal and <u>axillary arteries</u> is inconclusive  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xOXEuy)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h1bcgs)

* Supportive findings  [[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/xOXEuy)[[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h1bcgs)

* <u>Edema</u> and thickening of the vessel wall (<u>halo sign</u>)

* Noncompressible <u>artery</u> (compression sign)

* Stenosis and occlusion  [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/S1byTs)

* Disadvantages [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/R1blgs)

* Operator-dependent

* Less sensitive in detecting thoracic aortic involvement





#### Additional imaging studies [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/rqdf-I0)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)[[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/j1b_gs)[[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/P1bWSs)




* Indications

* High clinical suspicion for GCA despite inconclusive <u>duplex ultrasound</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h1bcgs)

* Suspected <u>large-vessel GCA</u>

* Modalities

* For suspected <u>cranial</u> and/or extracranial involvement: high-resolution <u>MRI</u>, <u>MRA</u>

* For suspected predominant extracranial involvement: <u>CTA</u> or <u>FDG-PET scan</u>

* Findings

* Similar to <u>ultrasound</u> findings (see above)

* <u>CTA</u>/<u>MRA</u>: <u>contrast enhancement</u> of the vessel wall; also detects stenotic or <u>aneurysmal</u> segments

* <u>FDG-PET scan</u>: increased tracer uptake in large <u>arteries</u> (e.g., the aorta and its branches)

## Pathology

* Panarteritis of the large and medium-sized <u>arteries</u>

* <u>Proliferation</u> of the <u>intima</u> (and subsequent stenosis of the <u>artery</u>)

* <u>Necrosis</u> of the media

* Fragmentation of the <u>internal elastic lamina</u>

* Predominantly mononuclear infiltration of the vessel wall with formation of <u>giant cells</u>

## Differential diagnoses

### Differential diagnoses of GCA




* <u>Polymyalgia rheumatica</u>  

* <u>Giant cell</u> arteritis and <u>polymyalgia rheumatica</u> often occur simultaneously and share common features (e.g., elevated <u>ESR</u>, <u>constitutional symptoms</u>).

* <u>Shoulder girdle</u>, <u>pelvic</u> stiffness, and <u>pain</u> are more common rather than ocular or cerebral findings.

* Other <u>vasculitides</u>

* <u>Takayasu arteritis</u>

* <u>Polyarteritis nodosa</u>

* <u>Granulomatosis with polyangiitis</u>

* <u>Systemic lupus erythematosus</u>

* <u>Rheumatoid arthritis</u>

* Other causes of monocular <u>vision loss</u>

* Carotid <u>artery</u> disease

* <u>Thromboembolism</u>

* <u>Retinal vein occlusion</u>

* Other causes of <u>constitutional symptoms</u>

* Infections (e.g., <u>tuberculosis</u>, <u>endocarditis</u>, <u>hepatitis</u>, <u>HIV infection</u>)

* <u>Hyperthyroidism</u>

* <u>Malignancy</u>

* <u>Fibromyalgia</u>

* <u>Multiple myeloma</u>

* Other causes of <u>headache</u>: See “<u>Differential diagnoses of headache</u>.”

## Treatment

### Approach [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




Consult rheumatology for all patients.




* Start high-dose <u>glucocorticoid</u> therapy for patients with newly diagnosed or recurrent GCA.

* Consider adding <u>glucocorticoid-sparing therapy</u> for all patients.

* Obtain urgent specialist consults as needed for complications. [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/qZbCcH)[[27]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/F1bgis)

* Visual symptoms: ophthalmology

* Neurological symptoms: <u>neurology</u>

* Large-vessel involvement: vascular <u>surgery</u>

* Taper <u>glucocorticoid</u> therapy to a <u>maintenance dose</u> once clinical remission has been achieved.





> [!WARNING]
> Immediate administration of high-dose <u>glucocorticoids</u> is crucial to lowering the risk of permanent <u>vision loss</u> in patients with GCA.




### <u>Glucocorticoid</u> therapy [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)[[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




This is the mainstay of treatment for patients with GCA.




#### Initial high-dose therapy (<u>induction therapy</u>) [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




* <u>Ischemic</u> organ damage (e.g., <u>vision loss</u>, cerebrovascular <u>ischemia</u>): high-dose IV <u>glucocorticoids</u> (e.g., <u>methylprednisolone</u>;  DOSAGE) followed by high-dose oral <u>glucocorticoids</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)

* No <u>cranial</u> symptoms (e.g., <u>vision loss</u>, <u>amaurosis fugax</u>, <u>stroke</u>): high-dose oral <u>glucocorticoids</u> (e.g., <u>prednisone</u> DOSAGE) [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)





#### <u>Maintenance therapy</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TUX6cx)




* Initiate after remission has been achieved.

* Slowly taper <u>glucocorticoids</u> to the lowest dose needed to control symptoms.  [[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TUX6cx)

* If symptoms recur, revert to the dose before the recurrence and reattempt tapering after 4–8 weeks.

* Treatment duration: no consensus (generally, ≥ 2 years) [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)

* Initiate <u>measures to prevent complications of glucocorticoid therapy</u> as needed.





### Adjunctive therapy [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)[[28]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/TUX6cx)




* <u>Glucocorticoid-sparing therapy</u>

* Indications

* Consider in all patients with newly diagnosed GCA.

* Patients with <u>glucocorticoid</u>-related adverse effects

* Patients with GCA relapse

* Options include:

* <u>Tocilizumab</u> (<u>IL-6</u> <u>receptor</u> <u>antagonist</u>)

* <u>Methotrexate</u> [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)

* <u>Abatacept</u> if other agents are not effective [[29]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AqdRar0)

* <u>Glucocorticoid-sparing therapy</u> is typically tapered after <u>glucocorticoid</u> therapy has been discontinued. [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U1bbTs)

* <u>Low-dose aspirin</u>

* Not routinely recommended

* Consider in patients with critical stenosis of <u>vertebral</u> or carotid <u>arteries</u>.  [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)





### Follow-up [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/f01k220)




* Clinical remission typically occurs after 2–4 weeks of treatment. [[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/fUXkcx)

* For patients without clinical remission, consider adding a <u>glucocorticoid-sparing agent</u> or switching the existing agent until remission is achieved.

* Patients in remission require long-term monitoring, including: 

* Imaging studies, and <u>inflammatory markers</u>

* Monitoring for complications of <u>immunosuppressive therapy</u>

## Complications

* Permanent <u>vision loss</u>: ∼ 20–30% if <u>giant cell</u> arteritis is left untreated   [[30]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AKXRQ_)

* Cerebral <u>ischemia</u> (e.g., <u>transient ischemic attack</u> and <u>stroke</u>): < 2% of cases  [[31]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_KX5Q_)

* <u>Aortic aneurysm</u> and/or dissection: ∼ 12% of patients  [[32]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zKXrQ_)




We list the most important complications. The selection is not exhaustive.
