# Corneal disorders

*Categories: Clinical knowledge > Ophthalmology > Conjunctive, sclera, and cornea > Corneal disorders*

[Original Article Link](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/9O0NuT)

---

## Summary

The <u>cornea</u> is the external, transparent layer of the <u>eye</u> covering the <u>iris</u> and <u>pupil</u>. The <u>cornea</u> is highly sensitive, and corneal disorders (e.g., <u>abrasions</u>, infection, <u>erosion</u>, <u>ulcers</u>, foreign bodies) are often painful and associated with reduced <u>visual acuity</u>. Diagnosis is most commonly clinical, based on <u>slit lamp examination</u> with <u>fluorescein staining</u>. Treatment includes <u>supportive care</u> (including <u>analgesics</u>) and foreign body removal, if present. Topical <u>antibiotics</u> may be used, e.g., in <u>corneal abrasions</u> and <u>corneal ulcers</u>. Therapeutic contact lenses are used in <u>keratoconus</u>. <u>Keratoplasty</u> (<u>corneal transplantation</u>) is generally a last resort for patients with significant visual impairment or failed treatment for infection.




For inflammatory disorders of the <u>cornea</u>, see “<u>Conjunctivitis</u>” and “<u>Keratitis</u>.”

## Corneal abrasion

### Definition [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)




A defect in the <u>epithelial</u> surface of the <u>cornea</u> caused by trauma




### Etiology [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Direct injury (e.g., scratch from fingernail or makeup brush)

* Foreign body under the <u>eyelid</u>

* Prolonged contact <u>lens</u> wear or improperly fitted lenses

* <u>Entropion</u>

* <u>Trachoma</u>





### Clinical features [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Foreign body sensation in the <u>eye</u>

* <u>Eye pain</u>

* <u>Epiphora</u>

* <u>Photophobia</u>

* Blurred <u>vision</u>

* <u>Conjunctival injection</u>





### Diagnostics [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Corneal abrasion</u> is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u>.

* Perform a thorough <u>eye examination</u>, including <u>slit lamp examination</u> with <u>fluorescein staining</u>.

* Characteristic findings on <u>slit lamp examination</u> include:

* Traumatic injury: linear or geographic shapes

* Contact <u>lens</u>-related: round, central defect composed of punctate lesions

* Foreign body under <u>eyelid</u>: multiple vertical lines





> [!TIP]
> Resolution of <u>pain</u> with <u>local anesthetic</u> in the setting of <u>superficial</u> <u>eye</u> injury suggests <u>corneal abrasion</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> Exclude <u>open globe injury</u> and <u>corneal foreign bodies</u> in all patients with <u>corneal abrasion</u>.

### Management [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Remove foreign body from the cornea</u> and/or <u>eyelid</u> if present.

* Provide analgesia for corneal pain. [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_QW5AO0)[[4]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zQWrAO0)

* Oral <u>NSAIDs</u>: mild to moderate <u>pain</u>

* Topical <u>NSAIDs</u>, e.g., <u>ketorolac</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE  [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)

* Systemic <u>opioid analgesics</u>: severe <u>pain</u> [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Consider prophylactic topical <u>antibiotics</u> for:

* Deep or potentially contaminated <u>abrasions</u> (e.g., <u>erythromycin</u>  DOSAGE) [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Contact <u>lens</u> wearers or <u>immunocompromise</u>: <u>antibiotics</u> with antipseudomonal activity, e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Consider short-term (i.e., < 24 hours) topical <u>anesthetics</u> (e.g., <u>tetracaine</u>) with caution.  [[5]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/08deO70)[[6]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/d8dol70)[[7]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/U8dbN70)

* Avoid <u>eye</u> patches.  [[3]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/_QW5AO0)[[8]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/ajWQ_O0)





> [!WARNING]
> Limit use of topical <u>NSAIDs</u> to 2–3 days due to risk of corneal toxicity. [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)




### Disposition [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Consult ophthalmology urgently for:

* Signs of active infection (e.g., corneal infiltrate)

* <u>Corneal ulcer</u>

* Evidence of <u>open globe injury</u>

* <u>Hypopyon</u> or <u>hyphema</u>

* <u>Retained foreign body</u>

* Chemical <u>burns</u>

* Arrange follow-up in 24 hours if <u>corneal abrasion</u> is > 4 mm, contact <u>lens</u>-related, or accompanied by a decrease in <u>visual acuity</u>.

* Refer to ophthalmology if symptoms have not improved at follow-up.





### Complications [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Infection (e.g., <u>bacterial keratitis</u>)

* <u>Corneal ulcer</u>

* Traumatic <u>iritis</u>

* Recurrent <u>corneal erosion</u>

## Corneal laceration

For full-thickness <u>lacerations</u>, see “<u>Open globe injury</u>.” [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[9]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/dOWorl0)




* Definition: traumatic corneal defect caused by a sharp object

* Diagnostics: <u>slit lamp examination</u> to exclude full-thickness defects and foreign bodies  [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)

* Management

* Consult ophthalmology urgently.

* <u>Conservative management</u> of small, <u>superficial</u> lesions is similar to that of <u>corneal abrasions</u>.

* Deeper or more extensive lesions may require surgical repair.

## Corneal foreign body

### Etiology [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)



* Most commonly results from dust or debris blown into the <u>eye</u> during:

* High-risk activities without adequate protective eyewear, e.g., grinding, drilling, sawing, welding, mowing

* Exposure to high winds




### Clinical features [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* History of sudden onset and persistent discomfort following trauma

* Signs of ocular inflammation: copious tearing, redness, foreign body sensation, difficulty keeping the <u>eye</u> open, <u>photophobia</u>, and blurred <u>vision</u>





### Diagnostics [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Perform a thorough <u>eye examination</u>, including <u>slit lamp examination</u>, evaluation for <u>open globe injury</u>, and <u>eversion of the upper eyelid</u>.

* Characteristic findings on examination include: 

* Visible foreign body

* Rust rings

* Corneal defect without visible foreign body

* If no foreign body is seen but suspicion of penetrating injury is high, obtain imaging (e.g., orbital <u>CT scan</u> or <u>ultrasound</u>). [[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Corneal foreign bodies</u> that penetrate the full thickness of the <u>cornea</u> should be considered as <u>open globe injuries</u> that require urgent ophthalmology consultation. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




> [!WARNING]
> <u>MRI is contraindicated</u> for the evaluation of suspected metallic foreign bodies.

### Management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




#### Corneal foreign body removal




Utilize a stepwise approach from the least to most invasive technique.




* Administer a topical <u>anesthetic</u> (e.g. <u>tetracaine</u> DOSAGE)

* Irrigate the affected <u>eye</u> copiously with saline.

* Sweep a moist cotton swab over the object and <u>cornea</u>.

* Attempt removal with a 25–27 gauge needle, <u>corneal spud</u>, or <u>burr drill</u>.

* Ask the patient to look at a distant object to stabilize the <u>eye</u>.

* Visualize the foreign body using magnification (e.g., loupes, <u>slit lamp</u>).

* Hold the instrument tangential to the <u>cornea</u>.

* If using a needle or <u>corneal spud</u>: Scoop or pick up the foreign body with the instrument.

* If using a <u>burr drill</u>: Hold the burr against the foreign body until removed.

* Residual rust rings may be removed during initial presentation or after 24–48 hours.





> [!WARNING]
> Do not attempt to remove the foreign body if an <u>open globe injury</u> is suspected.




#### Supportive treatment [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




* <u>Tetanus prophylaxis</u>

* Indicated for <u>penetrating eye injury</u>

* Consider for potentially contaminated foreign bodies, concomitant chemical <u>burns</u>, or tissue <u>necrosis</u>

* <u>Analgesia</u> and <u>antibiotic prophylaxis</u>: See “<u>Corneal abrasion</u>” for indications and recommendations.





### Disposition [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* Consult ophthalmology urgently for:

* Suspected <u>open globe injury</u>

* Inability to remove a foreign body

* Large or deep foreign bodies

* Foreign bodies affecting the central field of <u>vision</u>

* See also “<u>Corneal abrasion</u>.”





### Complications [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[10]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/3wYSir)




Complications from a <u>corneal foreign body</u> are rare but may include:




* <u>Corneal abrasion</u>

* <u>Open globe injury</u>

* Secondary <u>iritis</u> [[11]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/SPWyVl0)

* Rust rings may delay corneal healing and/or impair <u>visual acuity</u> (if centrally located).

## Corneal ulcer

### Definition [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Corneal ulcer</u>:  (ulcerative <u>keratitis</u>): a defect of the corneal <u>epithelium</u> and underlying stroma that occurs secondary to infection or inflammation

* Infectious <u>keratitis</u>: an infection of the <u>cornea</u> (also called “infectious <u>corneal ulcer</u>”) [[15]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/yQWdAO0)

* Peripheral ulcerative <u>keratitis</u>: inflammation at the margin of the <u>corneal stroma</u> caused by autoimmune disease (also called “peripheral corneal <u>ulceration</u>”) [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h3WcRO0)





### Etiology




<u>Corneal ulcers</u> most frequently occur as a complication of <u>keratitis</u>. [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/33WSRO0)




* <u>Bacterial keratitis</u>

* Common organisms: <u>Staphylococcus</u>, <u>Streptococcus</u>, <u>Mycobacterium</u>, and <u>Pseudomonas</u>

* <u>Risk factors</u>:

* Contact <u>lens</u> wearing

* <u>Corticosteroid</u> use

* <u>Eye trauma</u>

* Prior ocular <u>surgery</u>

* <u>Viral keratitis</u>

* <u>Herpes simplex keratitis</u>

* <u>Herpes zoster keratitis</u>

* <u>Fungal keratitis</u>: most common in agricultural workers and patients using <u>corticosteroids</u>

* Noninfectious <u>keratitis</u> [[16]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/h3WcRO0)[[18]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bjWH_O0)

* Autoimmune disease, e.g., <u>rheumatoid arthritis</u>, <u>systemic lupus erythematosus</u>

* <u>Keratoconjunctivitis sicca</u>

### Clinical features [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/33WSRO0)




* <u>Eye pain</u> and/or foreign body sensation

* <u>Epiphora</u>

* <u>Conjunctival injection</u>

* <u>Photophobia</u>

* Blurry/decreased <u>vision</u>

* Discharge from <u>eye</u>





### Diagnostics [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/33WSRO0)




<u>Corneal ulcer</u> is a <u>clinical diagnosis</u> based on <u>slit lamp examination</u> with <u>fluorescein staining</u>. Bacterial and viral cultures can help direct definitive treatment.




* Characteristic findings on <u>slit lamp examination</u>

* Corneal <u>epithelial</u> defect

* Stromal <u>edema</u> or infiltration

* <u>Anterior</u> chamber <u>cells and flare</u> (∼ 25% of patients)  [[19]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/VjWGzO0)

* Findings indicating viral <u>corneal ulcer</u>

* <u>Herpes simplex keratitis</u>

* Herpetic lesions on <u>conjunctiva</u> or lids

* <u>Slit lamp examination</u>: dendritic lesions or amoeba-shaped <u>ulcer</u>

* <u>Herpes zoster keratitis</u> 

* <u>Dermatomal</u> <u>vesicular</u> <u>rash</u>

* Possible signs of <u>uveitis</u>, <u>iritis</u>, or choroiditis

* <u>Slit lamp examination</u>: similar to <u>HSV keratitis</u>

* See also: “<u>Viral keratitis</u>”

### Management [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/33WSRO0)




* All patients

* Consult ophthalmology urgently.

* Provide <u>analgesia for corneal pain</u>.

* <u>Corneal ulcer</u> secondary to <u>bacterial keratitis</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2jWT-O0)

* Initiate broad-spectrum topical <u>antibiotics</u> (e.g., <u>ciprofloxacin</u> DOSAGE).

* Consider <u>topical corticosteroids</u> and/or systemic <u>antibiotics</u> in consultation with ophthalmology.

* <u>Corneal ulcer</u> secondary to <u>herpes simplex keratitis</u> [[20]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/2jWT-O0)[[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/muXV7-)

* Initiate topical or systemic <u>antiviral therapy</u>.

* Topical: e.g., trifluridine DOSAGE [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/muXV7-)

* Systemic: e.g., <u>famciclovir</u> (<u>off-label</u>) DOSAGE [[21]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/muXV7-)

* <u>Topical corticosteroids</u> may be administered in consultation with ophthalmology for <u>keratitis</u> affecting the stroma or <u>endothelium</u>.

* <u>Corneal ulcer</u> secondary to <u>herpes zoster keratitis</u>

* Initiate <u>antiviral therapy for herpes zoster</u>.

* See also “<u>Herpes zoster ophthalmicus</u>.”





### Disposition [[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)[[17]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/33WSRO0)




* All patients should be seen by ophthalmology within 24 hours.

* Admit patients with:

* <u>Herpes zoster ophthalmicus</u> and <u>immunocompromise</u> or retinal involvement

* Severe symptoms

* No access to urgent ophthalmologic follow-up care





### Complications [[1]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/CNYqWp)[[2]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/AN1Rdh0)




* <u>Vision loss</u>

* Corneal scarring and/or perforation

* <u>Endophthalmitis</u>





> [!WARNING]
> <u>Corneal ulcers</u> are an ophthalmological emergency and can result in permanent corneal scarring and <u>vision loss</u>.

## Corneal degeneration, dystrophy, and deposits

### Corneal degeneration




* Definition: changes of the <u>cornea</u> that cause corneal deterioration and, potentially, dysfunction

* Etiology

* Normal <u>aging</u>

* Secondary to various pathological processes (e.g., <u>calcium</u> salt deposits in <u>band keratopathy</u> associated with <u>hypercalcemia</u> in the setting of <u>sarcoidosis</u>)





#### Band keratopathy




* Definition: a type of <u>corneal degeneration</u> that involves the appearance of <u>a band</u>-shaped area of calcification across the central <u>cornea</u> [[12]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/zNYrdp)

* Etiology

* <u>Idiopathic</u>

* <u>Risk factors</u>

* <u>Hypercalcemia</u> (e.g., <u>hyperparathyroidism</u>, <u>sarcoidosis</u>), <u>hyperphosphatemia</u> (e.g., <u>chronic renal failure</u>)

* Chronic, inflammatory <u>eye</u> disease (e.g., <u>uveitis</u>, <u>keratitis</u>)

* Exposure to harmful chemicals (e.g., <u>mercury</u> vapors)

* <u>Family history</u> of <u>band keratopathy</u>

* Pathophysiology: increased serum <u>calcium</u>, serum <u>phosphate</u>, and/or corneal surface <u>pH</u> (caused by chronically inflamed eyes) → change in solubility of <u>calcium</u> and <u>phosphate</u> → <u>calcium</u> <u>phosphate</u> precipitation out of tears, <u>aqueous humor</u>, and corneal tissue → <u>calcium</u> <u>phosphate</u> deposition as salts in the Bowman layer and <u>superficial</u> stroma of the <u>cornea</u>

* Clinical findings

* Decreased <u>visual acuity</u>

* Foreign body sensation

* <u>Photophobia</u>

* Diagnostics

* <u>Slit lamp examination</u> showing band-like, horizontal, opaque white areas on the <u>cornea</u>

* Follow-up includes investigation of underlying causes and may include:

* Serum <u>calcium</u> and <u>phosphate</u> measurement

* Parathyroid <u>hormone</u> level measurement

* <u>Renal function tests</u>

* <u>Sarcoidosis</u> workup (e.g., serum <u>ACE</u>, chest <u>x-ray</u>)

* Treatment

* Treatment of the underlying cause (e.g., <u>uveitis</u>, <u>hypercalcemia</u>)

* <u>Superficial</u> <u>debridement</u> and lamellar <u>keratectomy</u>

* Prognosis: Visual deficits caused by <u>band keratopathy</u> can typically be treated successfully but will recur if the underlying condition is not addressed.

### Corneal dystrophy [[22]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/0mYeVp)




* Definition: A group of inherited noninflammatory disorders that cause the buildup of various substances in the <u>cornea</u> and result in morphological changes.

* Classification: There are many different types of <u>corneal dystrophy</u>. The most common are:

* <u>Fuchs corneal dystrophy</u> (see below for details)

* Lattice <u>corneal dystrophy</u>

* Map-dot-fingerprint <u>corneal dystrophy</u>

* Etiology: inherited condition

* Clinical findings: Because <u>corneal dystrophies</u> are a heterogeneous group of diseases, they can affect the <u>eye</u> in different ways. Symptoms may include:

* Progressive visual impairment

* <u>Corneal erosion</u> (see “Symptoms” in <u>corneal erosion</u>, above)

* Diagnostics: visible structural changes and/or deposits on <u>slit lamp examination</u>

* Treatment

* See “Treatment” in <u>corneal erosion</u>, above.

* <u>Keratoplasty</u> in severe cases or when other treatment modalities have not been successful

#### Fuchs dystrophy




* Definition: inherited disease that predisposes to progressive loss of corneal <u>endothelial</u> cells

* Pathophysiology: corneal <u>endothelial</u> cells are responsible for maintaining the transparency of the <u>cornea</u> by balancing water and <u>electrolyte</u> flow into and out of the corneal layers; deterioration of corneal <u>endothelial</u> cells → decreased outflow of water from the corneal stroma → corneal <u>edema</u>, opacification, and <u>bullous</u> <u>epithelial</u> detachment [[23]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/bmYHVp)

* Clinical findings

* Reduced <u>visual acuity</u>

* Blurred <u>vision</u>, glare, and halos, often improving over the course of the day  [[24]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/imYJ2p)

* <u>Eye pain</u> or foreign body sensation in the <u>eye</u>

* Diagnostics

* Early <u>Fuchs dystrophy</u> is asymptomatic, but <u>cornea</u> guttata (droplet-shaped structures in the <u>Descemet membrane</u> consisting of <u>collagen</u> and thought to have been secreted by stressed corneal <u>endothelial</u> cells) can be seen on <u>slit lamp examination</u>.

* Advanced disease: <u>Slit lamp examination</u> shows corneal thickening, <u>edema</u>, <u>bullae</u>, and <u>fibrotic</u> scarring.

* Treatment [[25]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/amYQVp)

* Asymptomatic patients do not require treatment.

* Symptomatic treatment

* Medical treatment to dehydrate the <u>cornea</u>: hyperosmolar <u>eye</u> drops, warm dry air (e.g., <u>hair</u> dryer held at arm's length)

* For <u>pain</u> or foreign body sensation in the <u>eye</u>: <u>NSAIDs</u>, bandage contact lenses

* <u>Keratoplasty</u>: indicated in advanced disease with marked loss of <u>vision</u> that does not improve over the course of the day and if <u>pain</u> cannot be alleviated by symptomatic treatment

### Corneal deposits




A variety of substances can accumulate in the <u>cornea</u> to create deposits. Two of the more well-known causes of corneal deposits are presented below.




#### Arcus senilis (<u>corneal arcus</u>)




* Definition: a condition associated with normal <u>aging</u>, in which annular deposits of lipids appear around the corneal margin

* <u>Epidemiology</u>: <u>Incidence</u> increases with age. [[26]](https://coursology-qbank.com/amboss/article/cmYaep)

* 60% in those 50–60 years

* Almost 100% in those > 80 years

* Clinical findings: asymptomatic

* Diagnostics: <u>slit lamp examination</u>

* Treatment

* In older patients: no treatment necessary

* Occurrence before 50 years of age: rule out <u>lipid disorders</u>

#### <u>Kayser-Fleischer ring</u>



* Definition: green-brown, <u>copper</u> deposits are a diagnostic sign of <u>Wilson disease</u> (See “Clinical features” in <u>Wilson disease</u>.)
